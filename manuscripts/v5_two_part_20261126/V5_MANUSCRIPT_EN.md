## Abstract

**Aim.** Floral traits mediate interactions with pollinators and the abiotic environment, but broad-scale associations among taxa do not establish how existing within-species variation is expressed in newly occupied climates. We asked whether climatic resemblance predicts multivariate resemblance in thistle capitula and whether novel climatic occupancy is accompanied by previously unobserved phenotypes or differences in the composition of existing variation.

**Location.** Global native ranges of sampled thistle taxa and native and introduced ranges of two widespread species.

**Methods.** We used 157,822 eligible native-range photographic records as the source for a geographically balanced comparison of image-referenced capitulum angle, floral chroma and projected outline. We assessed trait resemblance between climatically analogous cells and estimated signed climate–trait contrasts within taxon pairs. For *Cirsium arvense* and *Cirsium vulgare*, background-corrected climatic occupancy defined introduced analogous and non-analogue climates. Within introduced geographic regions, we compared phenotype distributions between matched cells, evaluated native-unobserved trait combinations against geographically held-out native samples and estimated directional and nonlinear relative enrichment of native-observed phenotypes.

**Results.** Climatically analogous cells had more similar trait combinations within taxa at both spatial grains and between taxa at the coarser grain; the between-taxon association was weaker at 1°. Annual precipitation was positively associated with downward photographic orientation, and precipitation seasonality with chroma, in both grains. Introduced occupancy outside the native climatic core accounted for 18.8% and 44.0% of background-corrected occupancy density in *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare*, respectively. Phenotypic distributions in non-analogue climates overlapped broadly with native distributions, with no supported increase in native-unobserved combinations. In *C. vulgare*, the estimated relative enrichment of the highest versus lowest native chroma quintile was 2.94 (pointwise 95% interval 1.40–6.17; multiplicity-adjusted P = 0.183 within the primary specification). Contrasts in water-related responses weakened when restricted to shared climatic support.

**Main conclusions.** Climatic associations with floral resemblance coexist with broad retention of observed within-species phenotypes in non-analogue climates. Our exploratory distributional contrasts suggest hypotheses about environmentally associated sorting of existing phenotypes rather than demonstrating distinct modes of natural selection or rapid genetic adaptation.

Keywords: capitulum; climatic occupancy; ecological fitting; floral chroma; intraspecific variation; biological invasion; phenotypic sorting; citizen-science imagery.

## 1. Introduction

Floral traits influence reproductive interactions, but flowers are also exposed to rain, radiation and variation in water availability. These abiotic conditions can affect floral development, pollen viability and reproductive performance. The orientation of a capitulum can modify exposure of reproductive structures to precipitation, whereas pigmentation can participate in physiological stress responses as well as visual signalling. Consequently, macroecological variation in floral traits need not be attributable solely to pollinator-mediated selection (Chen et al., 2013; Dalrymple et al., 2020; Sullivan & Koski, 2021). A joint examination of several traits, rather than reliance on a single colour category or taxon mean, offers a way to identify phenotypic dimensions associated with climate.

Phenotypic resemblance in similar climates may arise from parallel environmental responses, differences in species composition, shared ancestry, plasticity or selection. Even where an association is found across taxa, the corresponding response within an individual species is not necessarily the same. Intraspecific variation is particularly relevant to environmental change because the distribution of phenotypes already present within a species can affect how it occupies different environments. Ecological fitting describes establishment in new contexts using attributes already available to organisms, potentially including flexible phenotypes (Agosta & Klemens, 2008). In contrast, evolutionary adaptation from standing genetic variation requires differential fitness and heritable variation already present in the relevant ancestral population (Barrett & Schluter, 2008). These processes are related but cannot be distinguished by contemporary phenotypic distributions alone.

Biological invasions provide historically distinct settings in which to investigate the association of traits with newly occupied environments (Hulme & Barrett, 2013). Range-level differences in climatic occupancy may coexist with overlapping trait distributions, or they may be accompanied by differences in the frequency of particular phenotypes. Importantly, non-analogue climates are species-specific: two introduced species may occupy different directions of climatic space. A comparison of their raw phenotypic contrasts therefore does not by itself identify a species-specific difference in environmental response. Comparing responses on a common environmental scale and examining shared climatic support are essential for interpreting such contrasts.

Natural-selection theory provides a useful vocabulary for specifying possible distributional patterns. Directional selection may favour one end of a trait distribution, stabilizing selection may favour an interior optimum and disruptive selection may favour both extremes over intermediate phenotypes (Lande & Arnold, 1983; Svensson, 2023). However, estimating these processes requires relative fitness or an appropriate before–after selection design. The cross-sectional association between phenotype and environment is not a selection gradient. Here we use directional and nonlinear terms to characterize relative phenotypic enrichment as a descriptive analogue of these predictions, not as an estimator of selection.

Using geographically linked photographs of thistles, we addressed two connected questions. First, in native ranges, does local climatic resemblance correspond to resemblance in three capitulum traits, and which climatic dimensions correspond to signed trait differences? Second, within two introduced species, does occupancy of climates outside their native climatic cores coincide with greater phenotypic novelty, or with different representation of phenotypes already recorded in the native range? We further ask whether water-related associations can explain the contrasting phenotypic patterns. These analyses connect macroecological associations to hypotheses about within-species responses, without assuming that the across-taxon relationships are transferable to either focal species. The final focus on three traits and annual climate was developed through exploratory analysis; the final comparisons are not an independent preregistered confirmation.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Photographic measurements and environmental data

We retained three continuous visible-image measures from the archived measurement pipeline. Candidate capitula were localized by an object detector (YOLO); separate image-processing routines calculated (i) the direction of the capitulum's principal axis towards floral pixels relative to the image vertical (0° toward image top, 180° toward bottom, after available EXIF correction), (ii) CIELAB chroma C* = sqrt(a*² + b*²) over segmented floral pixels, and (iii) the height-to-width ratio of the projected outline bounding box after PCA-based rotation. Chroma quantifies photographed visible colour intensity, not calibrated spectral reflectance, anthocyanin concentration or pollinator-perceived colour. The angle is not gravity-calibrated, and the outline ratio may depend on camera projection and rotation. Record-level medians summarize usable heads; different eligible heads can contribute to different traits in one record. We therefore interpret the joint measurements as photographic-record phenotypes, not necessarily co-measured traits of an identical individual capitulum.

The final climatic definition uses five annual CHELSA v2.1 variables: annual mean temperature (BIO1), temperature seasonality (BIO4), annual precipitation (BIO12), precipitation seasonality (BIO15), and mean shortwave radiation. BIO15 represents relative monthly variability in precipitation rather than drought exposure during a plant's flowering period. No elevation, lightness, hue or temporal trend is included in the adopted models. Source-specific quality gates, geographic deduplication and sampling units are documented in the supporting information. Counts from the two parts describe distinct analysis cohorts and must not be summed.

### 2.2. Part 1: climatic resemblance and multivariate phenotype distances

The native photographic source contained 157,822 eligible records from 242 taxon names prior to geographic coverage selection. We summarized eligible records by taxon and 1° or 5° geographic cell using coordinate medians. Taxa with at least 40 records and eight occupied cells qualified for the balanced resampling design. Standardization was based on equal-taxon-weighted scales. The 1° and 5° cohorts contained 63 taxa (7,117 taxon–cell units) and 31 taxa (814 taxon–cell units), respectively.

For each of 24 rarefactions, eight cells were sampled per taxon. Climatic analogues were defined using Euclidean distance on the standardized five-variable climate vector, with threshold 0.9 sqrt(5/3) ≈ 1.162 to retain the same per-axis root-mean-square criterion as the three-variable baseline. We compared the mean Euclidean distance between three-trait vectors for all cell combinations with that among climatic analogues, both within taxa and among taxa assigned to different dominant longitude sectors. Qualified pairs required adequate analogue cells on both sides. The null permuted complete phenotype vectors within taxa, preserving their trait distributions and covariation. Statistics were averaged over rarefactions before permutation inference; cells, repeated draws and taxon pairs sharing a taxon were not treated as independent replicate experiments. The final annual five-axis comparisons used 499 permutation realizations and Holm correction across the two grains for each resemblance test. The three-variable annual comparison on the same source cohort was retained as a sensitivity analysis.

### 2.3. Part 1: signed environmental and trait contrasts

For the sampled pairs of distinct taxa, we calculated signed cell-level differences in standardized climate and traits. We removed the average climatic and phenotypic difference specific to each taxon pair, and fitted the five climatic differences simultaneously. This estimand characterizes local contrasts conditional on taxon-pair identity rather than a regression of species means or an independently evolved phylogenetic contrast. We obtained pointwise 95% intervals using 499 taxon-resampling weights, retaining dependence of pairs sharing taxa. The two spatial grains reuse the same source records. These exploratory coefficients were not established by a multiplicity-corrected family of all signed contrasts. Geographic autocorrelation, ancestry and unmeasured environmental covariates can remain.

### 2.4. Part 2: selection of focal species and climatic occupancy

We selected *Cirsium arvense* and *Cirsium vulgare* initially because they had substantial observational coverage in both native and introduced ranges. A retrospective audit of 350 source species-rank names against World Checklist of Vascular Plants regional statuses identified seven with explicit wild native and introduced records. Only the two focal species met coverage requirements in both ranges (at least 100 angle-assessable observations, 15 broad approximately equal-area 5° cells and 10 observers per range). This feasibility-based selection is not random sampling of the genus. The two species represented 156,608 of the 156,750 explicitly introduced wild records in the source archive. The 297,622 original focal wild records included both ranges, and the climate cohort comprised 144,178 deduplicated records. These are source and climate eligibility counts, not effective sample sizes for the three-trait analyses.

For each species, we projected five annual climate variables into a two-axis PCA-env space and estimated background-corrected occurrence density separately in native and introduced regions (Broennimann et al., 2012). We measured overlap as Schoener's D, the sum of cellwise minima of two normalized densities. The main non-analogue category was occupancy outside the native region's 95% probability-content core. This is an operational measure of current climatic occupancy, not historical expansion of physiological tolerance. A second classification compared introduced occupancy density with native occupancy density, and an additional sensitivity restricted comparisons to major climate backgrounds shared by both ranges.

### 2.5. Part 2: matched phenotype distributions and native-reference novelty

We retained the three-trait cohort at 1° grain, with 1,366 native and 860 introduced cells for *C. arvense* and 1,113 and 1,139 cells for *C. vulgare*. Within cells, eligible observations received inverse cell-count weights so cells contributed equally. We estimated smoothed three-dimensional phenotype densities in a common species-specific trait grid, using native standardization and fixed smoothing (0.5 native SD). For the main test of differences between introduced analogous and non-analogue climates, we matched 1° cells without replacement within 20° geographic blocks by minimum geographic separation. This gave 30 pairs in nine blocks and 146 pairs in 14 blocks, respectively; the density-surplus definition gave 183 and 316 pairs. Both members of each pair occur in the introduced range. These matched contrasts limit some geographic imbalance but do not control ancestry, environmental conditions, observation behaviour or actual introduction events.

To evaluate apparent novel phenotypes while accounting for incomplete sampling, we used 32 rarefactions with 256 native reference cells and 128 geographically held-out native cells. Native quintile boundaries and outside-range bins for three traits define 343 combinations. We compared the frequency of combinations unobserved in the reference, calibrated against held-out native cells. We further partitioned positive changes in the observed distribution between analogous and non-analogue cells into changes in native-observed (E) and native-unobserved (U) bins. The ratio E/(E+U) summarizes the share of positive redistribution in observed bins, not the proportion of individuals adapting through ancestral genetic variants. Because already observed bins can predominate under the null, we evaluated this statistic by geographic-block label reversals, preserving matched sample sizes and reference distributions. We retained the planned Holm family of 32 tests, rather than selecting the most favourable endpoint. This null is a distributional comparison, not a population-genetic model of selection.

### 2.6. Part 2: relative enrichment of previously observed phenotypes

We characterized the composition of existing phenotypes using the full native reference for each species. Each of the three traits was discretized into native quintiles with ranks r = −2, −1, 0, 1 and 2. All 125 interior joint combinations were observed in the complete native reference. We restricted the density-ratio model to those combinations, excluding a negligible outside-range fraction. For each pair, both climate groups contributed equal total weight, and a pair-specific intercept was profiled. The fixed model was

log[p_N(r)/p_A(r)] = a_pair + sum_j {b_j r_j + c_j (r_j² − 2)},

where p_N and p_A denote normalized within-cell observation distributions in non-analogue and analogous climates. The conditional surface describes relative representation; it is not an occurrence probability, an individual transition or relative fitness. The linear coefficient gives a directional tilt around the central rank; the quadratic coefficient gives curvature of the log ratio. The highest-versus-lowest conditional relative enrichment is exp(4b), and the curve relative to the middle quintile is exp(b r + c r²). The direction and curvature of all three traits were fitted simultaneously, but this additive model does not estimate trait-by-trait interaction terms or correlational selection.

We assessed each coefficient with an approximate regional score-sign test conditional on other model terms and pointwise cluster intervals. A whole-block label-reversal omnibus test assessed all six terms jointly. Primary and alternative climate definitions retain their original Holm-12 and Holm-24 families and the four omnibus comparisons. We compared coefficients directly between the species, accounted for shared geographic blocks, and retained geographic leave-one-block-out diagnostics and out-of-region prediction checks. Details and unsuccessful tests are in the SI.

### 2.7. Part 2: water-related response contrasts and shared support

To distinguish climate exposure contrasts from species-specific responses, we modelled matched-cell differences in the proportions of observations within the native lowest and highest chroma quintiles. For each species, the response was the non-analogue minus analogous proportion, with equal cell-pair weight. Climate predictors were differences in the five variables plus the difference in the product of centered annual precipitation and precipitation seasonality levels. This is a difference in a nonlinear climate surface, not the product of two climate changes. All five main effects were retained and allowed to vary by species. We used shared physical units (500 mm annual precipitation and 20 BIO15 units), centered at 759.93 mm and BIO15 = 33.06; the remaining climate axes used pooled equal-species native scales. The two focal species' high/low chroma categories remain relative to their own native distributions. These tail-frequency regressions do not adjust for the other two measured phenotypes, unlike Section 2.6.

We obtained pointwise intervals and two-sided tests using regional clustered influence contributions, incorporating cross-species dependence in shared blocks, and approximate block-score sign tests. Across two climate definitions, two responses, three water terms and three contrasts (two within-species estimates and their difference), 36 planned tests were retained for Holm adjustment. To examine whether estimates depended on different water environments, we further restricted each species' pairs to the intersection of the other species' convex hulls in water-level space at both endpoints and water-contrast space including the product difference. This establishes only partial water-related support, not identical treatments or full five-dimensional overlap. We retained the original support gates of at least 25 pairs and eight regions per species and full-rank design; no threshold was relaxed to obtain a comparison. The secondary water model and support diagnostic were developed following the earlier exploratory results.

### 2.8. Part 2: spatial structure of relative-enrichment patterns

To examine spatial structure underlying the exploratory curves, we used the same matched 1° cell pairs and native-reference three-trait cohort. For continuous traits we partitioned variance into among-region, among-cell within-region and within-cell components, after standardization by each species' equal-cell-weighted native SD. We calculated regional mean differences (non-analogue minus analogue) and their pair-count-weighted signed mean D and absolute mean A; 1 − |D|/A measures descriptive cancellation of opposing regional differences, not an explanatory or causal fraction. Pointwise bootstrap intervals for variance components used 3,999 whole-region resamples. The three-level variance partition is not a decomposition of the conditional enrichment coefficients. In a fixed exploratory prediction diagnostic, we allowed the chroma and angle directional and quadratic terms of the original density-ratio model to vary with paired differences in either the three non-water climatic axes or all five climatic axes. We used a fixed ridge penalty of 4 on the additional coefficients only, and compared with the constant six-term model and no-enrichment reference in leave-one-geographic-block-out conditional allocation of the pooled test-pair phenotype distribution. Folds with inadequate support or failed estimation were not discarded to obtain a favourable average. This test evaluates regional transfer of phenotype allocation, not prediction of absolute phenotype or fitness at new sites.

### 2.9. Part 2: direct reconstruction of the enrichment curves from water-related climatic differences

We audited the original six-term model's regional score contributions and refitted the same model with equal total weight per geographic region. We also counted the joint signs of lowest- and highest-quintile frequency contrasts within matched cell pairs and within regional aggregates. These descriptive checks distinguish aggregation and unequal regional weight from the conditional model's shape; they neither estimate region-specific fitness functions nor provide new significance tests.

We then asked whether the two climate–trait associations motivating Part 1 could reproduce the enrichment curves without assuming an intrinsic difference between non-analogue and analogous groups. We fixed two water predictors: the signed within-pair difference in precipitation seasonality divided by 20, multiplied by native chroma rank, and the signed precipitation difference divided by 500 mm, multiplied by native angle rank. We compared common-coefficient and species-specific versions, with and without the corresponding products of climate differences and centered squared trait ranks. No coefficient or sign from Part 1 was imposed. The water models lacked the constant six enrichment terms and used a fixed ridge penalty of four per species, with equal total training weight per species; shared coefficients had the equivalent combined penalty. The comparator models were no enrichment and the original constant six-term curve. This deliberately restricted test concerns sufficiency of the two specified water axes, not a complete environmental or causal model.

We held out each geographic block from both species jointly, retained the fixed training support threshold (20 pairs and seven blocks per species used for fitting), and predicted allocation of the held-out pair's pooled observed phenotype distribution to the two climatic groups. Pair intercepts only ensured equal total mass in the two cells. We compared conditional log loss, including equal-region-weighted loss, and refitted the original six-term model to the predicted allocations to reconstruct the five-point enrichment curves. We calculated curve RMSE on the log-relative-enrichment scale. Complete-fold evaluations only were retained; missing fits were not treated as null outcomes. This is a targeted exploratory check of environmental sufficiency conditional on observed phenotypes, not prediction of absolute floral phenotype, fitness or population establishment at a new site.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Native climatic analogues resemble one another in photographed capitulum traits

Within taxa, five-axis climatic analogues showed greater multivariate resemblance than expected under within-taxon permutation at both grains (standardized distance advantage over null: 0.084 at 1° and 0.238 at 5°; Holm P = 0.004 at both grains). Between taxa, the corresponding advantage was 0.031 at 1° (Holm P = 0.076) and 0.065 at 5° (P = 0.012). The matched three-axis sensitivity yielded between-taxon advantages of 0.033 and 0.050 (Holm P = 0.016 at both grains). Thus the evidence is strongest within taxa and consistent in direction between taxa, with weaker fine-grain support when seasonal climate is included. These patterns establish climate-conditioned resemblance, not independently evolved convergence.

### 3.2. Two dimensions of the water environment correspond to different floral traits

Conditional on taxon-pair average differences and the other four climatic axes, annual precipitation was positively associated with the downward-oriented photographic angle at 1° (standardized coefficient 0.117, pointwise 95% interval 0.015–0.220) and 5° (0.183, 0.022–0.339). Precipitation seasonality was positively associated with chroma (0.206, 0.067–0.348; and 0.226, 0.046–0.375, respectively). Annual precipitation–chroma coefficients did not have pointwise intervals excluding zero at both grains. No single climatic axis met the same two-grain criterion for outline ratio. The water-related patterns therefore differ by trait: the amount of rainfall corresponds to photographed head presentation, whereas precipitation seasonality corresponds to photographed colour intensity. These are exploratory pointwise intervals, not adjusted significance of the complete coefficient family.

### 3.3. New climatic occupancy coexists with broad resemblance to native phenotypes

Background-corrected climate overlap between native and introduced ranges was 0.474 in *C. arvense* and 0.442 in *C. vulgare*. The proportions of introduced occupancy density outside their native 95% cores were 18.8% and 44.0%, respectively; the reciprocal fractions of native occupancy outside introduced cores were 47.7% and 7.9%. These asymmetries describe distinct, species-specific climate spaces rather than literal interchange of niches between species.

In all eligible introduced cells, native-reference three-trait overlap was 0.894 and 0.917 in analogous climates, and 0.732 and 0.845 in non-analogue climates, for *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare*, respectively. After geographical matching, the corresponding contrasts were 0.714 to 0.732 and 0.864 to 0.817 (two-species Holm P = 0.559 and 0.124). Thus newly occupied climates contained phenotypes broadly overlapping with those recorded in native ranges, without robust evidence of a general loss of overlap. Climate D and phenotype D are distinct metrics and cannot be subtracted to estimate a mechanistic effect.

### 3.4. Existing phenotype composition provides more specific hypotheses than phenotypic novelty

Relative to the rarefied native references, the frequency of native-unobserved combinations changed from 15.22% to 11.58% in *C. arvense* and from 13.97% to 13.98% in *C. vulgare* (analogous to non-analogue climates; adjusted P = 1 for both). Native-observed combinations accounted for 87.52% and 82.93% of positive frequency redistribution; however, the null means were already 84.60% and 81.81% (raw one-sided P = 0.0684 and 0.2991; adjusted P = 1). No general expansion of phenotypic dispersion was supported. We therefore found neither robust evidence of increased native-unobserved variation nor an excess contribution of existing combinations beyond the distributional null. These negative tests do not establish equivalence or the absence of novel genetic variants.

The chroma distributions provided a more informative descriptive contrast. Using the all-eligible three-trait cohort and full native reference, the low-chroma fraction declined from 18.44% to 11.35% in *C. arvense* and from 20.31% to 10.87% in *C. vulgare*. The high-chroma fraction declined from 27.72% to 20.00% in the former but increased from 23.36% to 26.98% in the latter. These are matched spatial contrasts, not changes tracked through time.

The multivariate relative-enrichment model for *C. vulgare* yielded a positive chroma linear coefficient (b = 0.2696, nominal P = 0.0183, primary Holm P = 0.1831) and negative chroma curvature (c = −0.1154, nominal P = 0.0094, Holm P = 0.1128). Conditional on the other traits, relative enrichment of the highest versus lowest native chroma quintile was 2.94 (pointwise 95% interval 1.40–6.17). The relative curve peaked in the fourth quintile rather than increasing monotonically. The angle curve was convex (c = 0.1260, nominal P = 0.0101, Holm P = 0.1128), with both extreme quintiles more represented relative to the middle. In *C. arvense*, the chroma curve was concave and the angle tilt negative, but none of the six terms was nominally significant. Neither primary six-term omnibus test was significant (P = 0.117 and 0.323); the direct difference between the two chroma linear coefficients was also unresolved (P = 0.323). The chroma tilt of *C. vulgare* persisted within shared major climate backgrounds (relative enrichment 2.95, raw P = 0.0469) but was weaker under the density-surplus definition (1.41, P = 0.319). No coefficient survived the full Holm-24 family, and out-of-region predictive improvements were limited. The three curve shapes are hypotheses rather than established selection regimes.

### 3.5. Water-related contrasts depend on environmental support

The two species occupied different water environments in the primary matched comparison. The median within-pair precipitation difference was +190 mm for *C. arvense* and −36 mm for *C. vulgare*, but corresponding mean differences were positive for both (+343.62 and +32.71 mm). Precipitation seasonality increased by a median of 0.90 and 3.45 units. Under the density-surplus definition, both species showed negative mean precipitation differences. Consequently, the focal species cannot generally be characterized as expanding towards opposite ends of a moisture gradient.

At the common precipitation reference of 759.93 mm, a 20-unit increase in BIO15 corresponded to a conditional change in high-chroma frequency of −33.83 and +4.51 percentage points in the primary analysis, and −11.40 and +3.19 points in the density-surplus analysis. The nominal species contrasts were P = 0.0118 and 0.0041, respectively; neither survived the planned multiple-comparison family. Neither the precipitation-by-seasonality term nor its species contrast had robust support.

Joint water support in the primary classification was too sparse to estimate the species contrast (18 *C. arvense* pairs in seven blocks and 70 *C. vulgare* pairs in 13 blocks). In the density-surplus classification, restriction to shared support retained 147 and 226 pairs in 13 and 15 blocks. The high-chroma seasonality contrast decreased from +14.59 to +5.85 percentage points (pointwise 95% interval −3.08 to +14.79; raw cluster P = 0.179 and score-sign P = 0.260). All 20 geographic leave-one-block-out species contrasts remained positive, but neither species' own coefficient excluded zero. The annual-precipitation coefficients were also small and both positive in this restricted sample (+1.92 and +0.83 points per 500 mm), with wide uncertainty. This attenuation is descriptive; restriction changes the target sample and precision, and we cannot identify a common response or demonstrate that differences in climate exposure cause the contrasting phenotype compositions.

### 3.6. Opposing regional contrasts and the spatial distribution of phenotypic variation

In *C. arvense*, regional chroma differences were positive in five of nine regions and negative in four under the primary definition. The pair-weighted positive and negative contributions to the standardized mean contrast were +0.204 and −0.204 native SD, yielding a net difference of +0.0006 SD (99.85% cancellation of the weighted absolute regional contrasts). Under the density-surplus definition, the corresponding cancellation was 98.53%. In *C. vulgare*, positive regional differences accounted for most pair weight (91.8% in the primary definition and 93.7% in the density-surplus definition), yielding net contrasts of +0.218 and +0.170 native SD and cancellation of 21.07% and 14.70%, respectively. These are descriptive contrasts; the two definitions reuse records and regions.

In the primary *C. arvense* comparison, among-cell chroma variance decreased from 0.417 to 0.168 native SD squared, whereas within-cell variance was 0.456 and 0.475. The decline comprised both among-region and among-cell within-region components. Among-cell variance also decreased under the density-surplus definition, although the two spatial components were individually uncertain and within-cell variance increased. In *C. vulgare*, the primary angle variance increase was mainly among cells (+0.175 SD squared out of a total +0.192); this decomposition was not maintained under the density-surplus classification. Pointwise variance-component intervals and both definitions are reported in SI. These results do not demonstrate a general increase or decrease in within-population variance.

The fixed climate-dependent shape models did not improve held-out conditional allocation relative to the constant enrichment model in the completed comparisons. For primary *C. vulgare* and density-surplus *C. arvense*, all-five-axis models worsened pair-weighted loss by 3.20% and 1.79%, respectively. Primary *C. arvense* lacked adequate training support in one fold, and the all-five-axis model for density-surplus *C. vulgare* had one incomplete fit. No failed fold was silently omitted. Thus the regional structure of the observed trait contrasts supplies additional descriptive hypotheses, but the climatic drivers of the enrichment curves remain unidentified.

### 3.7. Direct regional checks of curve shape and reconstruction from water-related climatic differences

Equal regional weighting retained the signs of the primary concave chroma coefficient in *C. arvense* (−0.196 to −0.508), positive chroma tilt in *C. vulgare* (0.270 to 0.171) and convex angle coefficient in *C. vulgare* (0.126 to 0.246). However, in the density-surplus classification the equal-region chroma tilt in *C. vulgare* was only 0.005, and the primary chroma tilt received 61.3% of its absolute adjusted score contribution from one region. In the primary *C. vulgare* angle comparison, both extreme quintiles had larger frequencies in 27 of 146 individual matched cell pairs and seven of 14 regional aggregates; the corresponding counts under the alternative classification were 55 of 316 and five of 15. These are different aggregation units, and a positive curvature in the conditional model is not equivalent to simultaneous increases in the two marginal tails.

The direct water-only reconstruction did not adequately reproduce the shapes of the primary enrichment curves. For *C. vulgare*, the held-out species-specific water model including curvature improved conditional pair-weighted log loss by 0.953% relative to the no-enrichment comparator, compared with 0.999% for the constant six-term model. Its region-weighted gain relative to no enrichment was slightly negative, and only six of 14 regions had lower loss. Re-estimation of the enrichment curve from the predicted allocations retained a positive but attenuated chroma tilt (0.107 versus the observed 0.270), yet failed to reproduce negative chroma curvature (0.012 versus −0.115) or positive angle curvature (−0.005 versus 0.126). The respective log-curve RMSEs were 0.403 and 0.346; the constant model gave 0.054 and 0.017. These comparisons directly distinguish allocation skill from reproduction of the shape of the observed relative-enrichment curves.

Primary *C. arvense* lacked training support in one fold; full-fold evaluations of all fitted models for that species and of shared-response models for both species were therefore unavailable. Under the density-surplus classification all fits completed, but the two shared water models and the species-specific water models yielded only negligible improvements or deterioration relative to no enrichment (at most 0.033% improvement for *C. arvense*, and deterioration for all four water models in *C. vulgare*). Thus the shared or species-specific water-response hypotheses examined here do not provide a consistent out-of-region explanation of the observed curves. We cannot conclude that the two species share an environmental response, that their responses differ, or that water has no ecological effect.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Connecting climatic resemblance with within-species variation

Our first set of analyses demonstrates local climate-conditioned resemblance across multiple visible floral traits in thistles. The signal within taxa was present at both grains, whereas between-taxon evidence depended in part on spatial grain and the climatic definition. This matters because similar climates can be occupied by similar phenotypes through several processes, even without independent evolutionary convergence. The geographic distribution of a species' phenotypes is one component of the phenotype differences observed across taxa. The second part therefore asks a different question: within the same introduced species, which regions of its observed phenotypic variation are represented in non-analogue climates?

Although *C. vulgare* had a greater fraction of its introduced climate occupancy outside the native climatic core than *C. arvense*, it retained substantial overlap with native floral phenotypes. This combination motivates a distinction between the degree of environmental novelty and the degree of phenotypic novelty. A climatic occupancy difference need not require a correspondingly novel floral phenotype. It is consistent with ecological fitting, in which existing attributes or flexible expression enable occupation of new contexts (Agosta & Klemens, 2008), but does not establish successful establishment *because of* a particular existing floral phenotype. The absence of a supported increase in native-unobserved bins, coupled with the near-null share of positive redistribution in native-observed bins, is not evidence that selection preferentially recruits standing genetic variation. Finite sampling, trait discretization and broad occupied bins can themselves generate substantial overlap.

### 4.2. Rainfall and the functional ecology of capitulum orientation

The association between annual precipitation and downward photographic presentation accords with the hypothesis that head orientation modifies exposure of pollen and other reproductive tissues to rain. In a separate Asteraceae lineage, experimental reorientation of nodding *Cremanthodium campanulatum* capitula reduced achene production; the same study linked water exposure to reduced pollen viability (Chen et al., 2013). This is a plausible functional mechanism for the broad-scale association but not a direct demonstration in thistles. Orientation can also affect visitor access, pollen transfer, heat exchange and display visibility. An experiment on a zygomorphic flower found that horizontal presentation could balance rain protection and pollination efficiency (see DOI/PMID reference below). The optimal direction is therefore not necessarily universally downward.

This functional trade-off makes the exploratory convex angle curve of *C. vulgare* worth retaining, rather than focusing only on colour. Relatively high representation of both angular extremes might reflect spatially varying optima: lower angles in contexts where display and access matter, and larger angles where exposure of reproductive tissues is costly. A mixture of populations each having a different local optimum could generate a U-shaped pooled enrichment curve without disruptive selection operating within any population. Alternatively, variation in head developmental stage, camera viewpoint or orientation, and founder composition can produce similar patterns. Our angle coefficient lacked adjusted support and no robust precipitation–angle transfer was established within the focal species. Thus a rain-protection versus pollen-transfer trade-off is a testable hypothesis for floral presentation, not an identified explanation of the angle curve. No evidence of evolutionary branching, bimodality within populations, or incipient speciation follows from the pooled curve.

### 4.3. Colour intensity as a multifunctional trait: seasonal water stress and trade-offs

Flower colour is a biotic signal and may covary with physiologically important pigment pathways. Dalrymple et al. (2020) documented associations of broad-scale flower colour with abiotic and biotic conditions; they linked lower annual precipitation to more chromatically conspicuous flowers. In an experiment on five polymorphic species, Warren and Mackenzie (2001) found a performance interaction between pigmentation and water treatment, with pigmented morphs performing relatively better under drought and unpigmented morphs under well-watered conditions. Sullivan and Koski (2021) found contrasting relationships of anthocyanin-colour morph frequencies with temperature and aridity in a historical herbarium study. Together these results provide biological grounds for environmental dependence of pigmentation-related performance, but none is a demonstration that photographed C* in *Cirsium* is a measure of pigment amount or drought tolerance.

Our own association was with precipitation seasonality conditional on annual precipitation, not a simple annual rainfall gradient. High BIO15 can describe sites with pronounced dry and wet periods even at similar annual totals; if dry periods coincide with floral development, episodic water limitation could favour physiological responses correlated with pigmentation. Temperature, radiation, water demand and pollinator communities may covary with this seasonal structure. We did not measure the coincidence of dry periods with flowering, plant water status, vapour pressure deficit, pigments or visitor-mediated fitness. The proposed physiological mechanism is therefore conditional on future evidence.

The positive tilt and negative curvature of the *C. vulgare* chroma curve together suggest a candidate shift towards relatively high, but not necessarily the most extreme, chroma. If pigment-associated stress protection and the costs or other functional consequences of pigmentation compete, a moving intermediate optimum is one possible explanation. An alternative is that coloration is plastically expressed under environmental conditions, without phenotypes differing in reproductive success. The concave *C. arvense* curve suggests a candidate concentration near intermediate phenotypes, but its uncertain coefficients prevent identifying an optimum. Neither a physiological trade-off nor a cost of pigmentation was measured. The observed data motivate testing these possibilities rather than declaring high chroma beneficial.

### 4.4. What directional, stabilizing and disruptive selection would predict

Classical phenotypic selection analyses relate individual relative fitness to standardized traits and their nonlinear terms (Lande & Arnold, 1983). Selection and inheritance are empirically distinct: selection can act on nonheritable variation, whereas evolutionary response requires transmission across generations (Svensson, 2023). Our models have a different response: the conditional density of an observation in non-analogue relative to analogous climatic cells. Under a hypothetical single selection episode on a common source population, with no phenotype transitions, immigration or differential observation, a post-selection versus pre-selection phenotype density ratio would be proportional to mean relative fitness conditional on phenotype. Those assumptions do not apply here: the compared groups are contemporary, geographically different populations with unknown ancestry and observation processes. Environmental covariance can bias even selection analyses with measured fitness (Rausher, 1992), so treating our cross-sectional ratios as selection estimates would be especially unjustified.

The directional chroma tilt of *C. vulgare*, concave chroma enrichment in *C. arvense* and convex angle enrichment in *C. vulgare* are accordingly best described as directional, intermediate-favouring-looking and extreme-enriching patterns. The term "favouring-looking" refers only to relative observation density; in the Results we use "intermediate enrichment" and "extreme enrichment". If fitness experiments supported them, the first could be consistent with directional selection, the second with stabilizing selection and the third with disruptive selection. The positive tilt plus negative curvature in *C. vulgare* also illustrates why these descriptions are not mutually exclusive categorical labels: a shifted optimum can produce both linear and quadratic terms over the sampled ranks. A positive quadratic coefficient alone does not establish disruptive selection, nor does a negative one prove stabilizing selection; the shape and location of extrema, trait range, uncertainty and fitness evidence all matter. Our model does not estimate correlational selection among traits because it omits cross-products between trait ranks. The primary omnibus tests, multiple-comparison results, alternate climate definitions and direct species contrast do not establish these three processes in thistles.

### 4.5. Different exposure trajectories can create apparent differences between species

We initially considered whether the two species respond differently to moisture variation. The matched climate differences show why "non-analogue" does not mean the same ecological treatment. In the primary classification, *C. arvense* had a wetter median paired climate contrast and *C. vulgare* a drier one, whereas mean paired precipitation differences were positive in both. In the alternative classification both mean differences were negative. Consequently, a simple wet versus dry narrative is insufficient. Comparisons must distinguish annual totals from within-year variability, and exposure changes from conditional response coefficients.

The attenuation of the seasonality contrast in shared water support and the small, same-sign annual precipitation coefficients suggest that unequal exposure distributions may contribute to apparent between-species differences. They do not establish a shared physiological response: uncertainty is wide, the restricted sample differs from the full sample, and only water-related support was controlled. Nor can the attenuation be called a percentage of the species difference causally explained by water. The observed contrast could result from a common nonlinear environmental response sampled at different parts of its domain, a genuine difference in response between taxa, or geographical and historical differences correlated with climate. An important general implication is therefore methodological and ecological: species-specific climatic novelty must be linked to actual environmental coordinates before a difference in the distribution of phenotypes is attributed to a different selective regime. This also explains why the positive across-taxon precipitation-seasonality–chroma relationship in Part 1 cannot be assumed to predict the within-species response in Part 2.

### 4.5a. Spatial heterogeneity can mask mean contrasts without establishing different selection regimes

The nearly complete cancellation of signed regional chroma differences in *C. arvense* contrasts with the predominance of positive pair-weighted regional differences in *C. vulgare*. The former's smaller among-cell chroma variance in non-analogue climates is consistent with a spatial narrowing of cell-level phenotype means rather than a general contraction of within-cell variation. Importantly, this is a contrast among contemporary sets of matched cells, not temporal convergence of populations. Regional sign cancellation and variance partitioning characterize continuous marginal traits; they do not algebraically explain the conditional nonlinear enrichment curves. Indeed, the original enrichment model already controls each matched pair's intercept. The convex angle pattern in *C. vulgare* may be related to among-cell heterogeneity in the primary comparison, but that variance contrast did not persist under the alternative classification. Distinct local optima, shared environmental responses sampled in different contexts, founder composition, plasticity and sampling differences remain competing hypotheses. The lack of transfer improvement in the fixed climate-dependent shape models precludes attributing these spatial patterns to the measured annual climate axes alone.

### 4.5b. The two water-related associations do not reconstruct the observed enrichment shapes

A particularly useful link between the two parts of the study is the distinction between association and explanatory sufficiency. The annual-precipitation–angle and precipitation-seasonality–chroma associations in the native comparisons motivate specific environmental hypotheses for the introduced species. However, even allowing the two water differences to alter the directional and quadratic enrichment terms did not reproduce the primary *C. vulgare* chroma peak and angle U-shape in held-out regions. The weak conditional allocation skill was not equivalent to recovering the shape. The observed curves cannot therefore be presented as the demonstrated outcome of the two specified climatic responses or as evidence of their corresponding selection regimes. Regional differences in the mean trait contrast and in the spatial variance components offer additional descriptive constraints: any explanation should account for both the distributions of phenotypes and their spatial structure. The failure of this particular water-only model leaves open nonlinear responses to other environmental coordinates, flowering-period exposure, pollination, historical source composition, plasticity and photographic observation effects. No further outcome-driven search across climatic axes is undertaken here. The shared-response test was underpowered in the primary classification and did not improve prediction consistently in the alternative one, so it cannot resolve a common versus species-specific response mechanism.

### 4.6. Native variation and life history as alternative explanations

The two species contrast in both observed native climate occupancy and colour variation. *C. arvense* occupies a broader native central climate range on all five variables, whereas the native q20–q80 chroma width of *C. vulgare* is about 1.92 times greater (24.28 versus 12.63 C*). A broad existing phenotype distribution may permit more visible redistribution when new environments are occupied, whereas a broad existing climatic occupancy may reduce the fraction of introduced environments outside the native core. These are observations from two cases, not a test of the causal effect of trait variance or climate niche breadth. Photographic width is not genetic variance, and wider observed colour variation does not necessarily imply greater evolvability.

The perennial, largely dioecious *C. arvense* spreads extensively through roots and can persist vegetatively (Tiley, 2010). *C. vulgare* is usually biennial or a short-lived monocarpic plant, with reproduction predominantly via seeds (Zouhar, 2002). Consequently, the demographic contribution of sexual reproduction may differ. In *C. vulgare*, reproductive display might have a larger contribution to lifetime population growth if seed production is limiting; in *C. arvense*, vegetative persistence may buffer some variation in seed production. This does not imply that flower traits are unimportant in the latter: sexual reproduction requires pollen transfer and supplies dispersing offspring. Sex-specific capitulum structure and repeated clonal ramets in *C. arvense* also complicate photographic distributions. Differences in population age, introduction routes, founding phenotype composition, plasticity and imaging conditions remain plausible. Two species cannot identify the demographic mechanism, and no lack of adaptation in *C. arvense* is inferred.

### 4.7. Limitations, implications and decisive next tests

Our photographs describe visible phenotypes rather than calibrated biological traits or individual fitness. Environmental association, observer selection, lighting, stage and projection can affect both apparent chroma and angle. Geographic balancing and support restrictions reduce some imbalances, but neither establish representative population sampling nor identify ancestry. The native range is contemporary rather than an ancestral population, and introduced geographic blocks are not known independent invasion events. The full reference already occupied all 125 internal phenotype bins; the rarefaction and null analyses were essential to avoid confusing bin saturation with preferential use of existing variation. The estimated tails and relative curves should be read with their complete uncertainty and sensitivity results, not with nominal coefficients selected after exploration.

A decisive test would rear multiple source and introduced families or genotypes in replicated common gardens crossed with annual water amount and seasonality, preferably with controlled developmental timing and radiation. Standardized spectral and chemical measurements would separate colour expression from pigment concentration; orientation manipulations and rainfall treatments would quantify pollen damage, pollen transfer and seed production. Repeated phenotyping of the same genotypes would distinguish plasticity from heritable differences. Reproductive fitness, survival and clonal propagation where relevant would support estimation of environment-specific directional, stabilizing and disruptive selection; ancestry-informed population genomic sampling would be needed to distinguish adaptation from pre-existing alleles versus new mutations. These are proposed experiments, not analyses completed here.

In summary, the central contribution is a geographically extensive comparison of floral resemblance across climatic analogues together with a species-specific examination of phenotype composition in newly occupied climates. Existing phenotypes remain common in those climates, and the colour and angle curves motivate functional hypotheses about environmental sorting. The evidence supports climate-associated resemblance more strongly than the particular selection-like patterns. Integrating the two biological scales gives a framework for identifying candidate environmental and phenotypic dimensions for experimental work, without overinterpreting contemporary occurrence photographs as a record of evolutionary change.
