# Climatic resemblance and the redistribution of floral variation in native and introduced thistles

Working research article — V7 result freeze, 28 November 2026.
Main scientific results are frozen; no new model was fitted for this editorial revision. Earlier manuscripts and all positive and negative analyses remain archived.

## Abstract

**Aim.** Floral diversity may reflect environmental associations accumulated across native ranges and provide existing variation relevant to occupancy of new environments. We asked whether climatic resemblance corresponds to floral resemblance, and whether novel climatic occupancy coincides with retention or redistribution of native-observed floral phenotypes.

**Location.** Global native ranges of thistle taxa and native and introduced ranges of two widespread species.

**Methods.** We quantified photographic head orientation, floral chroma and projected outline, using 157,822 eligible native records as the source for balanced within- and among-taxon comparisons. For *Cirsium arvense* and *Cirsium vulgare*, we compared background-corrected climatic occupancy, Gaussian phenotype hypervolumes and geographically calibrated climate/trait novelty, and estimated continuous chroma enrichment across matched ranges and introduced climatic contexts.

**Results.** Climatic resemblance corresponded to multivariate phenotype resemblance within taxa at both spatial grains and among taxa at the coarser grain. Rainfall corresponded to downward orientation and precipitation seasonality to higher chroma. Raw five-dimensional occupied-climate volumes were 0.91 and 2.16 times their native volumes in *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare*, respectively; 98.3% and 81.5% of novel-climate phenotype volumes overlapped native volumes. Shared fractions remained above 50% in equal-cell-number sensitivity draws. Climate and trait novelty were positively associated within introduced regions in *C. vulgare* (Holm P = 0.018). In this species, climate-matched range contrasts showed convex chroma enrichment (quadratic Holm P = 0.0028), whereas introduced novel-climate contrasts showed concave enrichment tilted towards higher chroma (quadratic Holm P = 0.0264), sensitive to novelty definition.

**Main conclusions.** Climatically structured floral diversity coexists with broad native phenotype overlap and context-dependent representation of existing phenotypes in introduced ranges. The patterns motivate the hypothesis that existing variation facilitates responses to new environments; they do not demonstrate natural selection, genetic adaptation or its rate.

Keywords: capitulum; climatic occupancy; Gaussian hypervolume; floral chroma; intraspecific variation; biological invasion; phenotypic redistribution; citizen-science imagery.

## 1. Introduction

Floral diversity is relevant to both reproductive function and the occupation of heterogeneous environments. Floral orientation modifies exposure of reproductive structures to rain, while pigmentation participates in visual signalling and potentially in physiological responses to abiotic conditions. These functions suggest that floral phenotypes may be structured by multiple environmental dimensions rather than a single climatic axis (Chen et al., 2013; Dalrymple et al., 2020; Sullivan & Koski, 2021). Joint examination of multiple traits can reveal whether phenotypes resemble one another in similar environments and whether individual traits correspond to distinct environmental gradients.

Environmental associations accumulated across taxa and geographical populations have implications beyond their current distribution. A species may already contain phenotypes relevant to the occupation of new environments. Ecological fitting describes establishment using attributes already available to organisms, including phenotypically flexible expression (Agosta & Klemens, 2008). Adaptation from standing genetic variation, by contrast, requires heritable variation and differential fitness in the relevant ancestral populations (Barrett & Schluter, 2008). Contemporary phenotype distributions can motivate these mechanisms but cannot distinguish them directly. Invasions provide historically distinct settings in which trait distributions can be compared across environments (Hulme & Barrett, 2013).

We distinguish three aspects of phenotypic redistribution: displacement of a distribution's centre, change in its dispersion, and altered relative representation of particular trait values. The third is especially informative where similar means conceal opposing changes at the tails. A species occupying climates similar to its native range might retain much of its observed phenotype space, whereas greater climatic novelty might coincide with changes in the representation of existing phenotypes, with or without newly observed trait combinations. These are not mutually exclusive invasion strategies. A broad native climatic niche and broad standing phenotype distributions need not coincide, and neither observational property alone establishes an evolutionary capacity for adaptation.

Here we use geographically linked photographs of thistles to connect two biological scales. First, within the native ranges of sampled taxa, we ask whether climatic resemblance predicts resemblance in capitulum orientation, chroma and outline, and which annual climatic dimensions correspond to the signed trait differences. Second, within two widespread species, we ask whether changes in climate occupancy coexist with broad retention of native-observed phenotype space, whether phenotype distributions differ even between climate-matched ranges, and which parts of those distributions are relatively enriched in newly occupied climates. Finally, we distinguish overall trait shifts from spatial heterogeneity in the introduced comparisons. We treat selection terminology as motivation, not as an estimand: observed density ratios are not relative fitness or selection gradients (Lande & Arnold, 1983; Svensson, 2023). The final analytical focus was developed through exploration and does not constitute independent preregistered confirmation.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Photographic measurements and environmental data

We retained three continuous visible-image measures from the archived measurement pipeline. Candidate capitula were localized by an object detector (YOLO); separate image-processing routines calculated (i) the direction of the capitulum's principal axis towards floral pixels relative to the image vertical (0° toward image top, 180° toward bottom, after available EXIF correction), (ii) CIELAB chroma C* = sqrt(a*² + b*²) over segmented floral pixels, and (iii) the height-to-width ratio of the projected outline bounding box after PCA-based rotation. Chroma quantifies photographed visible colour intensity, not calibrated spectral reflectance, anthocyanin concentration or pollinator-perceived colour. The angle is not gravity-calibrated, and the outline ratio may depend on camera projection and rotation. Record-level medians summarize usable heads; different eligible heads can contribute to different traits in one record. We therefore interpret the joint measurements as photographic-record phenotypes, not necessarily co-measured traits of an identical individual capitulum.

The final climatic definition uses five annual CHELSA v2.1 variables: annual mean temperature (BIO1), temperature seasonality (BIO4), annual precipitation (BIO12), precipitation seasonality (BIO15), and mean shortwave radiation. BIO15 represents relative monthly variability in precipitation rather than drought exposure during a plant's flowering period. No elevation, lightness, hue or temporal trend is included in the adopted models. Source-specific quality gates, geographic deduplication and sampling units are documented in the supporting information. Counts from the two parts describe distinct analysis cohorts and must not be summed.

### 2.2. Part 1: climatic resemblance and multivariate phenotype distances

The native photographic source contained 157,822 eligible records from 242 taxon names prior to geographic coverage selection. We summarized eligible records by taxon and 1° or 5° geographic cell using coordinate medians. Taxa with at least 40 records and eight occupied cells qualified for the balanced resampling design. Standardization was based on equal-taxon-weighted scales. The 1° and 5° cohorts contained 63 taxa (7,117 taxon–cell units) and 31 taxa (814 taxon–cell units), respectively.

For each of 24 rarefactions, eight cells were sampled per taxon. Climatic analogues were defined using Euclidean distance on the standardized five-variable climate vector, with threshold 0.9 sqrt(5/3) ≈ 1.162 to retain the same per-axis root-mean-square criterion as the three-variable baseline. We compared the mean Euclidean distance between three-trait vectors for all cell combinations with that among climatic analogues, both within taxa and among taxa assigned to different dominant longitude sectors. Qualified pairs required adequate analogue cells on both sides. The null permuted complete phenotype vectors within taxa, preserving their trait distributions and covariation. Statistics were averaged over rarefactions before permutation inference; cells, repeated draws and taxon pairs sharing a taxon were not treated as independent replicate experiments. The final annual five-axis comparisons used 499 permutation realizations and Holm correction across the two grains for each resemblance test. The three-variable annual comparison on the same source cohort was retained as a sensitivity analysis.

### 2.3. Part 1: signed environmental and trait contrasts

For the sampled pairs of distinct taxa, we calculated signed cell-level differences in standardized climate and traits. We removed the average climatic and phenotypic difference specific to each taxon pair, and fitted the five climatic differences simultaneously. This estimand characterizes local contrasts conditional on taxon-pair identity rather than a regression of species means or an independently evolved phylogenetic contrast. We obtained pointwise 95% intervals using 499 taxon-resampling weights, retaining dependence of pairs sharing taxa. The two spatial grains reuse the same source records. These exploratory coefficients were not established by a multiplicity-corrected family of all signed contrasts. Geographic autocorrelation, ancestry and unmeasured environmental covariates can remain.

### 2.4. Part 2: focal species and climatic occupancy

The focal species, *Cirsium arvense* and *C. vulgare*, were chosen for observational coverage in both native and introduced ranges. A retrospective WCVP audit of 350 source species-rank names identified seven with explicit wild native and introduced records; only these two met the coverage criteria in both ranges (at least 100 angle-assessable observations, 15 broad approximately equal-area 5° cells and 10 observers per range). The selection is therefore feasibility-based, not a random sample of thistles. The original focal source contained 297,622 wild records before observer–cell deduplication; 144,178 were retained in the climate cohort. The complete three-trait cohort comprised 18,505 native and 22,269 introduced records in *C. arvense*, and 13,115 and 25,532 in *C. vulgare*, respectively. These Part 2 records overlap the broader Part 1 source and must not be added to it.

For each species, we projected five annual CHELSA variables into a species-specific common two-dimensional PCA-env space using the available climates of both ranges with equal range weighting. We estimated smoothed occurrence densities corrected for background climate availability (PCA-env-style approximation, not an exact ecospat implementation; Broennimann et al., 2012). Schoener's D was the sum of cellwise minima of two normalized density grids. We computed the fraction of introduced density outside the native 95% probability-content core, and the reciprocal fraction for the native density outside the introduced core. These fractions quantify occupied climate distributions, not geographic area, physiological niche limits or historical changes in tolerance. We used the native 95% core for the primary definition of analogous and novel introduced climate cells, retaining the previously defined density-surplus classification as a sensitivity analysis.

### 2.5. Part 2: phenotype-space occupancy and native-reference variation

We summarized complete three-trait records as cell medians, yielding 1,366 native and 860 introduced 1° cells for *C. arvense* and 1,113 and 1,139 for *C. vulgare*. We standardized each species by its native-cell reference and estimated smoothed three-dimensional phenotype densities on the same grid for all ranges and climate groups (40 bins per axis, 0.5 native SD smoothing; 0.35 and 0.75 in sensitivity analyses). The earlier two-dimensional marginal density plots and their D2D labels are retained in SI. Figure 4 now displays standard Gaussian hypervolume set-operation point clouds (Section 2.5a), distinct from the grid density-overlap estimates. A distinct analysis retained all eligible observations with weights inverse to the number of observations in the cell, thereby preserving within-cell variation. We report it separately as a sensitivity analysis, not as the source of the plotted cell-median clouds.

For within-introduced contrasts, analogous and novel 1° cells were matched without replacement within 20° geographical blocks by minimum geographical separation (30 pairs in nine blocks for *C. arvense*; 146 in 14 for *C. vulgare*). Under the density-surplus definition, 183 and 316 pairs were available. Region-level label exchange tested changes in phenotype overlap. To evaluate apparently novel phenotype combinations under incomplete sampling, we retained the existing 32 rarefactions (256 reference and 128 geographically held-out native cells) and joint native quintile/outside-range bins. Positive frequency changes in native-observed and native-unobserved bins were compared with the geographical-block null, with the full 32-test Holm family. This statistic does not estimate recruitment of ancestral alleles.

### 2.5a. Standard Gaussian hypervolumes and calibrated novelty

We fitted Gaussian hypervolumes using R 4.4.3 and hypervolume 3.1.7 (Blonder et al., 2014, 2018), to the established 1° cell medians, centered and scaled using each species' native cell-median mean and population SD. Bandwidth was fixed at 0.5 native SD per axis, sd.count = 3, with 95% probability content. Three-dimensional phenotype fits targeted 60,000 stochastic points before thresholding, and hypervolume_set comparisons were capped at 12,000 points. We distinguished intersection/comparison volume (shared fraction) from intersection/union (Jaccard). Novel climate was defined by the established background-corrected PCA-env 95% native core. Native/novel-climate trait comparisons included 1,366/30 and 1,113/190 cells, respectively. We doubled target point counts and raised the set cap, varied bandwidth (0.35/0.75), content (90%/99%) and trait dimensionality (angle/chroma only), and conducted eight fixed equal-cell-number draws. Equal-n draws are sampling sensitivity, not geographical matching or confidence intervals. The 108-comparison package pilot does not add inferential tests of expansion or turnover; the earlier regional block tests remain in SI.

Separate five-dimensional Gaussian fits to the observed annual climate values of the same complete-head cells used native standardization and a 100,000-point target. They quantify raw occupied-climate volume, not environmental availability-corrected niche. Background-corrected PCA-env remains the primary climate depiction. The separate five-dimensional background-corrected grid approximation (20 bins per axis, h = 0.5 pooled background SD, 95% content) has coarse numerical resolution and is reported as sensitivity, not a precise volume estimate.

For calibrated novelty, we used 32 fixed draws of geographically disjoint 256 native reference and 128 calibration cells. Mean distances to five reference neighbors in the five climate axes and separately in three or two trait axes were divided by their own held-out native 95th percentiles. Joint novelty category frequencies were averaged over draws, not derived by thresholding average distances. For the introduced-cell association test, we averaged normalized distances, ranked them globally, centered the ranks within geographical blocks and correlated the centered ranks. Regional covariance contributions were sign-reversed for inference, with Holm correction across four species-by-trait-dimension comparisons and leave-one-region-out sensitivity. Calibrated support categories are distinct from hypervolume intersection and background-corrected climate-density mass.

### 2.6. Part 2: continuous redistribution at matched climates and at climatic margins

We used two distinct comparisons to separate range-associated distribution differences from differences between introduced climatic contexts.

First, we directly matched native and introduced 1° cells using only their five annual climatic variables. Each variable was standardized using the native cell mean and SD. One-to-one matching maximized the number of admissible pairs and then minimized climate distance, using a root-mean-square caliper of 0.9/sqrt(3) native SD and a maximum per-axis difference of one native SD. No phenotype was used in matching. The matched cohorts contained 457 and 338 pairs, respectively, with native/introduced block counts of 20/17 and 13/20. All valid continuous phenotype values in these cells were retained, without novelty bins or truncation at native extrema. Climate balance is reported by axis, including residual differences.

Second, we compared the geographically matched analogous and novel cells *within* the introduced range, as defined in Section 2.5. The continuous refinement maintained the previous interior joint-phenotype support restriction, but used measured trait values, not quintile ranks. The alternative density-surplus definition used its own fixed matched cohort.

In both models, each cell contributed equal total weight and each matched pair had a profiled intercept. Conditional log density ratios contained first- and second-order terms for chroma, angle and outline simultaneously. For a trait standardized as z = (value − native median)/native SD, the relative density ratio at fixed values of the other two traits is R(z) = exp(bz + cz²), normalized to 1 at the native median. Figure 5 reports curves over each species' native 5th–95th percentile range in actual chroma units, not extrapolated tails. A positive curvature can indicate relative enrichment of extremes; a negative curvature with a positive tilt can indicate intermediate-to-high enrichment. Neither curvature is a selection gradient. The matched-range model used a sandwich covariance allowing pairs sharing geographical endpoints to be dependent, with t degrees of freedom based on the smaller block count minus one, and Holm correction across 12 coefficients. The within-introduced model used region-level score sign reversals and the full 24-coefficient Holm family (two species, three traits, two terms and two climatic definitions); shaded intervals are pointwise. The two comparisons use different cells, weights and contrasts and do not represent successive stages observed through time.

We retained the existing rank, full linear climate-slope, common-support, seasonal climate and held-out prediction analyses in SI. We also retained the descriptive comparison of native core versus native climatic periphery with the introduced analogous-versus-novel comparison. Correspondence between these curves is not a test of identical environmental transitions or adaptive processes.

### 2.7. Part 2: spatial structure of trait redistribution

For each species and climatic definition, we decomposed the novel-minus-analogous mean trait difference into positive and negative contributions of geographical blocks, weighted by their matched-pair counts. For block mean differences d_g and normalized pair weights w_g, the signed mean D_s = sum(w_g d_g) and absolute regional mean A = sum(w_g |d_g|) give the cancellation fraction 1 − |D_s|/A. This is a descriptive aggregation statistic, not a causal contribution or a species-difference test.

The equal-cell-weighted trait variance was partitioned into among-region, among-cell-within-region and within-cell components, all on the native SD² scale. We compared each component between analogous and novel cells and report pointwise geographical-block bootstrap intervals. Multivariate dispersion was the sum of the three standardized trait variances (covariance trace, not geometric hypervolume). Total and between-cell trace contrasts were tested by geographical-block label reversal, with Holm correction across the eight species-by-definition-by-metric tests. The earlier continuous and climate-based held-out reconstruction analyses are kept in SI to distinguish distributional evidence from an explanation of its environmental cause.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Native climatic analogues resemble one another in photographed capitulum traits

Within taxa, five-axis climatic analogues showed greater multivariate resemblance than expected under within-taxon permutation at both grains (standardized distance advantage over null: 0.084 at 1° and 0.238 at 5°; Holm P = 0.004 at both grains). Between taxa, the corresponding advantage was 0.031 at 1° (Holm P = 0.076) and 0.065 at 5° (P = 0.012). The matched three-axis sensitivity yielded between-taxon advantages of 0.033 and 0.050 (Holm P = 0.016 at both grains). Thus the evidence is strongest within taxa and consistent in direction between taxa, with weaker fine-grain support when seasonal climate is included. These patterns establish climate-conditioned resemblance, not independently evolved convergence.

### 3.2. Two dimensions of the water environment correspond to different floral traits

Conditional on taxon-pair average differences and the other four climatic axes, annual precipitation was positively associated with the downward-oriented photographic angle at 1° (standardized coefficient 0.117, pointwise 95% interval 0.015–0.220) and 5° (0.183, 0.022–0.339). Precipitation seasonality was positively associated with chroma (0.206, 0.067–0.348; and 0.226, 0.046–0.375, respectively). Annual precipitation–chroma coefficients did not have pointwise intervals excluding zero at both grains. No single climatic axis met the same two-grain criterion for outline ratio. The water-related patterns therefore differ by trait: the amount of rainfall corresponds to photographed head presentation, whereas precipitation seasonality corresponds to photographed colour intensity. These are exploratory pointwise intervals, not adjusted significance of the complete coefficient family.

### 3.3. Novel climatic occupancy coexists with broad native trait-space overlap

*Cirsium arvense* occupied broader native central climatic ranges on all five annual axes, whereas *C. vulgare* had a wider native floral chroma distribution (20th–80th percentile width 24.28 versus 12.63 C*). These differences distinguish two observational aspects of native breadth, not two proven invasion strategies.

Despite similar climate D values (0.474 and 0.442 in *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare*, respectively), the direction of non-overlap differed (Figure 4a,d). Introduced density outside the native 95% core accounted for 18.8% and 44.0%, respectively; reciprocal native density outside the introduced core accounted for 47.7% and 7.9%. These fractions do not describe a literal interchange of climatic niches between species. The raw five-axis Gaussian occupied-climate volume ratios (introduced/native) were 0.912 and 2.158; eight equal-n draws gave 0.887–1.107 and 2.009–2.199. Background-corrected coarse-grid ratios were separately 0.614 and 1.636; registered settings retained these directions, but the grids were too coarse for precise geometric estimates.

Head-trait distributions broadly overlapped across ranges in the grid-based supporting analysis (SI). Three-dimensional cell-median D between the native range and the entire introduced range was 0.831 and 0.869; the native-versus-novel-climate D was 0.735 and 0.827, respectively. Full cell-median native-reference overlaps for analogous versus novel introduced climates were 0.831 versus 0.735 and 0.859 versus 0.827. After geographical matching, these became 0.740 versus 0.735 and 0.847 versus 0.789 (two-species Holm P = 0.953 and 0.096). The separate observation-weighted analysis gave native-versus-novel D of 0.732 and 0.845 in the full cohort; its matched contrasts were likewise not supported after correction (P = 0.559 and 0.124). These results do not establish equivalence. New climatic occupancy coexisted with broad phenotype-space overlap rather than a demonstrated uniform increase in phenotypic novelty.

The standard three-dimensional Gaussian hypervolumes (Figure 4) yielded native-versus-novel-climate trait volume ratios of 0.335 and 0.866 and Jaccard overlaps of 0.327 and 0.609. The intersection accounted for 98.3% and 81.5% of the novel-climate trait volume; higher-point-density repeats gave 98.5% and 81.0%. Across eight equal-n draws, shared fractions were 72.3–88.2% and 63.5–75.7%, and volume ratios were 0.472–0.878 and 0.957–1.261. Shared volume remained a majority although the magnitude was sensitive to native coverage. All 16 earlier block tests of grid-based volume, turnover, centroid displacement and novelty mass were non-significant after correction (minimum adjusted P = 0.16). Geometric expansion, contraction and distinct species-specific radiation modes were therefore not established.

Among introduced cell-draws classified as climatically novel, 96.2% and 92.4% retained trait support within the native reference threshold; angle/chroma-only sensitivity gave 96.1% and 90.1%. These are draw-averaged classification frequencies, not geometric overlap fractions. Region-centered climate–trait rank correlations were 0.063 (857 cells, 15 regions; Holm P = 0.658) and 0.150 (1,126 cells, 24 regions; Holm P = 0.018), respectively. In *C. vulgare*, leave-one-region-out estimates were all positive (0.101–0.187), and the association persisted when outline was omitted (0.142, Holm P = 0.012). Broad native-observed trait support thus coexists with modestly greater trait novelty in less familiar climates in this species. The association does not establish species differences, causation, selection or inherited divergence.

Rarefied native-reference analyses detected no increase in native-unobserved combinations (15.22% to 11.58% and 13.97% to 13.98%, respectively; adjusted P = 1). Although native-observed combinations accounted for most positive frequency redistribution (87.52% and 82.93%), null expectations were already 84.60% and 81.81%; preferential representation of previously observed combinations was not supported after correction (SI). Climate D and phenotype D refer to different spaces, so their difference is not a measure of adaptive change.

### 3.4. Chroma redistribution differs between matched-range and novel-climate contrasts

The observed climate-matched distributions showed contrasting descriptive chroma widths (Figure 5a,d). Equal-cell-weighted chroma SD was 9.32 in native and 8.89 in introduced *C. arvense*, compared with 13.26 and 15.01 in *C. vulgare*. In the latter, the 10th–90th percentile range extended from 13.42–47.92 C* in native cells to 12.37–51.87 C* in introduced cells. The conditional matched-range model identified convex chroma enrichment in *C. vulgare* (quadratic coefficient 0.1417, pointwise 95% interval 0.0818–0.2015, Holm-12 P = 0.00284), with little linear tilt (P = 0.797 before adjustment). No term was supported after correction in *C. arvense*. Matching did not fully balance climates; introduced minus native mean radiation differences remained +0.338 and +0.306 native SD, respectively. These comparisons identify range-associated density differences within approximately comparable climates, not a climate-independent causal effect of introduction (Figure 5b,e).

Within introduced regions, the primary analogous-to-novel comparison showed a different pattern (Figure 5c,f). In *C. vulgare*, low-chroma frequency was approximately 20.3% in analogous and 10.9% in novel climates, while high-chroma frequency was 23.4% and 27.0%. In *C. arvense*, both low (18.4% to 11.3%) and high (27.7% to 20.0%) tails were less represented in novel cells. These marginal frequencies are descriptive. Conditional on the other head traits, the continuous *C. vulgare* chroma curve was positively tilted (b = 0.4564, pointwise 95% interval 0.1839–0.7288, Holm-24 P = 0.1128) and negatively curved (c = −0.1655, −0.2904 to −0.0406, Holm-24 P = 0.0264). The fitted curve reached its maximum near C* = 49 (relative density 1.37 at the native-median reference); this maximum was not independently estimated as a biological optimum. The positive angle curvature did not survive the full 24-test correction (P = 0.0730), and no term in *C. arvense* survived correction.

The negative chroma curvature did not persist under the density-surplus definition (c = 0.0393, Holm-24 P = 1). Climate-repaired subsets retained the primary *C. vulgare* chroma curvature sign descriptively, but changed the matched sample and do not provide independent confirmation. The earlier direct species comparison of rank-based chroma tilts was unresolved (raw P = 0.323); different within-species significance levels are not evidence of a species difference. The primary *C. vulgare* native-periphery curve also had positive tilt and negative curvature (0.280 and −0.084), but matched equivalent climate transitions across ranges lacked adequate support. Thus this resemblance provides a hypothesis, not a demonstrated shared response mechanism. All three traits, both novelty definitions, direct species contrasts and native-periphery support are retained in SI.

### 3.5. Supporting analyses: spatial structure and unresolved explanatory contrasts

The near-zero net chroma difference in *C. arvense* concealed positive and negative regional contrasts (Figure S1a). Under the primary definition, five of nine regions had positive and four negative contrasts, with pair-weighted signed contributions of +0.204 and −0.204 native SD. The cancellation fraction was 99.85%, compared with 21.07% in *C. vulgare*, for which positive regions accounted for 91.8% of the matched-pair weight and the net contrast was +0.218 native SD. Under the density-surplus definition, the cancellation fractions were 98.53% and 14.70%. This consistency is descriptive, not independent replication or a tested difference between species.

In *C. arvense*, among-cell chroma variance was 0.417 in analogous versus 0.168 in novel cells (native SD²), whereas within-cell variance was 0.456 versus 0.475 (Figure S1b). The among-cell decline had the same sign under the density-surplus definition, although the two spatial components were individually uncertain. This suggests a possible spatial narrowing of cell means without a corresponding loss of within-cell chroma variation. For *C. vulgare*, the primary angle variance increase was largely among cells, but this decomposition did not persist under the alternative definition. Total three-trait dispersion decreased by 43.5% in *C. arvense* and increased by 3.9% in *C. vulgare* in the primary matched comparison; under the alternative definition the changes were −1.6% and +4.1%. All eight multivariate dispersion tests were non-significant after Holm correction. The large primary decline was mostly attributable to the projected outline measure and is not robust evidence of morphological contraction (Figure S1d).

The annual-climate slope comparisons, native-prediction diagnostics, shared environmental support and held-out water-only reconstructions did not establish a general mechanism explaining the two species' redistribution curves. The annual slope comparisons did not establish range-specific slope differences after correction in either grain or climate-support cohort; individual coefficients are retained in the complete SI ledger. The among-taxon relationships in Part 1 should therefore not be transferred directly to either focal species. The two water axes did not adequately reconstruct the observed curve shapes in held-out regions. These results and unsupported comparisons are retained in SI rather than interpreted as established species-specific selection or the absence of climate effects.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Floral diversity as a present environmental pattern and a potential resource

Native climate-conditioned resemblance was supported most consistently within taxa, with evidence also among taxa at the coarser grain. Rainfall and precipitation seasonality corresponded to different visible traits. This establishes the first part of our framework: floral phenotype distributions are not unrelated to the environments occupied by thistles. It does not demonstrate independent convergence, ancestral states or the duration of natural selection. The second part asks how existing phenotypes are represented in a new geographic context. The standard Gaussian hypervolume and calibrated support-distance comparisons show broad native-observed trait overlap in novel climates despite raw occupied-climate expansion in *C. vulgare*. Shared volume remained a majority after equal-n subsampling, albeit with reduced overlap. The positive within-region association of climate and trait novelty further indicates that broad phenotype-space retention and environmentally associated differentiation can coexist. Ecological novelty need not be accompanied by an entirely distinct observed floral phenotype space. It is consistent with ecological fitting but cannot prove establishment because of a particular phenotype.

### 4.2. Water-related floral associations and their functional implications

The positive precipitation–downward-angle association motivates the hypothesis of protection of reproductive structures from rain; experimental orientation manipulations in another asteraceous lineage provide a functional precedent (Chen et al., 2013). Precipitation seasonality and chroma may correspond to multiple processes, including pigment-associated stress physiology and interactions with pollinators (Warren & Mackenzie, 2001; Dalrymple et al., 2020; Sullivan & Koski, 2021). Neither the observed angle nor uncalibrated photographic chroma directly measures pollen protection, pigment concentration, visual perception or drought tolerance. Annual precipitation seasonality does not measure actual drought exposure during flowering. Importantly, among-taxon associations should not be treated as within-species response coefficients; the failed transfer and reconstruction tests constrain that interpretation.

### 4.3. Range-associated variation and enrichment in newly occupied climates

The two continuous comparisons provide the most specific new distributional evidence. In approximately climate-matched native and introduced cells, *C. vulgare* showed relatively more extreme chroma phenotypes, while within its introduced range, cells outside the native climatic core showed relatively lower representation of low-chroma phenotypes and greater representation of intermediate-to-high chroma phenotypes. A wider photographed distribution across ranges can thus coexist with a more concentrated relative density in one climatic context. The observed distributions and conditional curves address complementary questions: the former describe marginal phenotype values and the latter describe relative representation conditional on the other measured traits. Convex and concave density ratios must not be read as evidence of disruptive or stabilizing selection. They refer to different contemporary spatial comparisons, not a documented sequence of broadening followed by selection. The latter curvature was sensitive to the novelty definition, and both findings require validation against photographic and source-population differences.

### 4.4. Two complementary hypotheses rather than two proven invasion strategies

The native climatic and chroma breadths suggest a useful contrast. *C. arvense* occupies a broader native climatic range and a larger fraction of its native density remains outside its introduced core, whereas *C. vulgare* has wider native chroma variation and a larger fraction of introduced density outside its native core. One possibility is that broader native environmental occupancy facilitates establishment in familiar climates while broader standing phenotype variation offers a larger repertoire of phenotypes that can be differentially represented in newly occupied climates. These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. The comparison consists of only two nonrandomly chosen taxa; climate availability, introduction effort, history and demography can also generate the observed asymmetry.

The largely dioecious, perennial *C. arvense* can persist and spread vegetatively (Tiley, 2010), whereas *C. vulgare* is usually biennial or short-lived monocarpic with seed-based reproduction (Zouhar, 2002). These life-history differences motivate hypotheses about the contribution of reproductive traits to establishment and persistence, but no demographic mechanism is estimated. Sex composition, clonal repetition, founding populations and plasticity may affect photographic distributions. No lack of adaptation in *C. arvense* is inferred from a weak pooled trait contrast.

### 4.5. Spatial heterogeneity and the limits of mean-based interpretation

The near-total cancellation of signed regional chroma contrasts in *C. arvense* is a reminder that a small pooled mean difference can mask geographically variable distributions. Its lower among-cell chroma variance in novel contexts, contrasted with the lack of a clear decrease within cells, motivates the hypothesis that the geographical arrangement of variation is important. Positive regional contributions were more dominant by pair weight in *C. vulgare*, but this descriptive contrast is not a direct test of different mechanisms. A geographical block or 1° cell is not a biological population or an independent introduction. The variance decomposition is not an algebraic explanation of the conditional enrichment curves, nor a measure of additive genetic variance. Neither new three-trait disparity nor its absence was established.

### 4.6. Limitations and experimental implications

Photographic lighting, projection, measurement and observer behaviour may contribute to trait variability. Current native ranges are not historical source populations. Climatic matching leaves residual imbalance and does not control all environmental or biotic factors, ancestry or introduction history. Novel climate is defined relative to current occupied density and differs between species, not a common experimental treatment. The observed chroma curvature depends on climatic definition and the refinement is exploratory. We therefore do not infer adaptive rates, preferential selection on standing genetic variation or distinct modes of natural selection.

Replicated common gardens using ancestry-informed native and introduced families, standardized spectral measurements, experimental rainfall exposure and reproductive fitness measurements would discriminate plasticity from heritable divergence and test the functional hypotheses. Expanding the comparison to further independent invasions or species with comparable data coverage would permit a direct test of whether native climatic breadth and standing phenotypic breadth predict phenotypic redistribution.

Overall, our data connect climate-conditioned resemblance across native thistles with broad native-observed trait-space overlap and nonlinear chroma enrichment within introduced species. The combination motivates the hypothesis that floral diversity can reflect long-term environmental responses and provide existing variation relevant to comparatively rapid responses to new environments. Its value is a set of distinguishable, testable hypotheses rather than proof of the evolutionary mechanism.
