## Introduction: why introduced ranges?
Introduced ranges offer a comparative setting for examining responses to newly occupied environments over relatively short invasion histories. Rapid adaptation may draw on variation already present within a species. Here we ask whether novel climatic occupancy is associated with redistribution of previously observed capitulum phenotypes, rather than a general increase in novel phenotypic combinations. We distinguish the association of climate with capitulum traits among native taxa (Part 1) from this within-species question (Part 2). The two parts address different biological scales rather than measuring evolutionary rates.

## Methods: why these two species?
For Part 2, we focus on *Cirsium arvense* and *Cirsium vulgare*. Their shared capitulum architecture permits the same measurement workflow, while their extensive records in both native and introduced ranges provide the geographic replication needed for within-species comparisons. The two focal species were initially selected for observational coverage. A retrospective audit of 350 source species-rank names (not the total species richness of the genus) using WCVP regional statuses identified seven with wild records in both native and explicitly introduced regions; only these two satisfied the stronger coverage criteria independently in each range: at least 100 angle-assessable observations, 15 occupied broad 5° equal-area cells and 10 observers. The weaker coverage criterion retained the same two species. No measured angle or rainfall values were used in the species-eligibility audit. The two species contributed 156,608 of 156,750 explicitly introduced wild observations in the source archive (99.91%). This is a feasibility-based case selection, not prospective random sampling of the genus or selection based on the significance of the current enrichment results.

## Methods: within-species research design
Use contemporary native phenotypes as a reference, not reconstructed ancestral variation. Within each species, classify introduced climatic occupancy relative to its background-corrected native 95% core in the frozen five-climate PCA-env space. Match introduced analogous and non-analogue 1° cells within 20° geographic blocks. Define native quintiles independently for all three traits and retain the 125 previously observed internal three-trait combinations. Fit the fixed matched-pair density-ratio model, with simultaneous linear and quadratic terms for all three traits, to compare relative enrichment of observed phenotypes in non-analogue versus analogous climates. Assess native-unobserved combinations separately using equal-size native references and geographically held-out native samples. The direction and curvature are candidate patterns consistent with selection hypotheses, not fitness functions, measured selection gradients, heritable variation or observed rates of evolution. No lightness, hue or time axis is added.

## Results and discussion
For *C. vulgare*, the highest relative to lowest native chroma quintile had estimated relative enrichment 2.94 (pointwise 95% CI 1.40–6.17; nominal P=.0183; Holm12 P=.1831). Negative chroma curvature and positive orientation curvature were additional exploratory candidates (nominal P=.0094 and .0101). The primary omnibus P=.323 and all adjusted coefficient tests were non-significant. The chroma direction persisted in the common-background comparison (2.95, nominal P=.0469) but weakened under density-surplus classification (1.41, P=.319). The between-species contrast in the primary directional chroma coefficient was unresolved (P=.323). No increase in native-unobserved combinations was supported; previously observed combinations accounted for most positive frequency changes, but the proportion did not exceed geographic null expectations significantly.

The climate niches of the two species are not literal geographical range swaps. The observed climate occupancy of *C. arvense* has a broader native spread, whereas *C. vulgare* occupies more of the climate space outside its own native 95% core (18.8% and 44.0% of introduced corrected occupancy density, respectively). The native chroma q20–q80 widths are 12.63 and 24.28 C*, respectively. These differences motivate hypotheses about the available reservoir of standing phenotypes, ecological fitting and the potential for phenotypic sorting. They did not determine the original species selection. Reproductive mode may also affect the role of floral traits in establishment. The contrasting life histories, founding composition, plasticity, observation and imaging conditions, genetic origin and fitness were not separated. The two cases cannot establish a general causal law or demonstrate rapid genetic adaptation.
