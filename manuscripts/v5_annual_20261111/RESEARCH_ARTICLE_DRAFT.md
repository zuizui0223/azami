# Climatic matching reveals local capitulum resemblance and trait-specific gradients in Cirsium

**V5 working Research Article draft — 11 November 2026.**  
**Running title:** Climate and thistle capitula  
**Target journal:** Global Ecology and Biogeography (candidate).  
**Status:** Exploratory manuscript text, not submitted or preregistered. This file is separate from the frozen V4 manuscript. The adopted environmental description is annual BIO01, BIO04, BIO12, BIO15 and shortwave radiation (RSDS). Continuous niche-rarity scores and BIO05/BIO10 substitutions are not part of the adopted V5 argument.

## Abstract

**Aim:** Floral traits have different potential functions in reproduction, yet whether climate-related differences in individual traits contribute to recurring multivariate floral phenotypes is poorly understood. We investigated local capitulum resemblance, the direction of climatic trait associations and the extent to which these relationships inform native–introduced comparisons.

**Location:** Native *Cirsium* taxa worldwide; native and introduced ranges of *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare*.

**Time period:** Multi-year photographs and 1981–2010 climatic normals.

**Major taxa studied:** *Cirsium* (Asteraceae).

**Methods:** We analysed image-referenced capitulum angle, corolla chroma (CIELAB C*) and projected outline in geographically linked photographs. We evaluated climatic analogue resemblance using 157,822 eligible native photographs aggregated at 1° and 5°, with within-taxon phenotype permutations. We related angle and chroma to annual precipitation and precipitation seasonality, and compared background-corrected climatic occupancy and head-trait distributions in two native–introduced species.

**Results:** Climatically matched sites exhibited greater multivariate capitulum resemblance within taxa at both grains (Holm P=0.004 each), and among taxa at 5° (P=0.012; 1° P=0.076). Higher annual precipitation corresponded to more downward-looking image angles (standardised β=+0.117 to +0.183), while greater precipitation seasonality corresponded to higher chroma (β=+0.206 to +0.226); these associations are consistent with, but do not test, rain-exposure and colour–water-environment hypotheses. Native–introduced climatic overlap was 0.474 in *C. arvense* and 0.442 in *C. vulgare*. Introduced *C. vulgare* showed a positive mean chroma difference (+1.74 C*; +2.46 after climatic frequency balancing), without a familywise-supported loss of whole-head overlap.

**Main conclusions:** Climatic resemblance is associated with recurrent local floral trait combinations and functionally interpretable trait gradients. The correspondence is not uniform across taxa, spatial grains or introduced ranges, and does not establish adaptive evolution.

**Keywords:** biogeography; capitulum; climatic niche; floral chroma; functional hypotheses; introduced ranges; photographic phenomics; spatial grain

## 1. Introduction

Flowers present reproductive organs to their environment through a combination of orientation, colour and morphology. Because these characters can influence the exposure of pollen to rain, the appearance of flowers to visitors and their interaction with radiation, their geographical distributions may reflect multiple functional demands. Experiments on other flowering plants have shown that floral orientation can affect rain damage and reproductive success (Chen et al., 2013; Nakata et al., 2022). Floral colour is likewise associated with climatic and biotic gradients at broad scales, although the functions of any particular pigmentation or optical feature cannot be inferred from colour alone (Dalrymple et al., 2020; Koski & Ashman, 2015). These observations lead to two linked expectations: individual floral traits may vary with environmental variables relevant to their proposed functions, and several visible traits may recur in similar combinations where climatic conditions are alike.

The two expectations have rarely been assessed at the same geographical resolution with continuous measurements of reproductive organs. Macroecological analyses commonly use one phenotype or a species mean, which obscures the actual locations where particular combinations of traits have been observed. Yet substantial variation occurs within species, and its representation depends on climate, geography and sampling level (Siefert et al., 2015; Kuppler et al., 2020). For a functional interpretation of floral morphology, it is therefore useful to distinguish (i) directional associations between climatic variables and individual traits from (ii) multivariate resemblance between flowers observed at climatically similar sites. The latter can be assessed against a null in which taxon-specific multivariate phenotype distributions are retained but their assignments to individual locations are permuted.

An additional challenge is whether geographical associations remain informative when a plant species occupies environments outside its typical native range. Introduced plants can occupy different realised climatic spaces, yet their reproductive phenotypes might retain combinations already present in the native distribution. Other traits could vary in their mean frequency without wholesale reorganisation of the joint phenotype space. Founder sources, phenotypic plasticity, regionally varying selective environments and photographic sampling can all contribute to such patterns (Hulme & Barrett, 2013; Colautti, Maron & Barrett, 2009; van Kleunen et al., 2018). Thus, local climatic matching and native–introduced phenotype contrasts provide complementary rather than interchangeable tests of how floral appearance is distributed across environments.

Public photographs offer a geographically repeated record of floral appearance that can complement species-level trait resources (Kattge et al., 2020; Schiller et al., 2021). They allow continuous measurement of orientation in the image frame, corolla chroma and projected outline, but these measurements must not be mistaken for a gravity-calibrated orientation, measured pigment concentration or intrinsic three-dimensional head shape. Colour can depend on illumination and image processing, and projected outline geometry on camera angle and rotation (Laitly et al., 2021). Large photographic samples can therefore reveal environmentally associated variation while leaving the causal functions of the traits open to experimental testing.

Thistles of the genus *Cirsium* share broadly comparable capitulum architecture across geographically and climatically heterogeneous distributions (Siniscalchi, Ackerfield & Folk, 2023; Moreyra et al., 2025). We combined two levels of observation within this genus using a consistent annual climatic framework of mean temperature, precipitation, their seasonalities and solar radiation. **First**, among native taxa, we asked whether climatically similar sites show greater joint capitulum resemblance than expected from each taxon's observed phenotypic distribution, and whether higher annual precipitation and greater precipitation seasonality are associated with photographic angle and chroma in directions compatible with proposed rain-protection and seasonal water-environment hypotheses. **Second**, we assessed whether climatic occupancy differences between the native and introduced ranges of *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare* are accompanied by divergence of entire photographed capitulum distributions or more selective differences in individual traits. These are observational tests of environmental correspondence; the functional predictions motivate them but are not assumed to have been experimentally verified in *Cirsium*.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Data sources, range status and photographic traits

We used the originally audited photographic observation tables and their associated image-based measurements. The native-taxon (S1) source contained **313,350 WCVP-classified native observations**, of which **157,822** met the pre-existing joint angle–chroma–outline and environment eligibility criteria for the annual local-analogue comparison. The focal species (S2) source contained **297,622 original wild observations** classified as native or explicitly introduced for *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare* using World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP) regional assignments (Govaerts et al., 2021). Two-species selection was based on native/introduced photo, observer and geographical coverage rather than phenotype-significance screening. Observations with ambiguous regional status were not silently reclassified as introduced. These numbers are source-level denominators, not the number of observations used in each individual regression or regional permutation.

Photo-derived capitulum traits were (i) an **image-referenced angle**, with larger values denoting a more downward-looking position in the photographic frame; (ii) flower-corolla colour chroma **C*** computed in CIELAB space; and (iii) an image-projected outline aspect index. For photographs with multiple heads, the original image-level measurement aggregation was retained. In analyses requiring complete multivariate observations, all three characters had to be measured on the same photograph; there was no trait imputation. The visual measures are *not* gravity-calibrated head inclination, biochemical pigment content or anatomy-independent outline dimensions. In particular, projected outline aspect depends on image rotation. Repeated observer–geographic-cell records were thinned according to the previously audited workflow, and tests did not treat individual photographs from the same geographic unit as independent experimental replicates.

### 2.2. Annual climate and environmental availability

The adopted environment comprises **BIO01** (annual mean temperature), **BIO04** (temperature seasonality), **BIO12** (annual precipitation), **BIO15** (precipitation seasonality) and **RSDS** (mean downwelling shortwave radiation). Climatic values were from the originally archived and authenticated CHELSA v2.1 normals for **1981–2010**. This annual definition is used throughout the adopted V5 argument; it was selected as an editorial consolidation after exploration, not prospectively preregistered. We did not substitute warmest-month, warmest-quarter, climate-rarity or elevation variables in these reported V5 tests. Climate at a photographed occurrence is a long-term geographical covariate and should not be interpreted as precipitation or temperature on the date the capitulum was observed.

For Part II, species-specific regions assigned native and introduced by WCVP defined broad geographic climatic background opportunities. Available climates were sampled on an area-weighted land grid and occupied climates were projected into a common two-axis PCA environment for each species. An availability-corrected occupation density was calculated for each range; **Schoener's D** summarised their overlap in the same projected climate space. We also report the fraction of the introduced range's *background-adjusted occupied climatic density* outside the principal **95% native occupied climatic region**. The background is a regional availability proxy, not an individual dispersal or land-use accessibility model; the resulting fraction is not a percentage of photographs, introduced genotypes or environments physiologically unavailable to the species.

### 2.3. Native climatic analogues and the 1° versus 5° comparison

The S1 multivariate measurements were aggregated as **taxon × latitude–longitude cell** means or medians according to the previously locked trait-specific procedure. Cells of **1°** and **5°** are angular geographic units: the north–south spacing is approximately **111 km** and **555 km**, respectively, whereas east–west distances decrease with latitude. They are not 1-km and 5-km cells, nor equal-area grids. Taxa had to be supported by sufficient eligible observations and independently occupied cells. This yielded **63 taxa and 7,117 taxon–cell aggregates at 1°**, and **31 taxa and 814 aggregates at 5°**, from the same initial 157,822-photograph eligibility pool. **The changes in results between grains therefore also reflect differences in included taxa.** The present study does not claim a pure grain effect in the absence of a same-taxa sensitivity analysis.

At each grain, climates and complete three-trait photographic vectors were separately standardised, assigning equal total weight to eligible taxa. For the 5-climate-axis analysis, climate analogues were defined using the source-locked scaled Euclidean-distance threshold (equivalent to a fixed root-mean-square standardised distance of 0.9 per axis; full-vector threshold approximately **1.162**). Eight eligible occupied cells per taxon were drawn without replacement in **24 geographical rarefactions**. For each qualifying between-taxon pair, the 64 combinations of those cells were contrasted to identify climatically analogous pairings. Within-taxon climate matching was evaluated separately. The trait response was the mean multivariate Euclidean-distance reduction in the climate-matched combinations relative to all eligible combinations, averaged across pairs and rarefactions.

The null model permuted **whole angle–chroma–outline vectors among selected cells within each taxon**, preserving its measured multivariate distribution and trait associations but breaking its assignment to the sampled climates. The analysis used the original permutation stream and source-specific **199 reassignments per rarefaction**. Permutations and rarefactions did not create independent biological observations. Where two angular grains or multiple response statistics were examined, we retained the recorded Holm-adjusted P values associated with their **original test family**; no smaller family was retrospectively substituted after observing positive results. The geographic sector assignment of taxa is a sampling descriptor, not independent evolutionary descent. For signed annual precipitation–angle and precipitation-seasonality–chroma associations, we report standardised coefficients from the original paired-site comparison and **pointwise bootstrap intervals**, not proof of causal climatic or phylogenetic effects.

A distinct **three-climate-axis** analysis of annual precipitation, annual mean temperature and RSDS decomposed the original climate-analogue distance measure into single-trait angle, chroma and outline responses. It retained the original spatially sampled taxon comparisons but used **499 taxon-preserving permutations** and controlled the **six tests (two grains × three traits)** with a separate Holm family. It is reported as a supplementary trait-specific interpretation of local resemblance, **not as a five-axis seasonal result**. The positive chroma component in both grains therefore cannot be presented as an independent replication of the five-climate-axis joint estimate.

### 2.4. Native–introduced climate and capitulum-space overlap

For each focal species, we compared the availability-corrected native and introduced annual climate occupancies in the same PCA projection. The main 95% occupied native climatic region was used as an operational boundary: introduced observations were classed as *native-core analogous* or *outside-core*, not as intrinsically novel climates or novel evolutionary adaptations. Head-space comparisons projected the measured image angle, chroma and outline into their original three-dimensional trait-density representation. The same native observed distribution supplied the reference for each introduced group. Schoener D was computed separately for native versus analogous and native versus outside-core introduced head distributions.

To limit differences in the number and placement of photographs, the principal comparison took equal numbers of eligible **1° geographic cells within each 20° geographic block**, and exchanged group labels at the regional-block level. The locked comparison contained **30 cell pairs in nine blocks for *C. arvense*** and **146 pairs in 14 blocks for *C. vulgare***. Both species were tested in the original two-species Holm family. The observed regional pairs are not independent origins of introduction. The camera-rotation-dependent outline was excluded in a two-trait angle-plus-chroma sensitivity analysis; using an image-derived three-trait density in the main existing comparison does not establish divergence of intrinsic three-dimensional anatomical form.

### 2.5. Chroma contrasts and precipitation controls in *C. vulgare*

Whole-head overlap can remain substantial even if an individual component's mean moves in one direction. We therefore reported the observed introduced-minus-native difference in **mean photograph-derived C*** across eligible 1° cells of *C. vulgare*. To describe the role of environmental composition, we also balanced observed native and introduced cells by their frequencies in **shared climatic PCA classes**, retaining only the bins in which both ranges had records. The original primary classification used a **20 × 20 coarse climate-bin** representation of an **80 × 80 PCA density grid** (grouping each 4 × 4 fine-cell section), with a coarser-bin alternative. The reweighted C* contrast is a *descriptive comparison over the shared climatic support*, not an estimate of a causal effect of climate or introduction.

We additionally compared chroma distribution dissimilarity among seven introduced regions against a distribution of leave-one-region-out comparisons among native regions. The fraction above the native 90th percentile is an extreme-region *descriptor*, not a formal test of independently evolved traits. To examine a possible precipitation interpretation, we distinguished (i) broad annual-climate native-core versus outside-core contrasts in median BIO12, BIO15 and C*; (ii) a previously explored categorical comparison of low-precipitation/high-seasonality versus wet/low-seasonality locations; and (iii) adjusted regressions of chroma on BIO12 and BIO15 with temperature, radiation and geographical terms. These contrasts are not interchangeable. The categorical low-rainfall/high-seasonality thresholds were formed **after examining the observational results**, and are therefore reported only as exploratory context.

### 2.6. Analytical scope and provenance

All analyses reported here are from the source-authenticated V5 annual-climate records in the repository. This draft recombines existing aggregate results; it does not involve a new original-image fit. All comparison-level sample sizes, multiple-testing families, 95% intervals and regional controls are attached to their respective results, rather than pooled into a single fictitious observation count or omnibus test. The adopted annual-climate story was consolidated after exploratory model development. The main inferential targets are *geographic associations and descriptive differences*, not experiments on flower function, common-garden adaptation or genetic reconstruction of historical introduction sources.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Climatically similar sites share more similar combinations of capitulum traits

The native climate-analogue analysis used **157,822 trait-complete photographs** as its source, yielding **63 taxa and 7,117 taxon–cells at 1°** and **31 taxa and 814 taxon–cells at 5°**. Climatic matching was accompanied by greater resemblance in the joint photographic angle–chroma–outline phenotype than expected when each taxon's multivariate trait values were reassigned among its sampled sites. This excess resemblance was detected **within taxa at both grains** (1°: +0.084, Holm P=0.004; 5°: +0.238, P=0.004). It was also positive in **between-taxon comparisons**, with support at 5° (+0.065, Holm P=0.012) but less precise evidence at 1° (+0.031, P=0.076). The eligible taxon set changes between grains, so these differences cannot be attributed only to spatial aggregation.

An earlier trait-specific decomposition of the **three-climate-axis** analogue comparison (annual precipitation, annual mean temperature and RSDS, without seasonality) identified **chroma as a recurrent component of local resemblance**. The excess reduction in chroma distance between different taxa was +0.0300 at 1° (six-test Holm P=0.024) and +0.0291 at 5° (P=0.030). A corresponding orientation resemblance was detected only at 1° (+0.0246, P=0.032; 5° +0.0034, P=1.000). These individual-trait findings are complementary **three-axis exploratory analyses**, and are not the same inferential test as the five-axis multivariate analysis above. Together they show that the photographed trait composition matters when interpreting total multivariate resemblance, but do not establish parallel independent evolutionary transitions.

### 3.2. Annual precipitation and its seasonality correspond to different floral characters

We next examined whether individual photographic traits varied in directions compatible with putative abiotic reproductive functions. In annual five-climate-axis native-taxon comparisons, sites with greater **annual precipitation** showed larger downward-looking **image-referenced angles**. Removing between-taxon-pair mean differences, standardised precipitation–angle coefficients remained positive at 1° (**β=+0.117**, pointwise 95% bootstrap interval **[0.015, 0.220]**) and 5° (**β=+0.183**, **[0.022, 0.339]**). The consistent positive direction is compatible with an expectation derived from rain-protection functions, but these images do not directly measure gravitational inclination, wetting of pollen or the effects on seed production.

Corolla **chroma C*** showed a different climatic association. Greater **precipitation seasonality (BIO15)** corresponded to more saturated photographed corollas after accounting for annual precipitation, temperature, temperature seasonality and radiation. Standardised location-pair coefficients were **β=+0.206 [0.067, 0.348]** at 1° and **β=+0.226 [0.046, 0.375]** at 5°. This direction suggests a possible link between colour and seasonal water environments, but the index does not indicate whether water stress occurs during the flowering period, and photographed C* is not a direct measure of pigments or stress tolerance. Both sets of trait coefficients are exploratory estimates with **pointwise**, rather than familywise, bootstrap intervals.

Thus, the locally recurring **multivariate head phenotype** was accompanied by **trait-specific environmental gradients** with plausible but distinct functional interpretations. Such agreement is at the level of geographically observed directionality; the relevant protective and physiological functions remain to be tested.

### 3.3. Native and introduced ranges occupy different annual climates while sharing photographed head space

The background-adjusted overlap of native and introduced annual climatic occupancy was **D=0.474 in *C. arvense*** and **D=0.442 in *C. vulgare***. The introduced occupied climatic density outside the principal 95% native climatic core accounted for **18.8%** and **44.0%**, respectively. These results establish differences in *sampled climatic occupancy* in the specified two-axis climatic projection, not independently evolved climatic tolerance.

In each species, introduced-range capitula from outside the native climatic core still overlapped the native photographed head-trait distribution. Within geographically matched introduced-region comparisons, native-reference head-space overlap in native-core-analogous versus outside-core climates was **D=0.740 versus 0.735 for *C. arvense*** (difference +0.005, two-species Holm P=0.953) and **D=0.847 versus 0.789 for *C. vulgare*** (difference +0.057, Holm P=0.096). Hence the direction of the latter difference suggested a greater shift in *C. vulgare*, but there was no familywise-supported change in the three-trait head-space overlap after regional matching. This failure to reject a difference does not establish identical phenotype distributions.

### 3.4. Introduced *C. vulgare* displays a directional chroma contrast within broadly overlapping floral space

Although joint photographed phenotype space remained broadly shared, **floral chroma differed in direction** between the native and introduced distribution of *C. vulgare*. The observed introduced-minus-native mean difference was **+1.741 C***. When native and introduced cells were reweighted to the same distribution of **shared annual climatic PCA classes**, the descriptive difference remained positive and was **+2.465 C*** (coarser climate bins: **+2.349 C***). The reweighted comparison shows that the signed chroma contrast was not removed by standardising the sampled climatic frequency distribution. It is not a formal causal effect of introduction or an adaptation test.

Regional contrasts reinforced the trait-specific nature of this difference. **Five of seven introduced regions** exceeded the native 90th-percentile benchmark for **chroma distribution dissimilarity (1−D)**, but **none exceeded the largest native regional departure**. In a separate geographically matched comparison of introduced climatic groups, chroma-only native-reference D was lower in outside-core than in analogous climates (approximately **0.965 versus 0.860**; difference **+0.105**), but a six-test Holm P=**0.462** did not support a general outside-core-specific separation after multiplicity correction.

The outside-core introduced observations also tended to be in drier and more precipitation-seasonal climates than the analogous introduced observations (median annual rainfall **697.0 vs 897.5 mm**; BIO15 **60.50 vs 30.05**), while median C* was **32.23 vs 31.40**. However, this combination was not an isolated drought–colour mechanism: direct dry/high-seasonality versus wet/low-seasonality contrasts produced **lower**, rather than higher, C* in the dry/high-seasonality subset (native **−1.91**, introduced **−3.12 C***). Within the outside-core introduced cells, region-adjusted annual precipitation and precipitation-seasonality partial slopes for C* were also unsupported (BIO12 β=−0.045, raw P=0.166; BIO15 β=−0.057, P=0.432; both Holm P=1). A positive annual-temperature–chroma slope in the pooled outside-core sample (β=+0.157, Holm P=0.025) likewise lost support under geographical-region adjustment. Thus the positive **native–introduced chroma contrast** is distinguishable from unverified climatic mechanisms for that contrast.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Climatic resemblance and trait gradients provide complementary evidence of floral organisation

The central result is that climatically similar sites tend to contain more similar photographic capitulum phenotypes than would be expected from random geographical allocation of each taxon's observed trait combinations. The correspondence was supported within taxa at both spatial grains and among different taxa in the eligible 5° comparison. These are complementary tests: the joint resemblance captures how the **combination** of head characters is distributed, whereas directional regressions show **which individual characters vary with particular climatic axes**. The original three-axis, trait-specific analogue decomposition further identified chroma as a recurrent component of resemblance at both grains. That independent analysis of a narrower climatic definition should not be treated as confirmation of the exact five-axis multivariate estimand, but strengthens the descriptive evidence that local chroma variation is geographically organised.

This pairing of findings gives the results a broader ecological interpretation than a single climate–trait correlation. Plant reproductive phenotypes comprise multiple characters whose potential functions differ, and trait combinations occur within an environmentally and historically structured geographic context. Retaining local measurements rather than replacing each taxon by one mean makes these spatial associations visible (Siefert et al., 2015; Kuppler et al., 2020; Govaert et al., 2024). The result is compatible with climate-associated organisation of floral appearance; it is not proof that independent lineages converged by natural selection. The eligible between-taxon set was also smaller at 5° (31 taxa) than at 1° (63 taxa), so the difference in their inferential support must not be ascribed to map resolution alone.

### 4.2. Precipitation–orientation and seasonality–chroma patterns agree with distinct functional hypotheses

Two climatic associations supply specific, testable functional interpretations of the multivariate result. **First, annual precipitation was positively associated with a more downward-looking image angle.** In a capitulum, downward presentation could potentially reduce rain contact with exposed pollen or reproductive surfaces. Floral-orientation experiments on other Asteraceae and angiosperms demonstrate that orientation can influence reproductive output and rain-mediated pollen transfer (Chen et al., 2013; Nakata et al., 2022). The observed geographical sign is therefore **consistent with the rain-protection prediction**. Nevertheless, the sampled trait is a photo-frame angle, not inclination against gravity, and annual rainfall is an imperfect proxy for flower-stage rain exposure. The association should motivate experiments with calibrated head orientation, measured pollen wetting and fertility, rather than be represented as evidence that nodding capitula are already demonstrated rain adaptations in *Cirsium*.

**Second, greater precipitation seasonality was associated with higher photographed floral chroma.** Seasonal contrasts in water availability could be related to colour expression or to the conditions under which different floral pigments are advantageous, while broad comparisons of floral colour indicate roles for both abiotic and biotic environments (Dalrymple et al., 2020; Koski & Ashman, 2015). The positive direction is **compatible with a seasonal water-environment hypothesis**, not a direct measurement of dry-season selection. BIO15 records the degree of annual precipitation variability, not whether a specific dry period coincides with anthesis. CIELAB chroma measures image colour intensity rather than pigment quantity, photoprotection or stress resistance. Thus, the next mechanistic test would need the same flowers and populations followed across known development stages with water, radiation, pigment/reflectance and reproductive measurements.

The distinction between **joint phenotype resemblance** and **functionally interpretable individual associations** matters. Observed local similarity can arise from several biological processes, including selective conditions, phylogenetic composition and plasticity. Directional trait–climate observations make predictions more precise, but do not establish the causal process that produced a photographed phenotype. Accordingly, the study identifies ecological associations in directions relevant to functional hypotheses, not the functions themselves.

### 4.3. Climatic occupancy expands while trait combinations and individual colour contrasts follow different patterns

The two native–introduced comparisons extend this framework without requiring the same direction in every trait. *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare* occupied different background-adjusted annual climatic distributions across ranges, yet their outside-core introduced capitula retained considerable overlap with the observed native head-trait space. This is compatible with substantial persistence or reuse of extant phenotypic combinations across distinct climatic circumstances. It is also compatible with sampling and source-population differences, and does not by itself demonstrate phenotypic invariance: the geographically matched whole-head shift was not familywise significant and the three-trait measurement includes a rotation-dependent outline index.

Within that broadly overlapping head space, *C. vulgare* showed a **positive mean chroma difference** between introduced and native ranges, and the difference remained when their sampled climatic frequencies were balanced. Rather than an exception that undermines the wider result, this is an example of **uneven variation among floral components**. The frequency or mean of one visible component can shift while many multivariate phenotype combinations remain shared. Five of seven introduced regions exceeded the 90th-percentile reference of native regional chroma dissimilarity, but none exceeded its maximum, so even this positive contrast is not evidence for a novel colour phenotype outside all native variation.

A possible adaptive explanation would link the colour difference to drier and more precipitation-seasonal climate. The data do not establish that link. Outside-core introduced sites were indeed less wet, more seasonal and more chromatic *on average*, but direct dry/high-seasonality comparisons and adjusted precipitation/seasonality regressions did not reproduce an independent positive climatic effect on C*. Climatic frequency matching was not experimental control, and a region-wide colour difference need not be caused by the climatic variable with which it co-occurs. Source populations, development stage, camera processing, plasticity and historical selection remain alternatives (Hulme & Barrett, 2013; Colautti, Maron & Barrett, 2009; Colautti & Lau, 2015). The positive colour contrast is therefore a result worth explaining, not proof of introduced-range adaptive pigmentation.

### 4.4. Ecological scope and decisive validation

This synthesis operates across two levels of evidence. Part I compares locally photographed capitula among and within taxa; Part II describes whole-range climatic occupancy and phenotype frequencies within two widespread species. The same annual climate variables permit a common conceptual description, but the two parts have different reference distributions, spatial replication and statistical null models. A local climate–phenotype correlation should neither be extrapolated automatically to adaptation of introduced populations nor be dismissed because whole-range phenotype overlap remains high.

Three validation requirements would most directly strengthen the functional interpretation. The **spatial-grain comparison** should use a fixed 31-taxon set at 1° and 5°, because eligibility and aggregation currently vary together. The **photographic phenotype space** should be checked against rotation-invariant head geometry, gravity-calibrated orientation and illumination-standardised colour (Laitly et al., 2021). Finally, **functional experiments or common gardens** with known native sources should directly measure rain protection, pigmentation, pollination and fitness under contrasting moisture environments. None of these uncompleted tests is represented as successful evidence in this manuscript. The existing geographical results nevertheless provide specific phenotype-by-climate hypotheses that these tests can assess.

## 5. Conclusion

Across globally sampled *Cirsium*, sites with similar annual climatic conditions showed recurrent similarities in photographed capitulum trait combinations, especially within taxa. **Greater precipitation corresponded to more downward-looking capitula, while stronger precipitation seasonality corresponded to greater corolla chroma**, directions compatible with distinct functional hypotheses. Native–introduced comparisons revealed differences in climatic occupancy and a positive chroma contrast in introduced *C. vulgare*, alongside substantial shared head-trait space. The study therefore identifies **climate-associated organisation of reproductive appearance with trait-specific expression across geographical contexts**. Functional adaptation, its origins and its consequences for reproduction remain hypotheses requiring direct tests.
