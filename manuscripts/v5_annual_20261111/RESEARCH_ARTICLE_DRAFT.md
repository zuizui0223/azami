# Climatic resemblance and uneven floral variation across native and introduced thistles

**V5 working Research Article draft — 11 November 2026.**  
**Running title:** Climate and thistle capitula  
**Target journal:** Global Ecology and Biogeography (candidate).  
**Status:** Exploratory manuscript text, not submitted or preregistered. This file is separate from the frozen V4 manuscript. The adopted environmental description is annual BIO01, BIO04, BIO12, BIO15 and shortwave radiation (RSDS). Continuous niche-rarity scores and BIO05/BIO10 substitutions are not part of the adopted V5 argument.

## Abstract

**Aim:** Climatic resemblance may organise floral appearance among locations without requiring equally strong differentiation of whole-head phenotypes when a species occupies new climates. We examined geographical correspondence in capitulum presentation, colour and climate across two nested levels of comparison.

**Location:** Native Cirsium taxa worldwide; native and introduced ranges of C. arvense and C. vulgare.

**Time period:** Multi-year photographs linked to 1981–2010 climatic normals.

**Major taxa studied:** Cirsium (Asteraceae).

**Methods:** We analysed geographically repeated photographic measurements of capitulum image angle, corolla chroma (CIELAB C*) and projected outline. A source pool of 157,822 quality-controlled native photographs supported comparisons of climate-matched taxon-by-location cells at 1° and 5°, evaluated against taxon-preserving permutations. For two introduced species, we estimated background-adjusted native–introduced overlap in five-dimensional annual climate as represented in a common PCA space, then compared capitulum overlap in geographically matched analogous versus outside-core climates. We assessed directional chroma differences as a complementary, individual-trait contrast.

**Results:** Climatically matched sites showed greater joint capitulum resemblance within native taxa at both resolutions; evidence among taxa was stronger at 5° than 1°. Annual precipitation covaried positively with photographed downward angle, and precipitation seasonality with chroma, in exploratory paired-location analyses. Native–introduced climatic overlap was D=0.474 for C. arvense and D=0.442 for C. vulgare. Introduced C. vulgare had higher mean photographic chroma than the native reference (+1.74 C*; +2.46 C* after matching climatic composition), although geographically matched tests did not establish corresponding multivariate capitulum divergence in outside-core climates.

**Main conclusions:** Floral resemblance and climatic resemblance correspond at local scales, while range-wide climatic occupancy differences do not necessarily show an equivalent shift in all photographed head traits. Individual colour dimensions may differ even where joint phenotypic spaces overlap. These observational patterns do not identify adaptive evolution.

**Keywords:** biogeography; capitulum; climatic niche; floral chroma; introduced ranges; morphospace; photographic phenomics; spatial grain

## 1. Introduction

Floral presentation and pigmentation connect reproductive morphology with the environment in which a flower functions. The orientation of a flower or capitulum can affect precipitation exposure and animal access; experiments on other flowering plants have documented effects of orientation on pollen transfer, rain damage and reproductive output (Chen et al., 2013; Nakata et al., 2022). Flower colour also varies over climatic gradients, although its optical and ecological consequences depend on pigments, radiation, water and pollinator communities (Dalrymple et al., 2020; Koski & Ashman, 2015). Such findings motivate a broad expectation of climatic organisation in floral phenotypes, but it remains difficult to determine whether a geographic association reflects typical differences among taxa, variable populations within a taxon, or repeated local combinations of floral characters.

This distinction is central to macroecology. Species averages can reveal environmental gradients while hiding variation within species, and the distribution of intraspecific trait variation is itself uneven across geographical and environmental settings (Siefert et al., 2015; Kuppler et al., 2020). More importantly, a species mean or a pooled multidimensional trait distribution does not retain *where* its particular trait combinations have been recorded. If floral resemblance is environmentally associated, photographs from climatically comparable locations should be more similar than would be expected merely because their respective taxa have overlapping trait distributions. This is a different claim from adaptive evolutionary convergence: it can be tested observationally by matching site climates and comparing local phenotype distances against a null that preserves the multivariate phenotype distribution of each taxon.

Range expansion offers another, conceptually distinct scale of comparison. Introduced plant populations may occupy different climates from those occupied in their native range; however, a shift in realised environmental occupancy need not correspond to a new combination of floral traits (Hulme & Barrett, 2013; van Kleunen et al., 2018). Pre-existing variation and environmental plasticity could permit establishment across a broader set of climates, while different founding sources, regional sampling or selection could change the frequencies of visible phenotypes. Native–introduced comparisons are particularly susceptible to misinterpretation because geographical trends, environmental availability and introduction history are rarely controlled at the same scale (Colautti, Maron & Barrett, 2009; Colautti & Lau, 2015). Climate overlap, whole-phenotype overlap and shifts in a single component such as floral chroma must therefore be evaluated as separate, complementary observations.

Geographically repeated photographs make these tests tractable at large scales. Continuous image-derived measurements retain intermediate colour and shape values and can complement traditional species-level plant trait compilations (Kattge et al., 2020; Schiller et al., 2021). Such measurements also require care: photographic colour can depend on lighting and camera processing, the appearance of head orientation depends on the image frame, and a projected outline can change when the same object is rotated (Laitly et al., 2021). These limitations do not remove the value of broad-scale measurements, but restrict inferences to what the photographs actually capture.

Thistles of the genus *Cirsium* span extensive and historically differentiated regions while retaining broadly comparable capitulum structure (Siniscalchi, Ackerfield & Folk, 2023; Moreyra et al., 2025). Here we connect two levels of geographical floral variation in this genus. **Part I** asks whether native-taxon capitula photographed at climatically similar sites are more similar in angle, chroma and projected outline than expected from taxon-preserving permutations, and whether annual precipitation and precipitation seasonality are associated with the individual characters. **Part II** asks whether *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare* occupy different climates in native and introduced ranges and whether this corresponds to differentiation of whole-capitulum phenotypic space or to uneven changes among its components. For consistent climate descriptions we use annual mean temperature, annual precipitation, temperature seasonality, precipitation seasonality and shortwave radiation. We test geographical correspondence, not adaptive evolution, and explicitly distinguish individual-trait mean shifts from multivariate distribution overlap.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Data sources, range status and photographic traits

We used the originally audited photographic observation tables and their associated image-based measurements. The native-taxon (S1) source contained **313,350 WCVP-classified native observations**, of which **157,822** met the pre-existing joint angle–chroma–outline and environment eligibility criteria for the annual local-analogue comparison. The focal species (S2) source contained **297,622 original wild observations** classified as native or explicitly introduced for *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare* using World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP) regional assignments (Govaerts et al., 2021). Two-species selection was based on native/introduced photo, observer and geographical coverage rather than phenotype-significance screening. Observations with ambiguous regional status were not silently reclassified as introduced. These numbers are source-level denominators, not the number of observations used in each individual regression or regional permutation.

Photo-derived capitulum traits were (i) an **image-referenced angle**, with larger values denoting a more downward-looking position in the photographic frame; (ii) flower-corolla colour chroma **C*** computed in CIELAB space; and (iii) an image-projected outline aspect index. For photographs with multiple heads, the original image-level measurement aggregation was retained. In analyses requiring complete multivariate observations, all three characters had to be measured on the same photograph; there was no trait imputation. The visual measures are *not* gravity-calibrated head inclination, biochemical pigment content or anatomy-independent outline dimensions. In particular, projected outline aspect depends on image rotation. Repeated observer–geographic-cell records were thinned according to the previously audited workflow, and tests did not treat individual photographs from the same geographic unit as independent experimental replicates.

### 2.2. Annual climate and environmental availability

The adopted environment comprises **BIO01** (annual mean temperature), **BIO04** (temperature seasonality), **BIO12** (annual precipitation), **BIO15** (precipitation seasonality) and **RSDS** (mean downwelling shortwave radiation). Climatic values were from the originally archived and authenticated CHELSA v2.1 normals for **1981–2010**. This annual definition is used throughout the adopted V5 argument; it was selected as an editorial consolidation after exploration, not prospectively preregistered. We did not substitute warmest-month, warmest-quarter, climate-rarity or elevation variables in these reported V5 tests. Climate at a photographed occurrence is a long-term geographical covariate and should not be interpreted as precipitation or temperature on the date the capitulum was observed.

For Part II, species-specific regions assigned native and introduced by WCVP defined broad geographic climatic background opportunities. Available climates were sampled on an area-weighted land grid and occupied climates were projected into a common two-axis PCA environment for each species. An availability-corrected occupation density was calculated for each range; **Schoener's D** summarised their overlap in the same projected climate space. We also report the fraction of the introduced range's *background-adjusted occupied climatic density* outside the principal **95% native occupied climatic region**. The background is a regional availability proxy, not an individual dispersal or land-use accessibility model; the resulting fraction is not a percentage of photographs, introduced genotypes or environments physiologically unavailable to the species.

### 2.3. Native climatic analogues and the 1° versus 5° comparison

The S1 multivariate measurements were aggregated as **taxon × latitude–longitude cell** means or medians according to the previously locked trait-specific procedure. Cells of **1°** and **5°** are angular geographic units: the north–south spacing is approximately **111 km** and **555 km**, respectively, whereas east–west distances decrease with latitude. They are not 1-km and 5-km cells, nor equal-area grids. Taxa had to be supported by sufficient eligible observations and independently occupied cells. This yielded **63 taxa and 7,117 taxon–cell aggregates at 1°**, and **31 taxa and 814 aggregates at 5°**, from the same initial 157,822-photograph eligibility pool. **The changes in results between grains therefore also reflect differences in included taxa.** The present study does not claim a pure grain effect in the absence of a same-taxa sensitivity analysis.

At each grain, climates and complete three-trait photographic vectors were separately standardised, assigning equal total weight to eligible taxa. For the 5-climate-axis analysis, climate analogues were defined using the source-locked scaled Euclidean-distance threshold (equivalent to a fixed root-mean-square standardised distance of 0.9 per axis; full-vector threshold approximately **1.162**). Eight eligible occupied cells per taxon were drawn without replacement in **24 geographical rarefactions**. For each qualifying between-taxon pair, the 64 combinations of those cells were contrasted to identify climatically analogous pairings. Within-taxon climate matching was evaluated separately. The trait response was the mean multivariate Euclidean-distance reduction in the climate-matched combinations relative to all eligible combinations, averaged across pairs and rarefactions.

The null model permuted **whole angle–chroma–outline vectors among selected cells within each taxon**, preserving its measured multivariate distribution and trait associations but breaking its assignment to the sampled climates. The analysis used the original permutation stream and source-specific **199 reassignments per rarefaction**. Permutations and rarefactions did not create independent biological observations. Where two angular grains or multiple response statistics were examined, we retained the recorded Holm-adjusted P values associated with their **original test family**; no smaller family was retrospectively substituted after observing positive results. The geographic sector assignment of taxa is a sampling descriptor, not independent evolutionary descent. For signed annual precipitation–angle and precipitation-seasonality–chroma associations, we report standardised coefficients from the original paired-site comparison and **pointwise bootstrap intervals**, not proof of causal climatic or phylogenetic effects.

### 2.4. Native–introduced climate and capitulum-space overlap

For each focal species, we compared the availability-corrected native and introduced annual climate occupancies in the same PCA projection. The main 95% occupied native climatic region was used as an operational boundary: introduced observations were classed as *native-core analogous* or *outside-core*, not as intrinsically novel climates or novel evolutionary adaptations. Head-space comparisons projected the measured image angle, chroma and outline into their original three-dimensional trait-density representation. The same native observed distribution supplied the reference for each introduced group. Schoener D was computed separately for native versus analogous and native versus outside-core introduced head distributions.

To limit differences in the number and placement of photographs, the principal comparison took equal numbers of eligible **1° geographic cells within each 20° geographic block**, and exchanged group labels at the regional-block level. The locked comparison contained **30 cell pairs in nine blocks for *C. arvense*** and **146 pairs in 14 blocks for *C. vulgare***. Both species were tested in the original two-species Holm family. The observed regional pairs are not independent origins of introduction. The camera-rotation-dependent outline was excluded in a two-trait angle-plus-chroma sensitivity analysis; using an image-derived three-trait density in the main existing comparison does not establish divergence of intrinsic three-dimensional anatomical form.

### 2.5. Chroma contrasts and precipitation controls in *C. vulgare*

Whole-head overlap can remain substantial even if an individual component's mean moves in one direction. We therefore reported the observed introduced-minus-native difference in **mean photograph-derived C*** across eligible 1° cells of *C. vulgare*. To describe the role of environmental composition, we also balanced observed native and introduced cells by their frequencies in **shared climatic PCA classes**, retaining only the bins in which both ranges had records. The original primary classification used a **20 × 20 coarse climate-bin** representation of an **80 × 80 PCA density grid** (grouping each 4 × 4 fine-cell section), with a coarser-bin alternative. The reweighted C* contrast is a *descriptive comparison over the shared climatic support*, not an estimate of a causal effect of climate or introduction.

We additionally compared chroma distribution dissimilarity among seven introduced regions against a distribution of leave-one-region-out comparisons among native regions. The fraction above the native 90th percentile is an extreme-region *descriptor*, not a formal test of independently evolved traits. To examine a possible precipitation interpretation, we distinguished (i) broad annual-climate native-core versus outside-core contrasts in median BIO12, BIO15 and C*; (ii) a previously explored categorical comparison of low-precipitation/high-seasonality versus wet/low-seasonality locations; and (iii) adjusted regressions of chroma on BIO12 and BIO15 with temperature, radiation and geographical terms. These contrasts are not interchangeable. The categorical low-rainfall/high-seasonality thresholds were formed **after examining the observational results**, and are therefore reported only as exploratory context.

### 2.6. Analytical scope and provenance

All analyses reported here are from the source-authenticated V5 annual-climate records in the repository. This draft recombines existing aggregate results; it does not involve a new original-image fit. All comparison-level sample sizes, multiple-testing families, 95% intervals and regional controls are attached to their respective results, rather than pooled into a single fictitious observation count or omnibus test. The adopted annual-climate story was consolidated after exploratory model development. The main inferential targets are *geographic associations and descriptive differences*, not experiments on flower function, common-garden adaptation or genetic reconstruction of historical introduction sources.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Climate matching predicts local resemblance more consistently within taxa than among taxa

The five-axis annual analysis found excess multivariate capitulum resemblance within native taxa at both 1° (+0.084, Holm P=0.004) and 5° (+0.238, Holm P=0.004). Between-taxon excess resemblance was positive but uncertain at 1° (+0.031, Holm P=0.076), and supported in the eligible 5° sample (+0.065, Holm P=0.012). The difference must be interpreted with the eligible-taxon count of 63 versus 31, rather than described as an isolated resolution effect.

Among matched comparisons of native taxon locations, annual precipitation was positively associated with a larger photographed downward angle after adjustment for other annual climatic axes (standardised beta +0.117, pointwise 95% interval [0.015, 0.220] at 1°; beta +0.183 [0.022, 0.339] at 5°). Precipitation seasonality likewise had a positive association with photographed corolla C* (+0.206 [0.067, 0.348] and +0.226 [0.046, 0.375], respectively). These are geographical associations, not direct evidence for rainfall shielding or drought-adapted pigments.

### 3.2. Native–introduced climatic occupancy shifts without an established obligatory displacement of whole-head space

The background-adjusted overlap of native and introduced climatic occupancy was D=0.474 for C. arvense and D=0.442 for C. vulgare. Introduced climatic density outside the main native 95% occupancy core represented 18.8% and 44.0%, respectively.

For matched analogous versus outside-core introduced climates, the native-reference head-overlap D changed from 0.740 to 0.735 for C. arvense (difference +0.005, two-species Holm P=0.953) and from 0.847 to 0.789 for C. vulgare (difference +0.057, Holm P=0.096). The second species showed a directionally lower native-reference overlap in outside-core climates, but neither species passed familywise error control for the matched whole-head contrast.

### 3.3. A positive chroma difference is visible in introduced C. vulgare

Despite broad shared head space, introduced C. vulgare had higher average photographed chroma than the native reference. The native–introduced difference was +1.741 C* in the original cell-weighted contrast. After matching the distributions of sampled climatic PCA classes between ranges, introduced minus native chroma was +2.465 C* (and +2.349 C* with a coarser climate-class specification). This directional contrast remained visible on a common set of occupied climates, but the weighting changes the target population and does not establish that climate caused the observed difference.

Regional comparisons pointed to the importance of chroma without implying entirely novel floral colours. Five of seven introduced regions exceeded the 90th percentile of native leave-one-region-out chroma dissimilarity, but none exceeded the maximum native regional departure. In an additional geographically matched analogous versus outside-core analysis, the single-trait chroma overlap with the native reference was lower in the outside-core group (D approximately 0.965 vs 0.860, delta +0.105), but its six-test Holm P was 0.462. The chroma difference is thus supported as a directional descriptive range contrast; a universal novel-climate-specific chroma shift is not established.

Descriptively, introduced C. vulgare locations outside the principal 95% native climatic core were drier and more seasonal than those inside it (median annual precipitation 697.0 versus 897.5 mm; BIO15 precipitation seasonality 60.50 versus 30.05), while median photographed chroma was also higher (32.23 versus 31.40 C*; 201 versus 938 eligible 1° cells). **This between-climatic-group contrast did not imply that lower rainfall and greater seasonality themselves predicted higher chroma.** When the combination of low precipitation and high seasonality was compared directly with the opposing wet/low-seasonality group, C* was instead lower in the dry/high-seasonality group by 1.91 C* in native and 3.12 C* in introduced cells. Moreover, within the outside-native-core introduced cells, simultaneous region-adjusted annual-climate slopes were negative but statistically unsupported for precipitation (BIO12 beta −0.045, pointwise P=0.166) and seasonality (BIO15 beta −0.057, P=0.432; both Holm P=1). Thus, the joint association in the between-group descriptive table was not an independent dry-seasonality effect. The earlier dichotomised rainfall contrast was selected after exploratory inspection and was not adjusted for temperature, radiation or other climatic and geographical covariates.

An exploratory regression within the C. vulgare outside-core group also found a positive association between annual mean temperature and chroma in a model including broad geographical differences (standardised beta +0.157, Holm P=0.025). This did not survive adjustment for region-specific intercepts and was not a significant novel-versus-analogous slope interaction. It cannot be interpreted as temperature-driven or introduction-driven floral adaptation.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Environmental resemblance is not a universal morphological prediction

Observed climatic resemblance corresponded with multivariate photographic head resemblance within native taxa and in the broader 5° between-taxon comparison. Nevertheless, the uncertainty of the 1° between-taxon result and the change in included taxa with aggregation limit claims about how broadly the pattern generalises. Species histories, regional floras, observer effects and camera orientation may contribute to measured resemblance. The result is compatible with environment-associated trait organisation; it does not identify repeated evolutionary convergence.

The positive precipitation–head angle and precipitation-seasonality–chroma associations are plausible directions for mechanistic research. However, annual climatic precipitation does not indicate what happened to pollen at anthesis, and photographed chroma is neither pigment chemistry nor independently calibrated optical reflectance. The functional hypotheses should remain hypotheses.

### 4.2. Range expansion separates the distribution of climates from the distribution of phenotypes

The focal species occupied annual climates with substantial differences between native and introduced range distributions, yet the head-trait distributions retained overlap and geographically matched comparisons did not support a consistent outside-core decrease. This does not demonstrate unchanged trait distributions: limited independent regions, imbalanced geographic support and the rotation-sensitive outline constrain inference. It does show why overlap between climatic niches and similarity of reproductive phenotypes should not be treated as interchangeable variables or automatically causal.

### 4.3. Introduced C. vulgare chroma: a trait-specific signal within a shared morphospace

The C. vulgare chroma result prevents a misleading conclusion that no traits differ after introduction. The introduced distribution has higher mean photographed C* than the native reference, and the difference persists after climate-class balancing. A single component can therefore show a directional range contrast without requiring an equally dramatic reorganisation of joint head-trait space. The regional reference provides a crucial boundary: the introduced deviations are sometimes large relative to typical native regional comparisons, but not beyond the most extreme regional differences observed in the native range.

The higher chroma observed in the outside-native-core group co-occurred with lower annual precipitation and greater precipitation seasonality, but that descriptive ecological grouping concealed a different comparison within the two precipitation regimes. **Low rainfall combined with high seasonality was not itself a robust predictor of increased chroma.** This distinction is essential: a direction of difference between environmental groups need not be the direction of a within-group or adjusted climatic association. Neither the signed colour contrast nor the positive temperature coefficient establishes a genetic or physiological cause. Differential sources of introduced populations, phenotypic plasticity, collection location, flowering stage, exposure and processing remain competing explanations. The question for subsequent work is not simply whether introduced flowers become more colourful, but under what shared climatic conditions and genetic origins such differences persist.

### 4.4. Sampling scale and further validation

Geographic grid size and taxonomic inclusion changed together: the 1° analysis included 63 taxa, whereas the 5° analysis included 31. Before claiming a spatial-grain mechanism, both resolutions should be rerun with the same preselected 31 taxa, maintaining matched photographs and comparable cell eligibility. A rotation-invariant outline measurement and gravity-referenced angle would independently strengthen the interpretation of joint reproductive morphology. Those are next validation steps, not analyses whose outcomes can be stated here.

## 5. Conclusion

The annual climatic environment is associated with the geographical distribution of photographed thistle capitulum traits, but the correspondence depends on sampling level and the trait considered. Climatically similar native locations show local resemblance, while introduced climates can differ substantially from native climates without an obligatory, statistically established shift of whole-head phenotype distributions. Introduced C. vulgare displays a detectable directional chroma contrast within this broader shared space. Observational photos reveal these contrasts, not their evolutionary or functional mechanisms.
