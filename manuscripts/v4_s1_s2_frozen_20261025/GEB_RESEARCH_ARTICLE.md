# Global photographic phenomics reveals climate-associated capitulum traits and local floral resemblance in Cirsium

**Running title:** Climate and thistle capitula

## Abstract

**Aim:** To examine how geographical variation and local resemblance in reproductive appearance correspond to climate, using geographically resolved, continuous measurements from public photographs.

**Location:** Worldwide native *Cirsium* taxa; native and introduced distributions of *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare*.

**Time period:** Photographs from different years; 1981–2010 climatic normals.

**Major taxa studied:** *Cirsium* (Asteraceae).

**Methods:** We linked eight presentation, colour and outline constructs to photographic records and locations, then analysed native-taxon gradients and separate native/introduced strata. We compared joint angle, chroma and aspect at climate-matched cells between taxa assigned to different longitude sectors, using within-taxon vector permutations. Geographic eligibility retained 63 taxa at 1° and 31 at 5°.

**Results:** Complete eight-construct records covered 156,683 photographs across 174 taxa. Across 178 native taxa represented by 168,907 angle-eligible records, taxa sampled in wetter warm-quarter climates had larger image-referenced capitulum angles (standardised β=+0.0454; Holm P=0.0092). Introduced *C. vulgare* showed greater chroma and lower lightness with radiation; introduced *C. arvense* showed lower lightness with elevation. Climate matching reduced joint capitulum distances relative to all sampled cell combinations (+0.041 at 1° and +0.094 at 5°), exceeding within-taxon permutation expectations (exploratory Holm-2 P=0.010 at each resolution). Excess resemblance remained positive across six additional random-seed series, with more stable support at 5°.

**Main conclusions:** Individual-trait gradients and local multivariate resemblance reveal climatic organisation of capitulum appearance within and among the sampled taxa. Retaining continuous measurements, their combinations and their locations links these ecological comparisons and identifies geographical correspondence beyond the compared taxa’s sampled trait distributions. Spatial grain and regional coverage define the scope of this photographic evidence.

**Keywords:** biogeography, capitulum, climate, floral colour, image phenomics, introduced ranges, morphospace, trait variation

## 1. Introduction

Geographical variation in flowers links plant reproductive strategies to the environments in which they occur. Floral presentation and colour influence interactions with pollinators and exposure to abiotic conditions, making their variation relevant to the geographical context of reproduction (Nakata et al., 2022; Dalrymple et al., 2020). A central biogeographic question is whether similar climatic settings accompany similar reproductive forms even when the taxa differ. Regional histories and climate jointly structure plant diversity (Ringelberg et al., 2020; Hanz et al., 2022). Understanding floral resemblance therefore requires both identifying geographical trait gradients and asking where local combinations of traits recur under comparable conditions.

Experimental and comparative studies give these comparisons a functional context. Nodding capitula can protect pollen from rain damage (Chen et al., 2013), motivating an expectation of more downward-oriented heads in wetter environments. Experiments on ultraviolet-absorbing floral patterns support a role for photoprotection (Koski & Ashman, 2015), while darker inflorescences can warm more in sunlight, providing a thermal hypothesis for colour variation in cool conditions (Lacey & Herr, 2005). Broad-scale and elevational studies identify floral-colour relationships with radiation and elevation (Dalrymple et al., 2020; Berardi et al., 2016). Global analyses also associate climate with the extent of variation in some floral and vegetative traits (Kuppler et al., 2020). These findings leave open whether different taxa show similar local combinations of reproductive traits where their sampled climates are similar.

Answering that question requires geographically repeated measurements. Within-species variation contributes substantially to plant trait diversity (Siefert et al., 2015), and its representation depends on sampling level and extent (Anadon-Rosell et al., 2025). Retaining local trait values can strengthen relationships with measured environmental conditions (Govaert et al., 2024). Species means describe typical phenotypes, while measures of trait breadth describe their spread, but neither retains the locations at which particular phenotypes occurred. Even a sampled multivariate trait distribution does not, by itself, preserve that geographical arrangement. For reproductive appearance, repeated measurements must therefore retain both the place of observation and the combination of traits observed there. This permits comparisons between actual climatic analogues against the resemblance expected from the compared taxa's sampled phenotype distributions.

Public photographs offer geographical coverage and visible detail for such comparisons, complementing trait databases with persistent taxonomic and regional gaps (Kattge et al., 2020; Grenié et al., 2025). Photographic approaches have enabled trait prediction using species-linked training information (Schiller et al., 2021), while citizen photographs have revealed climatic and urban associations of colour-morph frequencies (Kim, Bae & Kang, 2026). A further opportunity is to retain continuous variation within each visible trait, combinations of traits on the same photographic record and the location of that record. Image angles, colour coordinates and outlines preserve intermediate values within categories such as upright, nodding or pale. Their joint geographical representation allows local comparisons of reproductive appearance across climatic conditions.

Thistles of the genus *Cirsium* provide a common capitulum architecture across contrasting environments and regional radiations (Moreyra et al., 2025; Siniscalchi, Ackerfield & Folk, 2023). Public photographs capture variation in their presentation, floral colour and outline. Extensive native and introduced distributions of *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare* add within-species comparisons across different environmental opportunities and establishment histories (Hulme & Barrett, 2013; Colautti & Lau, 2015). Analysing each range separately describes colour gradients within a shared species identity and its sampled environmental context, complementing the broader comparison among native taxa.

Here, we combine organ detection, continuous pixel and geometry measurements, and geographical linkage to address three connected questions. First, among native taxa, does capitulum presentation vary with warm-quarter precipitation after accounting for temperature, radiation and elevation? Second, within the native and introduced distributions of the two widespread species, how does floral colour vary with radiation and elevation? Third, do taxa assigned to different geographic sectors have more similar joint capitulum phenotypes at locations matched for precipitation, temperature and radiation? For this final comparison, we examine image-referenced angle, chroma and aspect ratio against permutations preserving each taxon's sampled cell-level multivariate phenotype distribution. Together, the analyses connect climatic gradients in individual traits with local resemblance in their combinations, testing the climatic organisation of geographical variation in reproductive appearance.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Sources, taxon eligibility and non-independence

The measurement and comparison workflow is summarised in Figure 1. We used two archived observation sources. The native-taxon source (S1) contained **313,350** WCVP-assigned native observations and supplied the native climatic-gradient and local climate-analogue analyses. The two-species source (S2) contained **297,622** wild observations of *Cirsium arvense* and *C. vulgare*: **141,014 native, 156,608 introduced**. WCVP assignments distinguished native from explicitly introduced regional status (Govaerts et al., 2021). Records with other statuses were not reassigned as introduced.

The two focal species were initially selected for extensive photographic coverage in both ranges. A subsequent audit of all **350** original species-rank names identified **seven** with both native and explicitly introduced wild records. Only the two focal species met all three coverage requirements in each range: at least **100** angle-eligible observations, **15** occupied broad 5° cells defined using equal sine-latitude bands, and **10** observers. This retrospective feasibility screen used eligibility and coverage, not measured trait values or significance; the species were not randomly selected. They contributed **156,608 of 156,750** explicitly introduced wild observations (**99.91%**) in this source archive, with strong geographical concentration. Full eligibility results are in Table S1.1 and [Supporting Information](SI_S2_FOCAL_SPECIES_SELECTION_AUDIT.md).

Source totals, photographs, detected heads and trait-eligible measurements represent different sampling levels. Eligibility was evaluated separately for each response. Native observations can occur in both S1 and S2, and the local climate-analogue analysis reuses S1; these comparisons do not provide independent source replication. Species-specific source and angle-eligible counts appear in Table 1. Source identifiers and precise coordinates were retained internally, while public outputs use permissible aggregates. Figure 2 shows analysis-specific sampling coverage pooled into 5° latitude–longitude cells, with cells containing fewer than five eligible records omitted.

### 2.2. Continuous visible phenotypes and matched photographs

The image workflow joined detection, measurement and geographical linkage. YOLO located candidate capitula; subsequent routines measured their pixel colours, image axis and segmented outline. We reused archived numerical exports and their trait-specific quality flags. Detection therefore defined candidate regions, while the continuous measurements were calculated from image pixels and geometry. The source-linked scripts and exact definitions are documented in [Table S2.1](SI_PHOTOGRAPHIC_TRAIT_MEASUREMENT_DEFINITIONS_20261029.md).

Eight constructs covered three domains: presentation (image-referenced capitulum angle), colour (CIELAB lightness and chroma, and HSV-derived circular hue), and outline (aspect ratio, circularity, solidity and width-profile coefficient of variation). Angle increases from the image top (0°), through horizontal (90°), to the image bottom (180°), after available EXIF orientation correction. Hue used sine and cosine coordinates, counted jointly as one construct. Aspect was the height-to-width ratio of the bounding box after PCA-based rotation of the segmented mask. Circularity and solidity described boundary compactness and convex-hull occupancy; the width coefficient described variation among row-wise mask widths. Table S2.1 specifies the floral-pixel masks and geometry calculations.

Eligible head-level values were summarised by medians within each observation–photo record, retaining taxon and location. The contributing heads could differ among trait domains. Joint comparisons therefore refer to the same photographic record, rather than necessarily the same individual capitulum. Continuous coordinates preserve observed variation without imposing botanical category boundaries. Their units remain photographic: image angle is not gravity-calibrated inclination, colour is not a pigment assay, and outline depends on projection and flowering stage.

The native angle analysis used **168,907 records from 178 taxa**. The eight-construct comparison retained **156,683 photographs across 174 taxa** with complete measurements and environmental data, making all 28 construct pairs comparable on the same records. The three-trait climate-analogue comparison applied its own eligibility gate (Section 2.7).

### 2.3. Environmental data and analysis-specific predictors

Environmental values were extracted at source-pixel resolution from CHELSA v2.1 climatic normals for 1981–2010 and WorldClim v2.1 elevation. Native-taxon and within-species regressions used the same four predictors: BIO18 (mean monthly precipitation during the warmest quarter), BIO05 (maximum temperature of the warmest month), RSDS (downwelling shortwave radiation) and elevation. The maximum variance inflation factor in the original four-variable source matrix was **4.9559**. Climatic normals describe long-term conditions rather than weather at the photographed flowering event.

The local climate-analogue analysis used a separate three-axis representation: annual precipitation (BIO12), mean temperature (BIO01) and RSDS. These coordinates describe climatic conditions at sampled occurrences; they do not estimate the fundamental niche or available environmental background. The regression model was fixed after earlier model exploration. The H3c comparison was an exploratory, post-freeze extension, with its matching threshold and two-metric family fixed before that analysis was run. Neither constitutes a prospectively preregistered confirmatory study.

### 2.4. Supplementary matched-photograph trait associations

The eight-construct comparison is a descriptive analysis of the existing observations, **not an independent source cohort**. It retains all 28 construct-pair associations, using taxon-centred phenotypes with equal total taxon weight within taxa and equally weighted taxon means among taxa. Scalar pairs use squared correlations; hue uses both circular coordinates. Associations before and after four-predictor adjustment, descriptive ranks among 280 domain-label allocations, thresholded three-trait combinations and the limited involucre proxies are documented in [Supporting Information](SI_V4_FULL_COVARIATION_AND_CONFIGURATION_CONTEXT.md). These analyses describe phenotypic covariation without establishing genetic or functional integration (Pigliucci, 2003; Diggle, 2014).

Additional exploratory comparisons of climatic breadth, trait breadth and taxon centroids are reported in the [breadth supplement](SI_H3_CLIMATE_MORPHOSPACE_BREADTH_20261029.md); single-trait breadth and environmental moderation of covariance are in the [covariance supplement](SI_H3_ENVIRONMENT_MODERATED_TRAIT_COVARIANCE_20261029.md). The complementary H3c measure is a normalised Gaussian-KDE density similarity, not a thresholded hypervolume intersection (Blonder et al., 2014). It remains in the original two-metric correction family and is reported in the [climate-analogue supplement](SI_H3_CLIMATE_ANALOGUE_CONDITIONAL_OVERLAP_20261029.md).

### 2.5. Climatic associations within and among native taxa

Native-taxon models separated taxon-level climate means from within-taxon environmental deviations, using equal total taxon weighting and taxon-cluster-robust uncertainty. All four predictors entered simultaneously. Availability and quality gates were applied for each outcome, and hue coordinates were tested jointly with two degrees of freedom. Holm correction covered the original **80-test family**: 10 constructs (eight principal constructs and two supplementary involucre proxies) × four predictors × two taxonomic scales. Coefficient confidence intervals are pointwise 95% intervals. The complete unadjusted, BH-adjusted and Holm-adjusted results, including the within-taxon angle estimate, remain in [Supporting Information](SI_S1_BIO18_ANGLE_RAW_BH_HOLM_CONTEXT.md).

Spatial sensitivity analyses retained the original 168,907 angle records and 178 taxa. One set added geographic predictors. A complementary analysis retained the four-predictor model and assigned taxa to their dominant 20° spatial block using occupied 5° cells. It resampled intact taxon blocks 399 times across 26 blocks and omitted each of the five largest contributing blocks. The two approaches assess model dependence and geographical sampling stability, respectively; neither causally partitions climate from geography. They are separate from the original Holm family ([Supporting Information](SI_S1_BIO18_SPATIAL_SENSITIVITY_20261029.md)).

### 2.6. Within-species native and introduced range comparisons

We fitted the four predictors separately within *C. arvense* native, *C. arvense* introduced, *C. vulgare* native and *C. vulgare* introduced strata. Fits balanced observer × broad 5° longitude/sine-latitude cell, adjusted identifiable month and year effects, and used cell-clustered uncertainty. The original **128-test family** comprised eight photographic constructs × four predictors × four strata; hue counted as one construct. Holm correction was applied separately from S1. For the three introduced-stratum colour associations highlighted in Results, we present their native counterparts from the original models, with the full six-row comparison in [Supporting Information](SI_S2_COLOUR_NATIVE_INTRO_FIXED_HOLM_CONTEXT.md).

Range status denotes a regional distribution classification rather than a dated introduction or known ancestral population. Comparisons are made **without** comparing native–introduced rainfall niches. This is not a randomised climate intervention, and no **direct interaction test** of range × environment was fitted. Climatic support and predictor scaling differ among strata. Geographic clines and source-population sampling can complicate evolutionary interpretations of native–introduced differences (Colautti, Maron & Barrett, 2009).

For spatial sensitivity, we restored the original cohorts, including the all-12 source-pixel environmental-completeness rule, and verified all six coefficients and denominators before resampling. We retained the original model, weighting and date adjustments, resampled whole 20° latitude–longitude blocks 399 times within each stratum, and separately omitted the five largest contributing blocks. Bootstrap percentile intervals describe spatial sampling sensitivity; they are not new Holm-adjusted intervals ([Supporting Information](SI_S2_COLOUR_SPATIAL_BLOCK_SENSITIVITY_20261029.md)).

### 2.7. Local climatic analogues and multivariate capitulum resemblance

We tested whether similar climates correspond to greater joint capitulum resemblance between taxa assigned to different longitude sectors. Here, local refers to occurrence-based grid-cell summaries. From S1, we retained observations with eligible image-referenced angle, CIELAB chroma and two-dimensional outline aspect ratio on the same photograph, together with finite BIO12, BIO01 and RSDS values. Within each taxon × 1° latitude–longitude cell, we calculated the median of each of the six coordinates. Eligible taxa required at least 40 matched photographs and eight occupied cells. Each axis was standardised using weighted means and standard deviations that gave equal total weight to each eligible taxon. A parallel 5° sensitivity analysis repeated aggregation, eligibility and standardisation for its own sample; the two resolutions therefore have different standardised distance scales.

Taxa were assigned to the longitude sector containing the largest number of their occupied cells: west (<−30°), middle (−30° to <60°) or east (≥60°). We compared taxa assigned to different sectors. This groups taxa by sampled geography; individual matched cells need not lie in different sectors, and the groups are not independent evolutionary lineages.

For each of 24 rarefactions, we sampled eight occupied cells without replacement per taxon. For each eligible taxon pair, the 64 cross-taxon cell combinations were compared using Euclidean distance in the three standardised climate coordinates. All combinations at distance ≤0.9 were climatic analogues. A taxon pair qualified if at least three cell pairs matched, involving at least two cells of each taxon. This was a many-to-many comparison rather than nearest-neighbour matching.

For a qualifying taxon pair, the distance reduction was

\[
\Delta D = \overline{d}_{\mathrm{all\ 64\ cell\ pairs}} - \overline{d}_{\mathrm{climate\ analogues}},
\]

where \(d\) is Euclidean distance in standardised angle, chroma and aspect ratio. Positive values indicate closer photographic capitulum phenotypes among climatic analogues. We averaged these reductions equally across qualifying taxon pairs, then across the 24 rarefactions. Each taxon can enter different numbers of pairs; equal taxon weight applies to standardisation, whereas the comparison statistic gives equal weight to taxon pairs.

For each rarefaction, 199 permutations reassigned complete three-trait cell vectors among the eight selected cells within each taxon. The same taxon's permutation was shared across all its pairwise comparisons. This retains its sampled multivariate phenotype distribution and trait covariation while disrupting correspondence with local climate. Climate coordinates, analogue masks and regional assignments remain fixed. For each permutation index, statistics were averaged across rarefactions before computing a two-sided Monte Carlo P value, centred on the mean null statistic and using the plus-one correction. The 24 rarefactions are not independent tests. At each resolution, Holm correction retained the original two-metric family: distance reduction and supplementary KDE density similarity.

After the initial analysis, sensitivity checks varied the matching threshold (0.7 and 1.1), omitted individual traits, omitted geographic sectors at 1° resolution, and examined six additional random-seed series under the original settings. These checks used 24 rarefactions and 99 permutations per setting or series. They evaluate sensitivity and Monte Carlo stability on the same source data ([Supporting Information](SI_H3C_POSTPOSITIVE_ROBUSTNESS_20261029.md)).

### 2.8. Provenance and reproducibility

Source identities, fixed environmental extraction and executable model specifications link the reported estimates to the archived observations. The internal identifiers S1 and S2 denote source and test families, not current section numbers. H3c reuses S1 and does **not** create a source S3 or change the original 80/128-test families. The original coefficients, source hashes and complete correction tables remain unchanged. Supporting Information preserves additional exploratory analyses so that the focused main-text presentation does not redefine their test families. Durable anonymous access to redistributable tables and code is being prepared; source photographs retain their original licences.

**Scope:** no native-versus-introduced climatic-niche analysis, no native/introduced range × BIO18 interaction test, and no reanalysis of archived four-dimensional climatic niche occupancy is included.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Global image measurements and precipitation-associated capitulum presentation

The photographic framework assembled continuous presentation, colour and outline measurements jointly for **156,683 shared native photographs from 174 taxa**, supporting all 28 construct-pair comparisons on the same records (Supporting Information). The native source comprised **313,350 observational records**, of which **168,907 records from 178 taxa** met the angle-specific eligibility criteria. These are distinct measurement cohorts, rather than interchangeable counts of individual capitula. For the within-species comparison, two species met the native- and introduced-range source-coverage criteria, yielding **297,622 wild source observations** (Table 1).

Across native taxa, the strongest multiplicity-supported climatic result was the association of **image-referenced capitulum angle with precipitation during the warmest quarter (BIO18)**. The standardized among-taxon coefficient was **+0.045447** (pointwise 95% CI **+0.022349 to +0.068545**, raw P=**0.00011508**, Holm P=**0.00920635**) using the 168,907 angle-eligible records. Because angles increase towards the bottom of the image, taxa photographed in wetter regions tended to show more downward-looking head presentation after conditioning on maximum warm-month temperature, solar radiation and elevation. This was the **only Holm-surviving association among the 80 original native-taxon trait–environment tests**. The within-taxon BIO18 coefficient is supplied with its raw, BH and Holm values in Supporting Information; it did not survive Holm correction (Figure 3; Table 2).

In a separate spatial-block resampling sensitivity, the original **+0.045447** coefficient was reproduced without geographic fixed effects. Whole-taxon bootstrap resampling across **26 spatial blocks** gave a **95% percentile interval of +0.014569 to +0.084483** (399 draws), with **99.5%** of draws retaining the positive sign and **all five largest block exclusions** retaining positive coefficients. Geographic-term conditioning separately attenuated the partial coefficient and cannot distinguish climate from spatially aligned taxon turnover; both analyses are reported as exploratory sensitivity in [Supporting Information](SI_S1_BIO18_SPATIAL_SENSITIVITY_20261029.md), not additional Holm tests.

### 3.2. Radiation and elevation gradients in native and introduced ranges

In the four species-by-range-status models, **three of 128 original trait–environment tests** passed Holm correction, all in introduced strata. For *C. vulgare*, increasing solar radiation (RSDS) was associated with higher photographic chroma (standardized β=**+0.134504**, CI **+0.066158 to +0.202850**, Holm P=**0.01456813**) and lower lightness (β=**−0.142801**, CI **−0.216718 to −0.068884**, Holm P=**0.01925414**), each from **27,200 trait-eligible observations**. For introduced *C. arvense*, lightness declined with elevation (β=**−0.173732**, CI **−0.250914 to −0.096550**, Holm P=**0.00131233**) among **26,171 trait-eligible observations** (Figure 4; Table 2).

The corresponding native-stratum point estimates were heterogeneous. In *C. vulgare*, radiation × chroma was **+0.136883** in the native stratum (95% CI **+0.056600 to +0.217166**, Holm P=**0.102398**) versus +0.134504 in the introduced stratum. Native *C. vulgare* lightness × radiation was **−0.048808** (CI **−0.147073 to +0.049457**, Holm P=1.0), compared with −0.142801 in the introduced stratum. For *C. arvense*, native lightness × elevation was **+0.056267** (CI **−0.018662 to +0.131195**, Holm P=1.0), compared with −0.173732 in the introduced stratum. The native–introduced differences in the **Holm decisions** are descriptive outcomes of separate regressions; **no direct range-status × environmental predictor interaction was tested**. None of the 16 original photo-angle × exposure terms in the four strata survived Holm correction.

The six native and introduced colour coefficients and eligible sample sizes were also reproduced exactly before a **20° spatial-block sensitivity**. Among the three original Holm-positive introduced terms, source-block bootstrap intervals remained negative for *C. arvense* lightness × elevation (**−0.22949 to −0.05578**) and *C. vulgare* lightness × solar radiation (**−0.28093 to −0.01521**). The introduced *C. vulgare* chroma × radiation interval (**−0.07581 to +0.20119**) included zero despite **94.7%** of block samples preserving its positive sign; all five largest contributing-block omissions maintained the original sign for each term. Native *C. vulgare* chroma × radiation had a positive spatial-block interval (**+0.06363 to +0.22540**). These post-freeze intervals are **exploratory spatial sensitivity**, not original S2 128-family Holm adjustments, not tests of native–introduced slope differences, and not a spatial–climate causal decomposition ([Supporting Information](SI_S2_COLOUR_SPATIAL_BLOCK_SENSITIVITY_20261029.md)).

### 3.3. Similar local climates are associated with more similar capitulum phenotypes

Photographic capitulum phenotypes were more similar between taxa assigned to different longitude sectors when comparisons were restricted to locally analogous climates. Joint quality control for image-referenced head angle, CIELAB chroma and two-dimensional outline aspect ratio yielded **157,822 records across 242 taxa**. Geographic sampling criteria retained **63 taxa** for the primary 1° analysis and **31 taxa** for the 5° sensitivity analysis. The local climate-analogue analysis matched annual precipitation (BIO12), mean temperature (BIO01) and shortwave radiation (RSDS), comparing taxa assigned to different longitude sectors.

At 1° resolution, the mean reduction in three-trait distance between climate-matched cells relative to all sampled cell combinations was **+0.04096** standardised distance units across 24 eight-cell rarefactions. The corresponding reduction was **+0.09433** at 5°. Both reductions exceeded expectations from permutations that reassigned complete three-trait vectors among cells within each taxon while preserving its sampled cell-level phenotype distribution (199 permutations; **Holm-2 P=0.010** at each resolution, from the original exploratory two-metric family; Figure 5). Mean reductions under this null were −0.00127 and +0.00018, respectively.

The distance effect remained positive in all six additional random-seed series at both resolutions. Statistical support was more stable at 5°; the 1° estimates were sensitive to the matching threshold, trait composition and regional coverage ([Supporting Information](SI_H3C_POSTPOSITIVE_ROBUSTNESS_20261029.md)). These results identify a local correspondence between sampled climatic conditions and the joint photographic appearance of capitula across the compared taxa. Full matching and complementary density-overlap estimates are provided in [Supporting Information](SI_H3_CLIMATE_ANALOGUE_CONDITIONAL_OVERLAP_20261029.md).

## 4. Discussion

Together, the three analyses indicate climatic organisation of geographical variation and resemblance in reproductive appearance. Among native *Cirsium* taxa, wetter warm-quarter climates accompanied more downward-looking capitulum angles in photographs. Within the ranges of two widespread species, floral colour varied with radiation or elevation, with three associations supported in introduced strata. Across taxa assigned to different geographic sectors, joint appearance was more similar at climate-matched cells than expected from within-taxon reassignment of the observed phenotype vectors. These results connect individual-trait gradients to the geographical arrangement of combinations of traits. Public photographs made this comparison possible by retaining continuous measurements, their combinations on the same photographic records and their observed locations.

### 4.1. Precipitation, head presentation and the rain-protection hypothesis

Native taxa photographed in wetter warm-quarter climates tended to have a larger downward-looking image angle, after accounting for temperature, radiation and elevation. This among-taxon association identifies a geographical dimension of capitulum presentation; the within-taxon estimate did not retain support under the same multiple-testing correction.

The avoidance of pollen wetting and wash-off provides a biologically specific explanation to examine. In *Cremanthodium campanulatum*, water damaged pollen and experimentally holding normally nodding capitula upright reduced achene production (Chen et al., 2013). Work on *Platycodon grandiflorus* also connects floral orientation to pollen transfer and rain-damage avoidance (Nakata et al., 2022). These studies link orientation to the exposure of reproductive tissues, giving the *Cirsium* gradient a functional interpretation to test across climates.

Our predictor is a **1981–2010 climatic normal**, and our response is an angle within an image. The association is therefore not evidence of rainfall-mediated selection. A direct functional comparison would measure capitulum inclination relative to gravity and relate it to pollen wetting, viability or loss under comparable rain exposure. The geographic pattern identifies taxa and climatic settings in which to connect head presentation to reproductive performance.

### 4.2. Floral colour and climate across native and introduced ranges

The conspecific native–introduced comparison provides a second ecological perspective. Introduced *C. vulgare* displayed greater chroma and lower lightness with radiation, while introduced *C. arvense* displayed lower lightness with elevation. These are three Holm-supported comparisons from a single 128-test family. They extend the climatic perspective from differences among native taxa to geographical variation within the sampled ranges of species.

The greater chroma of introduced *C. vulgare* is qualitatively consistent with the positive radiation–chromatic contrast relationship reported across flowering plants, although the colour metrics differ (Dalrymple et al., 2020). This pattern motivates a photoprotection hypothesis. Experiments in *Argentina anserina* show that UV exposure can favour greater UV-absorbing petal coverage, providing an independent functional basis for considering radiation-related floral pigmentation (Koski & Ashman, 2015). Visible chroma and UV absorption are different optical properties, however. Our accompanying decrease in lightness describes another aspect of photographic colour; it does not establish pigment concentration or UV screening. Because chroma and lightness share the same photographed colour information, their associations need not represent separate mechanisms.

The lower lightness of introduced *C. arvense* at higher elevations raises a distinct thermal prediction: where higher elevation accompanies cooler growing conditions, darker reproductive tissues could facilitate solar heat gain. In *Plantago lanceolata*, lower developmental temperatures produced darker, less reflective inflorescences that subsequently became warmer in sunlight (Lacey & Herr, 2005). Elevational observations in *Silene vulgaris* also linked darker, more anthocyanin-rich calyces to cooler environments, with temperature and precipitation associations providing stronger support than a direct solar-radiation relationship (Berardi et al., 2016). These findings motivate a thermal interpretation of the *C. arvense* gradient. Elevation is not itself a measure of floral temperature, and broadband shortwave radiation is not UV exposure. Reflectance, pigment composition and head-temperature measurements at contrasting sites would help distinguish the photoprotective and thermal hypotheses.

The native estimates help distinguish the occurrence of an association from evidence of a range difference. For radiation × chroma in *C. vulgare*, the native coefficient (**+0.136883**) was close to the introduced coefficient (**+0.134504**) on the reported standardised scales, despite the native Holm P of 0.102398. This is compatible with an association present in both distributional contexts. For *C. arvense*, the native lightness × elevation point estimate was weakly positive (+0.056267), whereas the introduced estimate was negative. That contrast identifies a more specific question for matched environmental sampling, but neither comparison supplies a formal test of slope differences.

Native and introduced populations may differ through source-population sorting, plasticity, ecological fitting or evolution following establishment (Hulme & Barrett, 2013; Colautti & Lau, 2015; van Kleunen, Bossdorf & Dawson, 2018). Controlled experiments have found stress-related expression differences between native and introduced *C. arvense* (Guggisberg et al., 2013), supporting the biological interest of these comparisons. Our within-range gradients provide a geographical basis for testing these processes and the colour-related functional hypotheses; distinguishing changes following introduction requires matched environmental comparisons and a direct range × environment interaction.

### 4.3. Local climatic similarity and joint capitulum resemblance

The local comparison extends individual-trait gradients to the combinations of traits found at particular sites. Taxa assigned to different geographic sectors had more similar joint photographic capitulum phenotypes at cells matched for precipitation, temperature and radiation. The distance reduction exceeded expectations when complete cell-level trait vectors were reassigned within each taxon. This comparison preserves the sampled distribution of each trait and its covariation with the other two. The additional resemblance therefore concerns the geographical arrangement of those phenotypes: knowing which grid-cell climates they occupied was informative beyond knowing the compared taxa's sampled trait distributions.

This finding connects the among-taxon presentation gradient and within-range colour gradients to a broader biogeographic question: where do similar reproductive forms occur among different taxa? Studies of biome similarity have combined functional and phylogenetic evidence to examine resemblance across regions (Ringelberg et al., 2020). Here, repeated photographic observations identify a grid-cell correspondence in reproductive appearance. Angle, colour and outline need not each follow the same gradient for their joint appearance to correspond locally with climate. The result extends the spatial perspective of local trait–environment studies (Govaert et al., 2024) to multidimensional reproductive appearance across regional groups of taxa.

The geographical signal has a defined empirical scope. Null-relative distance effects remained positive across six additional random-seed series at both resolutions; unadjusted P < 0.05 occurred in two of six series at 1° and all six at 5°. At 1°, omitting chroma or the middle longitude sector reversed these effects under paired sensitivity draws. Thus spatial grain, trait composition and regional coverage affect the evidence. Because the 5° analysis has fewer taxa and a separately standardised trait space, its larger numerical reduction does not quantify a stronger biological effect in common units.

Environmental responses within taxa, regional population differentiation and correlated local conditions could each contribute to this pattern. Its ecological value is to identify comparable climatic settings in which different taxa express similar combinations of reproductive traits. Calibrated field measurements at those sites would test whether their photographic resemblance extends to anatomical and optical traits; a source-matched phylogeny would then allow questions about the evolutionary origins of that resemblance.

### 4.4. What public photographs add to floral macroecology

Globally distributed public photographs supported joint measurement across 156,683 records and 174 taxa. The complete eight-construct cohort made all 28 construct pairs comparable on the same photographic records, avoiding changes in record composition between pairwise summaries. The three-trait application then retained geographical placement to compare climatic analogues. These are two demonstrated capabilities of the framework: characterising continuous covariation on a common sample and evaluating climatic correspondence within sampled taxon distributions.

This approach complements trait prediction from species-linked photographic training data (Schiller et al., 2021) and macroecological analyses of colour-morph frequencies (Kim, Bae & Kang, 2026). It measures multiple visible dimensions directly from image regions and carries their joint record into geographical comparisons. In remote sensing, combining spectral and structural observations has likewise connected complementary measurements to distinct biodiversity questions (Kamoske et al., 2022). Here, the comparison enabled by that combination is local climatic correspondence in reproductive appearance. The full within- and among-taxon image-metric covariance analysis remains in Supporting Information, while climate matching demonstrates an ecological application of the shared measurements.

Measurement interpretation determines how this contribution can be extended. Public images provide photographic appearance; calibrated imaging can additionally recover physical reflectance over specified wavelengths (Wang et al., 2023). Illumination, viewpoint and flowering stage may contribute to our observed variation, and cell medians do not remove systematic differences in photography. Validation of citizen-photo colour in other organisms supports its potential but does not calibrate these *Cirsium* measurements (Laitly et al., 2021). Standardised multi-view images, gravity references and spectra at climate-matched sites would connect the extensive photographic record to field traits. The current framework provides both a repeatable comparison and a geographically explicit basis for selecting those sites.

### 4.5. Statistical scope and generalisability

The analyses combine large photographic samples with smaller numbers of taxa, geographic cells and range strata. Their precision and transferability depend on these higher-level units. Holm correction preserves the declared 80/128 regression-test families and the separate H3c two-metric family; it does not account retrospectively for all earlier model exploration. H3c is exploratory, and its within-taxon permutations disrupt spatial trait arrangement without controlling all spatial autocorrelation or photographic confounding. Geographic sectors group sampled taxon distributions and do not establish independent evolutionary origins.

The regional representation is uneven: the eastern sector contributes six eligible taxa at 1° and five at 5°, while the native–introduced comparison covers two species. Further geographically balanced sampling is needed to assess generality. Together with the measurement limits described above, these features define the present inference as climatic associations of photographic capitulum traits and local multivariate resemblance. Functional protection, reproductive performance and the processes producing population differences require direct measurements.

## 5. Conclusion

Public photographs reveal climatic organisation of geographical variation and resemblance in *Cirsium* capitula. The precipitation–presentation association among native taxa and the within-range colour gradients identify individual dimensions of this pattern. The local climate-analogue comparison extends it to the geographical arrangement of joint phenotypes: different taxa resemble one another more closely at comparable sampled climates than expected from within-taxon reassignment of their observed trait vectors. Retaining continuous values, combinations on the same photographic records and their locations connects these ecological comparisons. Within the spatial and taxonomic coverage examined, this approach extends floral macroecology from describing individual-trait gradients to identifying where similar reproductive forms occur across taxa.
