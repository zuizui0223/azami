# Global photographic phenomics reveals precipitation-associated capitulum presentation and floral colour gradients in Cirsium

**Running title:** Climate and thistle capitula

## Abstract

**Aim:** To test whether continuous traits recovered from globally distributed photographs reveal climatic associations across native *Cirsium* diversity, and whether comparable trait–environment patterns are detectable in historically distinct native and introduced ranges of the same species.

**Location:** Worldwide native *Cirsium* taxa; native and introduced records of *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare*.

**Time period:** Photographic observations with varying dates; 1981–2010 climate normals.

**Major taxa studied:** *Cirsium* (Asteraceae).

**Methods:** We used automated capitulum localisation, continuous image-referenced angle, CIELAB floral colour and two-dimensional outline measures, with trait-specific quality screening. The native-source atlas comprised 313,350 observations; its angle analysis used 168,907 eligible records across 178 taxa. Four environmental predictors (warm-quarter precipitation, warm-month maximum temperature, solar radiation and elevation) were modelled across taxonomic scales (80 Holm-adjusted tests). A source-audited comparison of 297,622 wild records from two species examined four native/introduced strata (128 tests). Same-photo covariance was retained as supplementary descriptive evidence.

**Results:** Among native taxa, greater warm-quarter precipitation corresponded to a larger, more downward-looking image-referenced capitulum angle (standardised β=+0.0454; Holm P=0.0092). In introduced *C. vulgare*, higher radiation corresponded to higher image chroma (β=+0.1345) and lower lightness (β=−0.1428); in introduced *C. arvense*, elevation corresponded to lower lightness (β=−0.1737). These three colour associations survived Holm correction, while none did in native strata. However, the native *C. vulgare* radiation–chroma point estimate was similar to its introduced estimate.

**Main conclusions:** Public photographs enable large-scale measurement of continuous floral traits beyond categorical descriptions. The precipitation–presentation association is consistent with a rain-exposure hypothesis, while the introduced-range colour gradients are compatible with prior radiation- and elevation-related colour research. Neither rainfall protection nor evolutionary change after introduction is established by these observational comparisons.

**Keywords:** biogeography, capitulum, citizen science, climate, floral colour, image phenomics, introduced ranges, trait variation

## 1. Introduction

Plants have diversified across climatic regimes and biogeographic regions, but it remains difficult to determine how much of their visible morphological diversity reflects local environmental associations rather than lineage history or geographic turnover. This distinction matters because similar climatic settings may harbour species with different evolutionary backgrounds, whereas related species may occupy contrasting climates. Large functional-trait databases have transformed macroecology, but observations compressed into one or a few values per species cannot recover the full geographic distribution of phenotypes or separate differences among taxa from variation within them (Kattge et al., 2020; Siefert et al., 2015).

Categorical botanical descriptions introduce a second source of compression. A head may be described as upright or nodding, a corolla as pale or pigmented, or an outline as broad or elongated, even when the underlying phenotypes vary continuously. These classifications are useful for identification but do not quantify intermediate states, their frequency or their correspondence to environmental gradients. Repeated biodiversity photographs offer a complementary record: computer vision can localise the same reproductive structure across observations, and image-derived angles, colour coordinates and outlines can be compared numerically (Schiller et al., 2021). Unequal sampling, camera position, lighting and species identification still require explicit quality filters and uncertainty; the outcome is a geographically repeated **photographic phenotype**, not an error-free anatomical or genetic measurement (Laitly et al., 2021).

The genus *Cirsium* is particularly informative for such a comparison. Its capitulum is a compact reproductive structure containing many florets, bordered by an involucre; within this common architecture, taxa vary in whole-head presentation, corolla colour, silhouette and involucre form. More importantly, *Cirsium* includes geographically separated regional radiations and spans contrasting climatic regimes. Nuclear phylogenomic work identifies rapid Pleistocene radiations following dispersal into Japan and North America (Moreyra et al., 2025), and an independent analysis of North American thistles associated diversification dynamics with precipitation, semiaridity and Pleistocene landscape history (Siniscalchi, Ackerfield & Folk, 2023). Together with extensive public-image coverage, these histories make the genus a useful replicated **comparative context** for environmental organisation of visible flower-head traits. The radiations themselves do not show that each morphological difference is adaptive or that independent origins have been established in the present photographs.

An especially concrete hypothesis concerns precipitation and the direction in which capitula are presented. If floral organs are vulnerable to rain, more downward-oriented heads could lessen water exposure of pollen-bearing and receptive tissues; alternatively, head geometry may be shaped by structural constraints or taxonomic turnover that happens to covary with climate. In another Asteraceae, experimentally altered nodding capitula of *Cremanthodium campanulatum* provided evidence that orientation and involucral morphology can protect pollen from water and ultraviolet exposure (Chen et al., 2013). This establishes the **biological plausibility**, not the mechanism, for a comparative analysis of precipitation and *Cirsium* head presentation. Solar radiation and elevation provide a complementary set of hypotheses for floral pigmentation and visible colour: published macroscale and population studies have documented radiation-linked colour variation and elevational floral-colour clines (Dalrymple et al., 2020; Berardi et al., 2016). Their responses cannot be assumed to apply universally or to represent the same spectral and chemical measurements as photographic CIELAB values.

A global comparison among native taxa can identify whether mean photographic traits vary with climate, but cannot by itself reveal whether a conspecific trait–environment relationship persists when a plant expands beyond its historical range. Human-mediated introductions provide a complementary, natural-experiment-like comparison: a species' native and secondary distributions share much of their taxonomic background, but have different biogeographic histories and may experience different combinations of environmental conditions. Trait–environment slopes across the two distributions can generate hypotheses about pre-existing variation, ecological fitting, plasticity and evolution following establishment (Hulme & Barrett, 2013; Colautti & Lau, 2015). Experimental work on *C. arvense* has already documented native-versus-introduced differences in gene-expression responses to shading and nutrient stress (Guggisberg et al., 2013), making the comparison biologically motivated while leaving floral-colour evolution untested. Introduced populations are not randomised experiments, and their present-day photographs cannot date evolutionary changes or disentangle these processes by themselves.

We therefore combine **global photographic phenotyping** with two linked ecological comparisons. First, we quantify multiple continuous capitulum traits and retain their matched-photograph covariance as an exploratory description of trait organisation (**Part 1**). Second, we test climatic relationships within and among native taxa, focusing on the corrected association between warm-quarter precipitation (BIO18) and image-referenced capitulum angle (**Part 2**). Third, we use two sufficiently photographed *Cirsium* species to examine the environmental gradients of the same image-derived traits separately within their native and introduced distributions (**Part 3**). These parts reuse source material and address distinct observational questions, **not independent experiments**. Neither a phylogenetic test of repeated adaptation nor a native-versus-introduced climatic-niche comparison is claimed.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Sources, taxon eligibility and non-independence

The Part 2 source population comprised **313,350** WCVP-assigned native observations. The Part 3 source population comprised **297,622** wild observations of *Cirsium arvense* and *C. vulgare*, classified separately by WCVP native or explicitly introduced regional status. Part 3 uses those four **species × range-status strata** to estimate photographed phenotype–environment associations, not to infer climatic-niche shifts or changes caused by introductions. Both focal species were initially chosen for extensive, measureable records across native and introduced ranges. A subsequent **source-verified census of all 350 original pre-detector species-rank names** identified seven species with both WCVP-assigned wild native and explicitly introduced regional records. The same *stronger* feasibility rule required, **in each range independently**, at least **100 image-angle-eligible observations**, **15 occupied equal-sine-latitude broad 5° geographic cells**, and **10 independent observers**. Only the two original focal species passed all three requirements **in both range statuses** (Table S3). The other five species had **2–32 assessable introduced-range photographs** and just **1–4 introduced cells**, below even the stronger minimum. This complete screen **followed** the initial focal choices; it retrospectively validated that no other original source species met the same coverage gates, rather than providing prospective random taxon selection. The source audit evaluated sampling adequacy without selecting on actual photographed angle values, climate associations or significance. Together the two species account for **156,608 of 156,750** explicitly introduced wild source observations (99.91%) within this **particular original source archive**, not 99.91% of all globally introduced *Cirsium* observations. Their introduced records are strongly geographically concentrated, particularly in North America. They therefore represent a narrow observational case set rather than independent replicated invasions.

For clarity, *C. arvense* contributed **78,646 native / 70,964 introduced** original wild observations, and *C. vulgare* contributed **62,368 / 85,644**, respectively. Their **angle-assessable** records numbered **49,072 / 43,195** and **33,637 / 47,833**, respectively (Table S3). These measurement-eligibility counts support **feasibility**, not a prediction that angle must be environmentally associated; the final Holm-surviving Part 3 findings concern colour, whose assessable denominators differ by trait.

These population totals represent different source definitions and must not be presented as numbers of heads measured for every phenotype. Part 2 and the native observations contributing to Part 3 can contain the same underlying photographs; they therefore do **not** provide independent confirmation when an estimate has a similar sign.

WCVP range assignments distinguish wild native from explicitly introduced status, without silently reclassifying other statuses as introduced (Govaerts et al., 2021). We retained source photograph/observation provenance and quality eligibility internally. Public reporting uses aggregates; original observer identifiers, precise source coordinates and photographs not licensed for redistribution are not re-hosted in the analytical results. Taxa, photos and detected heads are different levels of the sampling hierarchy.

### 2.2. Continuous visible phenotypes and matched photographs

Eight image-derived constructs were analysed: (1) capitulum angle relative to the image or EXIF-corrected camera vertical (0° towards the image top, 90° horizontal, 180° towards the image bottom); (2) colour lightness; (3) chroma; (4) circular hue, represented jointly by its sine and cosine coordinates; (5) capitulum outline aspect ratio; (6) circularity; (7) solidity; and (8) width-profile coefficient of variation. The constructs form three registered observational domains: **presentation** (one), **colour** (three) and **outline** (four). Hue has two computational coordinates but is **one biological construct** for joint testing and the 28 construct-pair comparison.

Image-referenced angle is not a directly calibrated angle to gravitational vertical. Colour estimates are not measures of anthocyanin concentration, and two-dimensional outline measures are sensitive to perspective, occlusion and flowering stage. The Part 1 integration comparison used the exact set of 156,683 photographs across 174 taxa that had all eight constructs and the needed environmental values. This is not interchangeable with the larger angle-specific sample of 168,907 photographs/observations across 178 taxa. The limited involucre (bract) proxies were analysed separately.

### 2.3. Shared four-variable environmental model

Predictors were sampled at source-pixel resolution from CHELSA v2.1 climatological layers and WorldClim v2.1 elevation. The **single frozen primary model** contained BIO18 (mean monthly precipitation during the warmest quarter), BIO05 (maximum temperature of the warmest month), RSDS (downwelling shortwave radiation) and elevation. BIO18 is not a measure of rainfall during each actual flowering event, of individual storms, or of protection from precipitation. The original source environmental matrix had a maximum variance inflation factor of **4.9559** for this four-variable specification, not proof of causal separability.

The model was chosen after earlier V4 exploration and should not be described as prospectively preregistered or as an independently selected confirmatory model. Alternative four-predictor BIO12 models, VIF-pruned five-/seven-predictor sets and the 11 scenario explorations are preserved as **sensitivity/background evidence**, not pooled with the final Holm family or substituted into main-text conclusions.

### 2.4. Part 1 — Visible phenotypic integration and trait configurations

**Part 1 is a distinct analytical question applied to existing photographed observations, not an independent source cohort.** The principal analysis uses the identical **156,683 native-source photographs of 174 taxa** with all eight visible constructs; the separate Part 3 strata supply supplementary descriptive RV medians. We retain all original measurements from three image domains: presentation (one), colour (three) and outline (four). For biological interpretation, we focus on **post hoc, exploratory cross-domain pairs**—angle×chroma, angle×aspect and chroma×aspect—rather than treating correlated numerical descriptions of the same colour or outline as independent functions.

The 28 unordered construct pairs were estimated on the same photograph-complete native-taxon cohort. Within-taxon covariation was calculated from taxon-centred phenotypes with equal total taxon weight, whereas between-taxon covariation was based on equally weighted taxon means of the matched observations. For scalar pairs we used the squared correlation as an RV-style association magnitude; hue comparisons used the full two-dimensional circular embedding. Environmental adjustment removed the fitted fixed effects of the frozen four predictors, without removing the species mean itself. This produces conditional descriptive residual associations, **not** estimates of genetic covariance or the fraction of integration caused by environment.

Median pairwise RV was compared within and across the registered domains. For an outcome-blind descriptive reference, the eight trait labels were reassigned exhaustively to groups of sizes 1, 3 and 4 (**280** allocations), and the original within-module minus between-module contrast was ranked among the allocations. These label ranks condition on one measured covariance matrix, whose components partly share pixels and mathematical definitions; they are not phylogenetically independent replications or hypothesis-test P values for heritable modules. Raw RV estimates also have sample-size- and dimension-dependent baselines, which the exploratory equal-sampling sensitivity attempts to diagnose.

The full configuration analysis is detailed in [Supporting Information](SI_V4_FULL_COVARIATION_AND_CONFIGURATION_CONTEXT.md) and combines image angle, chroma and outline aspect ratio. One representation dichotomized each construct at its own taxon-level median, enabling enumeration of the eight visual combinations within taxa. A separate higher-order analysis contrasted observed triads with pair-constrained maximum-entropy expectations. A change in binarization rule was explicitly examined, because the direction of such parity or three-high contrasts is not invariant.

Two apparent involucre projection/spreading proxies **were included in the original native-taxon 80-test multiple-comparison family, but were interpreted only in Supporting Information**, because they were available for only **1,168 observations and 39 taxa each**, and chroma could be compared with these proxies on **1,007 common photographs and 38 taxa**. The subset-specific four-predictor VIF was **6.14**; those data therefore remain descriptive Supporting Information rather than a basis for primary four-variable mechanistic inference. No direct bract spine length, glandular secretion or defense measurement was available.

### 2.5. Part 2 — Native-taxon capitulum angle and BIO18

The native-taxon model separated among-taxon climate means from within-taxon environmental deviations using equal total taxon weighting and taxon-cluster-robust uncertainty. Predictor availability and quality gating were evaluated for each photographed outcome. All four frozen predictors were included simultaneously, and sine/cosine hue terms were combined in a joint two-degree-of-freedom test representing **one biological trait**.

The **80 biological comparisons** in the original native-taxon test family comprise 10 image constructs (eight principal head/colour/outline constructs plus two SI-only involucre proxies), four predictors and two taxonomic scales. Holm step-down correction was applied to this family separately from the two-species comparisons in Part 3. Reported 95% coefficient intervals are **pointwise**; the prior environmental model exploration is not retrospectively covered by the frozen model's Holm correction. The complete native-taxon 80-test family, including **unadjusted, BH-adjusted and Holm-adjusted P values**, is available in Supporting Information; a dedicated original-coefficient comparison for image angle × BIO18 at the two observational scales is [documented separately](SI_S1_BIO18_ANGLE_RAW_BH_HOLM_CONTEXT.md).

### 2.6. Part 3 — Native/introduced within-species trait–environment associations

The same four predictors were used **within each** of four strata: *C. arvense* native, *C. arvense* introduced, *C. vulgare* native and *C. vulgare* introduced. Within-stratum fits balanced observer × 5° geographic cell, adjusted identifiable month/year effects and used cell-clustered uncertainty. The original **128-test family** contains eight photographic traits × four predictors × four strata and was Holm-adjusted **separately** from Part 2. Hue sine/cosine terms were counted as one biological construct. No formal native-versus-introduced **range × environment** interaction was fitted.

To interpret the three Holm-surviving introduced-stratum colour coefficients, we additionally displayed their **exact native counterparts from the original frozen family**, including raw, BH and Holm-adjusted P values and pointwise confidence intervals (Table S2). This is a descriptive six-row comparison, not a new fit or a test that the slopes differ between ranges. Predictor scaling, climatic support, sampling effort and photography differ among strata.

To retain original source provenance without treating introduction as a climatic treatment, each focal species was analysed in **separate native and introduced photographic strata** under the same frozen four-variable BIO18/BIO05/RSDS/elevation model. The model conditions the image-derived trait on the local environmental predictors **within that stratum**. It does not estimate native-versus-introduced differences in climate occupancy, multivariate climatic-niche expansion, introduced-minus-native niche centroid shifts, or range-status × BIO18 effects.

Range labels are coarse WCVP regional statuses, not records of founding population ancestry, introduction timing or genetic changes. Spatial cells and photographic observers remain unevenly sampled. The temporal contrast is therefore **conceptual**: the 24 October V4 database provides no residence-time estimates, ancestral genotypes, population-level introduction dates or lineage divergence times. Invasion-biogeography studies find that geographic clines and unmatched source-population sampling can generate or reverse apparent native–introduced differences, even in common-garden datasets (Colautti, Maron & Barrett, 2009; Colautti & Lau, 2015). Comparing Part 2 among-taxon coefficients to Part 3 within-stratum coefficients highlights possible **differences in observational scale**; it does not constitute a randomized natural experiment, a paired genotype design or a formal test that the coefficients differ between scales.

### 2.7. Provenance and reproducibility

The frozen observational statistics were obtained from provenance-verified source observations, a fixed source-pixel climatic extraction and executable model/test-family specifications, with full corrected test results and RV matrices in the separate statistical supplement. The analysis contains source-level identifiers and third-party photographs that cannot all be openly redistributed. For submission, reviewer access to releasable source tables and code must be made identity-concealed and durable; neither access nor an archived V4 DOI is represented as complete in this draft.

**Archival naming:** the original validated 24 October source and CI use the internal identifiers **S1** (native-taxon observation/model family) and **S2** (two-species native/introduced family). In this article, **Part 1, Part 2 and Part 3** are the three editorial research questions. Part 1 reuses source S1 observations and supplementary source S2 covariance; it does **not** create a source S3, a third multiple-testing family or an independent replication. The numbers, source hashes and frozen corrections are unchanged.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Part 1 — Cross-domain presentation, colour and outline associations

Among the **156,683 shared native photographs from 174 taxa**, exploratory, four-environment-adjusted descriptive comparisons between **biologically different image domains** showed weak negative among-taxon signed correlations for head image angle × colour chroma (**r=−0.2251**) and angle × outline aspect (**r=−0.2469**). The corresponding within-taxon correlations were much closer to zero (**r=−0.0281** and **r=−0.0694**). Colour chroma × outline aspect showed essentially no association at either level (among **r=+0.0199**, within **r=−0.0182**; Table 1, Figure 2). Angles increase from image-upward to image-downward appearance; aspect is a **2D relative shape**, not absolute head width or landing surface.

These three pairs were selected for biological interpretation after examining the original results; no independent pairwise significance or phylogenetic test is claimed.

The strong correlations between lightness and chroma or between circularity and solidity are **among numerical measurements within a colour or outline domain**, potentially reflecting a shared pigment/appearance gradient or the same segmented boundary. They do not represent independent pollination, landing or defense functions. The **complete 28-pair raw/adjusted RV table and heatmap**, 24/28 among-over-within count, 280 label allocations and eight thresholded three-trait configurations (123 of 135 eligible taxa) are preserved in [Supporting Information](SI_V4_FULL_COVARIATION_AND_CONFIGURATION_CONTEXT.md). The latter occupancy and its higher-order contrast depend on photographic sampling and dichotomisation, not confirmed functional complementarity. Within-stratum *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare* photographic RV medians also remain in Supporting Information.

### 3.2. Part 2 — Among-taxon image angle corresponds to BIO18

Among the **80 frozen native-taxon** trait–environment tests, one passed Holm correction. Specifically, the among-taxon standardized coefficient of camera-image capitulum angle on BIO18 was **+0.045447** (95% pointwise CI **+0.022349 to +0.068545**; raw P = **0.00011508**; Holm P = **0.00920635**) using **168,907** angle-eligible records. Thus, wetter warm-quarter climates were associated with larger photographed presentation angles (a more downward-looking image-frame direction) across the analysed native taxa after conditioning on BIO05, RSDS and elevation. This observation alone does not resolve whether the corresponding heads droop more relative to gravity or are exposed to less rain. The raw and multiplicity-adjusted evidence for both the among- and within-taxon original coefficients is reported in Supporting Information, rather than elevating the raw-only within-taxon result to another main finding.

### 3.3. Part 3 — Native/introduced colour–environment associations

In introduced *C. arvense*, colour lightness was negatively associated with elevation (standardized β = **−0.173732**, CI **−0.250914 to −0.096550**; n = **26,171**; Holm P = **0.00131233**). In introduced *C. vulgare*, RSDS showed a positive association with chroma (β = **+0.134504**, CI **+0.066158 to +0.202850**; n = **27,200**; Holm P = **0.01456813**) and a negative association with lightness (β = **−0.142801**, CI **−0.216718 to −0.068884**; the same n; Holm P = **0.01925414**). Chroma and lightness from the same colour-processing pipeline are not independent replications of a radiation mechanism. No common Holm-supported Part 3 photographic-angle association was established across the two introduced species.

The **native counterparts** of these three introduced-stratum coefficients show that "Holm-supported only in introduced strata" is not synonymous with "the associations only exist after introduction" (Table S2). For *C. arvense*, lightness × elevation was weakly positive in the native stratum (β = **+0.056267**, 95% CI **−0.018662 to +0.131195**, Holm P = **1.0**), compared with **−0.173732** in the introduced stratum. For *C. vulgare*, the chroma × RSDS coefficient was **+0.136883** in native observations (CI **+0.056600 to +0.217166**, raw P = **0.0008325**, BH P = **0.017760**, Holm P = **0.102398**) and **+0.134504** in introduced observations (Holm P = **0.014568**). Native *C. vulgare* lightness × RSDS was **−0.048808** (CI **−0.147073 to +0.049457**, Holm P = **1.0**) compared with **−0.142801** in its introduced stratum. These are **descriptive within-stratum point estimates**, not inferential contrasts between the stratum slopes.

The original frozen Part 3 family tests eight observable constructs against the four environmental predictors within each of the **four** species × range-status strata. Among these **128** Holm-adjusted tests, three colour coefficients in introduced strata survived (this section), whereas **none of the 16** original head-image-angle × exposure terms survived Holm adjustment. This indicates that the frozen analysis detects some photographic colour–environment structure in the focal strata, without a common supported head-angle association. Failure to reject those angle tests is **not** evidence of equal or unchanged head angles.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Part 1 — Distinguishing biological domains from correlated image measurements

Photographic phenotyping made it possible to compare multiple traits from the **same** 156,683 native images, rather than combining independent species-level summaries. Its original 28-pair matrix remains fully available in Supporting Information. Within-domain association magnitudes are often larger than cross-domain magnitudes, but colour lightness and chroma are different numerical properties of the same photographed corolla, while circularity and solidity share a segmented outline. Their co-variation must not be counted as independent biological adaptation.

The exploratory **cross-domain** pattern is modest: after adjustment for four environmental variables, the image-referenced angle was negatively associated among taxa with chroma (**r=−0.2251**) and 2D outline aspect (**r=−0.2469**), compared with weaker corresponding within-taxon estimates (**−0.0281**, **−0.0694**). Chroma and aspect themselves were nearly unrelated among taxa (**r=+0.0199**). These descriptive correlations are **neither a fixed three-trait syndrome nor a test of adaptive compensation**. Images of a tilted head may also change its apparent silhouette, and **photographic CIELAB chroma** does not establish pigment chemistry or spectral contrast. Thus the covariance analysis provides useful context for interpreting climate associations of separately quantified phenotypes, not the central ecological discovery or proof of coordinated evolution. All 28-pair associations, original RV summaries and threshold-dependent configurations are retained in Supporting Information.

### 4.2. Part 2 — Precipitation, head presentation and the rain-protection hypothesis

The clearest corrected climatic association involving presentation was the **positive between-taxon BIO18 coefficient** for image-referenced head angle (**β=+0.045447, Holm P=0.00920635**, from 168,907 eligible observations across 178 taxa). A larger angle corresponds to a head **appearing more downward in the image**, so native taxa photographed in wetter warm-quarter climates tended to show that presentation. This was the only Holm-surviving association among the 80 original S1 native-taxon trait–environment tests. The within-taxon BIO18 estimate did not survive the same multiplicity correction, and it is not independent confirmation of an intraspecific rain response.

A biologically specific explanation is that downward presentation could reduce the direct exposure of pollen-bearing and receptive tissues to rainfall. Water may disrupt pollen viability or dislodge grains, while a protective involucre may alter which surfaces are wetted. Experimental work on another Asteraceae, *Cremanthodium campanulatum*, documented damage to pollen by water and lower achene production after nodding capitula were artificially held upright (Chen et al., 2013). This precedent makes **avoidance of pollen wetting and wash-off** a concrete functional hypothesis for *Cirsium*, rather than an inferred mechanism of the present images. Similarly, observations in other flowers show that the protective position need not always be the vertical extreme (Nakata et al., 2022). The testable prediction is reduced wetting and higher pollen survival under a particular gravity-calibrated capitulum inclination during comparable rain events, not simply a high photographed image angle.

Several alternative explanations remain. BIO18 is a **1981–2010 climatic normal** rather than rainfall during any photographed flowering event; the estimated orientation is relative to the camera frame, not a gravity-calibrated field axis. Taxonomic turnover across geographical regions, shared ancestry, photographic viewpoint, observer selection and local head architecture could all contribute to the association. Prior alternative model specifications did not uniformly retain the same corrected support and cannot be pooled as independent positive results. Therefore, this result provides a **global comparative pattern consistent with rain avoidance**, not evidence of rainfall-mediated selection or replicated evolutionary transitions. Discriminating explanations requires photographed heads independently measured against gravity, region- and lineage-aware replication and direct rain-exclusion/wetting experiments alongside pollen viability or loss.

### 4.3. Part 3 — Floral colour and climate across native and introduced ranges

Part 2 concerns the geographic arrangement of **different native taxa** and cannot tell us whether an established species expresses comparable trait–environment relationships after crossing a biogeographic barrier. This motivates the Part 3 comparison: native and human-mediated secondary ranges of the **same species** offer different historical settings for asking whether climatic associations of photographed traits are detectable or change in direction. In invasion ecology, such trait–environment contrasts are used to develop hypotheses about ecological fitting, standing variation, phenotypic plasticity and evolution after establishment (Hulme & Barrett, 2013; Colautti & Lau, 2015). They are a **natural-experiment-like comparison**, not a randomised climate intervention or evidence that introduced populations are newly evolved. The two available species were chosen for audit-confirmed photographic coverage in both ranges, not because their trait associations were significant.

The source-audited two-species dataset comprised **297,622 wild observations** (**141,014 native, 156,608 introduced**). In the separate four-variable models, introduced *C. vulgare* had higher photographic chroma (**β=+0.134504, Holm P=0.014568**) and lower lightness (**β=−0.142801, Holm P=0.019254**) with higher solar radiation. Introduced *C. arvense* had lower lightness with increasing elevation (**β=−0.173732, Holm P=0.001312**). These are **three Holm-supported comparisons from a single 128-test family**, not evidence of three independent mechanisms: *C. vulgare* chroma and lightness were derived from the same photographed colour pixels. None of the native-stratum comparisons survived the same stringent Holm correction, but this **difference in threshold decisions** is not itself a significant native–introduced difference.

The directions of the introduced-range coefficients provide interpretable ecological context. Greater chroma and lower lightness under high radiation are compatible with prior broad-scale findings of more chromatic floral colours in high-radiation settings (Dalrymple et al., 2020). The elevation-associated reduction in image lightness in *C. arvense* fits the broader literature on altitudinal floral colour and flavonoid gradients (Berardi et al., 2016). Their chemical and functional interpretation remains open: CIELAB values estimated from nonstandardised photographs are neither direct measures of anthocyanin content nor calibrated floral reflectance, and elevation combines correlated climatic and ecological gradients. The estimates therefore motivate field pigment and standardised-colour comparisons rather than establish a photoprotective adaptation.

Examining the original native coefficients is important for interpreting **why Holm significance occurred only in introduced strata**. In *C. vulgare*, the native coefficient (**+0.136883**) for radiation × chroma was nearly the same as the introduced coefficient (**+0.134504**) on their reported standardised scales, although the native Holm P of 0.102398 did not pass and the native BH-adjusted P did. A detectable association in the introduced stratum is therefore compatible with a **pre-existing colour–environment relationship remaining visible after range expansion**, rather than a response newly acquired through invasion. Unequal environmental support, observer composition, effective sampling and measurement variation can yield differing corrected P values for very similar point estimates. In *C. arvense*, native lightness × elevation was weakly positive (**β=+0.056267**) whereas the introduced point estimate was negative; this contrast raises a more specific question about regional association structure, but does not by itself establish evolutionary divergence. Standardisation and climate coverage also differ across strata. A **direct interaction test** of range × environment, on comparable predictor scales and overlapping environmental support, was not fitted, so neither retained slopes nor changed slopes were established statistically.

Finally, the ecological motivation is stronger than a simple native–introduced mean-colour comparison but narrower than an inference about the **rate or cause of adaptation**. Prior work found distinct stress-related gene expression between native and introduced *C. arvense* under controlled conditions (Guggisberg et al., 2013), showing that this species is a meaningful system for future experimental comparisons. Our photographic observations do not establish whether existing plasticity, source-population selection, post-introduction evolution or differences in photographic conditions produced the present colour associations. The analysis tests within-stratum trait–environment gradients **without** comparing native–introduced rainfall niches or claiming measured post-introduction evolutionary change.

### 4.4. What public photographs measure—and cannot measure

The gain from public photos is the ability to retain many observations and to compare the joint distributions of continuous visible traits across environmental settings. This complements traits summarized in reference databases, but does not replace standardized field phenotyping. Even correct image segmentation yields outline measures dependent on view angle and head maturity; JPEG colour is not a pigment assay; and an EXIF-corrected frame does not define gravity. The present results should accordingly be read as **image-derived phenotypic associations**.

Future mechanistic tests can be staged without rewriting the present source freeze: (i) independent orientation landmarks including a gravity marker and camera-roll record; (ii) spectral reflectance and chemical pigment assays; (iii) three-dimensional or standardized multi-view capitulum geometry, including true bract/spine structures; (iv) phylogenetic and cytotype-aware comparisons; and (v) pollinator, herbivory, rainfall and reproductive-fitness observations. Such data would permit testing whether the apparent modules are developmentally or genetically separable, and whether any configuration actually improves ecological function. These are next-study questions rather than claims about the present three-part study.

### 4.5. Statistical scope and generalisability

The estimates derive from a fixed, post hoc selected four-predictor model. Holm control applies to the declared 80/128 test families **within that model**, not to every environmental specification explored before the model was frozen. Pointwise intervals do not provide simultaneous coverage. Multiple photographs, observers, spatial cells and taxa still have complicated dependencies; clustered standard errors and balancing reduce but do not eliminate these concerns. A future independent sample and photo-to-field validation could change both the estimated coefficients and apparent module contrast.

The biological scope is native *Cirsium* source taxa in Parts 1–2 and exactly **two** introduced species in Part 3; extending the conclusion to all flowering plants or claiming a repeatable adaptation to introduction is premature. Strongly photographed taxa and regions, taxonomic revisions and camera behaviour could all affect which parts of morphospace enter the study. We explicitly exclude putative molecular-module reuse and evolutionary gain/loss reconstructions from the evidence for this paper.

## 5. Conclusion

Globally distributed public photographs can be transformed into repeated quantitative estimates of *Cirsium* capitulum presentation, colour and two-dimensional form, avoiding some information loss inherent in categorical descriptions and single taxon means. Among native taxa, capitula photographed in climates with greater warm-quarter precipitation tended to have a larger downward-looking image angle after accounting for temperature, radiation and elevation. This corrected association is consistent with a rain-protection hypothesis centred on pollen wetting and wash-off, but the protective mechanism itself remains untested.

The conspecific native–introduced comparison provides a second ecological perspective. In introduced *C. vulgare*, solar radiation was positively related to photographic chroma and negatively related to lightness, whereas introduced *C. arvense* showed lower lightness at higher elevation. These patterns are compatible with existing knowledge of abiotic floral-colour gradients. Only introduced-stratum coefficients survived Holm correction; this difference in statistical support does not by itself demonstrate that a trait–environment relationship changed after introduction. Original native estimates and complete test families are retained for this reason.

Together, the study advances a reproducible **global photographic phenomics** approach and identifies climate-linked floral phenotypes whose functional and evolutionary explanations can now be tested, while treating weak photographic cross-trait covariance as supporting description rather than evidence of an integrated adaptive syndrome.
