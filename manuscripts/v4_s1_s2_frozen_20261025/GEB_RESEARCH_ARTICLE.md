# Global photographic phenomics reveals climate-associated capitulum traits and local floral resemblance in Cirsium

**Running title:** Climate and thistle capitula

## Abstract

**Aim:** How are floral traits associated with climate across geographically separated thistles? We quantified individual trait gradients and asked whether taxa show greater local capitulum resemblance where their sampled climates are similar.

**Location:** Worldwide native *Cirsium* taxa; native and introduced distributions of *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare*.

**Time period:** Photographs from different years; 1981–2010 climatic normals.

**Major taxa studied:** *Cirsium* (Asteraceae).

**Methods:** Public photographs supplied continuous capitulum presentation, colour and outline measurements. Native-taxon models included 168,907 angle-eligible observations from 178 taxa; separate native/introduced models used a two-species source of 297,622 wild observations. We then matched precipitation, temperature and radiation between sampled cells of geographically separated taxa, comparing three-trait photographic distances against within-taxon vector permutations. Geographic eligibility retained 63 taxa at 1° and 31 at 5°.

**Results:** Native taxa in wetter warm-quarter climates had more downward-looking photographic capitula (standardised β=+0.0454; Holm P=0.0092). Introduced *C. vulgare* showed greater chroma and lower lightness with radiation, and introduced *C. arvense* showed lower lightness with elevation. Climate-matched cells had smaller joint capitulum distances than expected under within-taxon permutations (distance reductions +0.041 at 1° and +0.094 at 5°; exploratory Holm-2 P=0.010 at each resolution). The null-relative effect remained positive across six additional random-seed series, with more stable statistical support at 5°.

**Main conclusions:** Climatic associations of floral appearance extend from individual trait gradients to local multivariate resemblance across geographically separated taxa. Retaining the geographical correspondence between climate and phenotype reveals this structure within sampled taxon distributions. Spatial-grain and sampling sensitivities constrain its generality; its functional and evolutionary causes remain to be tested.

**Keywords:** biogeography, capitulum, climate, floral colour, image phenomics, introduced ranges, morphospace, trait variation

## 1. Introduction

Explaining how plant form varies across climates and biogeographic regions is a central aim of trait-based ecology. Climatic conditions and regional histories can both contribute to the phenotypes represented in a flora: functional diversity across the Canary Islands, for example, differs along environmental gradients among plant groups of different origins (Hanz et al., 2022). Reproductive traits add a distinctive dimension to this problem. Their environmental associations concern structures involved in reproduction, and need not follow the same patterns as vegetative traits. Global comparisons have found trait-specific climatic associations of within-species variation in both floral and vegetative traits (Kuppler et al., 2020). An unresolved question is whether geographically separated plants occupying similar climatic conditions also resemble one another in their combined floral phenotype.

Answering this question requires preserving where phenotypes were observed. A species mean describes its typical phenotype but does not identify which phenotypes occur in each part of its distribution. The extent of variation likewise does not specify its geographical arrangement. Local measurements can therefore reveal a different aspect of a trait–environment relationship from a single value assigned to each species (Siefert et al., 2015). In European forest herbs, retaining local intraspecific trait values strengthened relationships with measured environmental conditions (Govaert et al., 2024). This motivates a spatially explicit comparison of reproductive phenotypes: for taxa occurring in different regions, are flower heads more similar at sampled locations that share similar climates? Such a comparison concerns the local correspondence between climate and phenotype within the observed distributions of taxa.

Repeated public photographs provide a way to retain this geographical information while measuring continuous floral variation. Trait databases have greatly expanded comparative coverage, but gaps remain across taxa and regions, including introduced plants (Kattge et al., 2020; Grenié et al., 2025). Computer vision can locate reproductive structures and extract quantitative image traits from distributed observations (Schiller et al., 2021). Angles, colour coordinates and outlines retain intermediate states that categorical descriptions such as upright, nodding or pale compress. Their interpretation must nevertheless follow the measurement: they describe photographic appearance, which also depends on viewpoint and illumination (Laitly et al., 2021).

Thistles of the genus *Cirsium* offer a common reproductive structure across contrasting environments. Their capitula contain many florets surrounded by an involucre, while varying in presentation, colour and outline. The genus also includes geographically separated regional radiations. Phylogenomic studies document rapid diversification following dispersal into Japan and North America, and relate North American diversification to precipitation, semiaridity and landscape history (Moreyra et al., 2025; Siniscalchi, Ackerfield & Folk, 2023). This combination of shared head architecture, regional histories and extensive photographic coverage provides a comparative setting for examining climatic associations of floral appearance.

Individual traits suggest specific environmental comparisons. Downward head presentation could reduce exposure of reproductive tissues to rain; experiments on *Cremanthodium campanulatum*, another Asteraceae, found reproductive benefits of nodding capitula under high-elevation conditions (Chen et al., 2013). Floral colour also varies along radiation and elevational gradients in other systems (Dalrymple et al., 2020; Berardi et al., 2016). These precedents motivate associations to examine in *Cirsium*. Native and introduced distributions of the same species provide a complementary biogeographic comparison, because trait–environment associations may be expressed under different environmental opportunities and establishment histories (Hulme & Barrett, 2013; Colautti & Lau, 2015). These comparisons are observational; field function and changes caused by introduction require separate evidence.

We examine three linked questions using geographically referenced photographic traits. First, does capitulum presentation among native taxa vary with warm-quarter precipitation after accounting for temperature, radiation and elevation? Second, how do floral-colour gradients with radiation and elevation appear within the native and introduced distributions of two widely photographed species? Third, do taxa from different geographic sectors have more similar joint capitulum phenotypes at locations matched for precipitation, temperature and radiation? For the third comparison, we combine image-referenced angle, CIELAB chroma and outline aspect ratio, and assess local resemblance against permutations that preserve each sampled taxon's multivariate phenotype distribution. The three comparisons connect individual trait gradients with the geographical arrangement of whole-head photographic appearance, while retaining their different sampling units and inferential scopes.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Sources, taxon eligibility and non-independence

We used two archived observation sources. The native-taxon source (S1) contained **313,350** WCVP-assigned native observations and supplied the native climatic-gradient and local climate-analogue analyses. The two-species source (S2) contained **297,622** wild observations of *Cirsium arvense* and *C. vulgare*: **141,014 native, 156,608 introduced**. WCVP assignments distinguished native from explicitly introduced regional status (Govaerts et al., 2021). Records with other statuses were not reassigned as introduced.

The two focal species were initially selected for extensive photographic coverage in both ranges. A subsequent audit of all **350** original species-rank names identified **seven** with both native and explicitly introduced wild records. Only the two focal species met all three coverage requirements in each range: at least **100** angle-eligible observations, **15** occupied broad 5° cells defined using equal sine-latitude bands, and **10** observers. This retrospective feasibility screen used eligibility and coverage, not measured trait values or significance; the species were not randomly selected. They contributed **156,608 of 156,750** explicitly introduced wild observations (**99.91%**) in this source archive, with strong geographical concentration. Full eligibility results are in Table S3 and [Supporting Information](SI_S2_FOCAL_SPECIES_SELECTION_AUDIT.md).

Source totals, photographs, detected heads and trait-eligible measurements represent different sampling levels. Eligibility was evaluated separately for each response. Native observations can occur in both S1 and S2, and the local climate-analogue analysis reuses S1; these comparisons do not provide independent source replication. Species-specific source and angle-eligible counts appear in Table 1. Source identifiers and precise coordinates were retained internally, while public outputs use permissible aggregates.

### 2.2. Continuous visible phenotypes and matched photographs

The image workflow used YOLO to locate candidate capitula, followed by source-linked routines for image-axis, colour and segmented-outline measurements. We reused the archived, quality-screened numerical exports. Source observation counts therefore do not imply successful measurement of every phenotype. The detection and continuous-trait routines are documented in the archived scripts `07_screen_downloaded_images_with_yolo.py` and `56_run_primary_traits_continuous_v2.py`; independent biological calibration remains necessary.

Eight constructs were measured: image-referenced capitulum angle, CIELAB lightness, chroma, circular hue, outline aspect ratio, circularity, solidity and width-profile coefficient of variation. Angle increases from the image top (0°), through horizontal (90°), to the image bottom (180°), after available EXIF orientation correction. Hue was represented by sine and cosine coordinates but counted as one biological construct in joint tests. The constructs describe presentation, colour and outline. Angle is not calibrated to gravitational vertical, photographic colour is not a pigment assay, and outline geometry depends on projection, occlusion and flowering stage.

The native angle analysis used **168,907 records from 178 taxa**. Supplementary eight-construct comparisons required **156,683 photographs across 174 taxa** with complete measurements and environmental data. The three-trait climate-analogue comparison applied its own same-photograph eligibility gate (Section 2.7).

### 2.3. Environmental data and analysis-specific predictors

Environmental values were extracted at source-pixel resolution from CHELSA v2.1 climatic normals for 1981–2010 and WorldClim v2.1 elevation. Native-taxon and within-species regressions used the same four predictors: BIO18 (mean monthly precipitation during the warmest quarter), BIO05 (maximum temperature of the warmest month), RSDS (downwelling shortwave radiation) and elevation. The maximum variance inflation factor in the original four-variable source matrix was **4.9559**. Climatic normals describe long-term conditions rather than weather at the photographed flowering event.

The local climate-analogue analysis used a separate three-axis representation: annual precipitation (BIO12), mean temperature (BIO01) and RSDS. These coordinates describe climatic conditions at sampled occurrences; they do not estimate the fundamental niche or available environmental background. The regression model was fixed after earlier model exploration. The H3c comparison was an exploratory, post-freeze extension, with its matching threshold and two-metric family fixed before that analysis was run. Neither constitutes a prospectively preregistered confirmatory study.

### 2.4. Supplementary matched-photograph trait associations

The eight-construct comparison is a descriptive analysis of the existing observations, **not an independent source cohort**. It retains all 28 construct-pair associations, using taxon-centred phenotypes with equal total taxon weight within taxa and equally weighted taxon means among taxa. Scalar pairs use squared correlations; hue uses both circular coordinates. Associations before and after four-predictor adjustment, descriptive ranks among 280 domain-label allocations, thresholded three-trait combinations and the limited involucre proxies are documented in [Supporting Information](SI_V4_FULL_COVARIATION_AND_CONFIGURATION_CONTEXT.md). These analyses describe phenotypic covariation without establishing genetic or functional integration (Pigliucci, 2003; Diggle, 2014).

Additional exploratory comparisons of climatic breadth, trait breadth and taxon centroids are reported in the [breadth supplement](SI_H3_CLIMATE_MORPHOSPACE_BREADTH_20261029.md); single-trait breadth and environmental moderation of covariance are in the [covariance supplement](SI_H3_ENVIRONMENT_MODERATED_TRAIT_COVARIANCE_20261029.md). The complementary H3c measure is a normalised Gaussian-KDE density similarity, not a thresholded hypervolume intersection (Blonder et al., 2014). It remains in the original two-metric correction family and is reported in the [climate-analogue supplement](SI_H3_CLIMATE_ANALOGUE_CONDITIONAL_OVERLAP_20261029.md).

### 2.5. Climatic associations within and among native taxa

Native-taxon models separated taxon-level climate means from within-taxon environmental deviations, using equal total taxon weighting and taxon-cluster-robust uncertainty. All four predictors entered simultaneously. Availability and quality gates were applied for each outcome, and hue coordinates were tested jointly with two degrees of freedom. Holm correction covered the original **80-test family**: 10 constructs (eight principal constructs and two supplementary involucre proxies) × four predictors × two taxonomic scales. Coefficient confidence intervals are pointwise 95% intervals. The complete unadjusted, BH-adjusted and Holm-adjusted results, including the within-taxon angle estimate, remain in [Supporting Information](SI_S1_BIO18_ANGLE_RAW_BH_HOLM_CONTEXT.md).

Spatial sensitivity analyses retained the original 168,907 angle records and 178 taxa. One set added geographic predictors. A complementary analysis retained the four-predictor model and assigned taxa to their dominant 20° spatial block using occupied 5° cells. It resampled intact taxon blocks 399 times across 26 blocks and omitted each of the five largest contributing blocks. The two approaches assess model dependence and geographical sampling stability, respectively; neither causally partitions climate from geography. They are separate from the original Holm family ([Supporting Information](SI_S1_BIO18_SPATIAL_SENSITIVITY_20261029.md)).

### 2.6. Within-species native and introduced range comparisons

We fitted the four predictors separately within *C. arvense* native, *C. arvense* introduced, *C. vulgare* native and *C. vulgare* introduced strata. Fits balanced observer × broad 5° longitude/sine-latitude cell, adjusted identifiable month and year effects, and used cell-clustered uncertainty. The original **128-test family** comprised eight photographic constructs × four predictors × four strata; hue counted as one construct. Holm correction was applied separately from S1. For the three introduced-stratum colour associations highlighted in Results, we present their native counterparts from the original models, with the full six-row comparison in [Supporting Information](SI_S2_COLOUR_NATIVE_INTRO_FIXED_HOLM_CONTEXT.md).

Range status denotes a regional distribution classification rather than a dated introduction or known ancestral population. Comparisons are made **without** comparing native–introduced rainfall niches. This is not a randomised climate intervention, and no **direct interaction test** of range × environment was fitted. Climatic support and predictor scaling differ among strata. Geographic clines and source-population sampling can complicate evolutionary interpretations of native–introduced differences (Colautti, Maron & Barrett, 2009).

For spatial sensitivity, we restored the original cohorts, including the all-12 source-pixel environmental-completeness rule, and verified all six coefficients and denominators before resampling. We retained the original model, weighting and date adjustments, resampled whole 20° latitude–longitude blocks 399 times within each stratum, and separately omitted the five largest contributing blocks. Bootstrap percentile intervals describe spatial sampling sensitivity; they are not new Holm-adjusted intervals ([Supporting Information](SI_S2_COLOUR_SPATIAL_BLOCK_SENSITIVITY_20261029.md)).

### 2.7. Local climatic analogues and multivariate capitulum resemblance

We tested whether locally similar climates correspond to greater joint capitulum resemblance between geographically separated taxa. From S1, we retained observations with eligible image-referenced angle, CIELAB chroma and two-dimensional outline aspect ratio on the same photograph, together with finite BIO12, BIO01 and RSDS values. Within each taxon × 1° latitude–longitude cell, we calculated the median of each of the six coordinates. Eligible taxa required at least 40 matched photographs and eight occupied cells. Each axis was standardised using weighted means and standard deviations that gave equal total weight to each eligible taxon. A parallel 5° sensitivity analysis repeated aggregation, eligibility and standardisation for its own sample; the two resolutions therefore have different standardised distance scales.

Taxa were assigned to the longitude sector containing the largest number of their occupied cells: west (<−30°), middle (−30° to <60°) or east (≥60°). We compared taxa assigned to different sectors. This groups taxa by sampled geography; individual matched cells need not lie in different sectors, and the groups are not independent evolutionary lineages.

For each of 24 rarefactions, we sampled eight occupied cells without replacement per taxon. For each eligible taxon pair, the 64 cross-taxon cell combinations were compared using Euclidean distance in the three standardised climate coordinates. All combinations at distance ≤0.9 were climatic analogues. A taxon pair qualified if at least three cell pairs matched, involving at least two cells of each taxon. This was a many-to-many comparison rather than nearest-neighbour matching.

For a qualifying taxon pair, the distance reduction was

\[
\Delta D = \overline{d}_{\mathrm{all\ 64\ cell\ pairs}} - \overline{d}_{\mathrm{climate\ analogues}},
\]

where \(d\) is Euclidean distance in standardised angle, chroma and aspect ratio. Positive values indicate closer photographic capitulum phenotypes among climatic analogues. We averaged these reductions equally across qualifying taxon pairs, then across the 24 rarefactions. Each taxon can enter different numbers of pairs; equal taxon weight applies to standardisation, whereas the comparison statistic gives equal weight to taxon pairs.

For each rarefaction, 199 permutations reassigned complete three-trait cell vectors among the eight selected cells within each taxon. The same taxon's permutation was shared across all its pairwise comparisons. This retains its sampled multivariate phenotype distribution and trait covariation while disrupting correspondence with local climate. Climate coordinates, analogue masks and regional assignments remain fixed. For each permutation index, statistics were averaged across rarefactions before computing a two-sided Monte Carlo P value, centred on the mean null statistic and using the plus-one correction. The 24 rarefactions are not independent tests. At each resolution, Holm correction retained the original two-metric family: distance reduction and supplementary KDE density similarity.

After the initial analysis, sensitivity checks varied the matching threshold (0.7 and 1.1), omitted individual traits, omitted geographic sectors at 1° resolution, and examined six additional random-seed series under the original settings. These checks used 24 rarefactions and 99 permutations per setting or series. They evaluate sensitivity and Monte Carlo stability on the same source data ([Supporting Information](SI_H3C_POSTPOSITIVE_ROBUSTNESS_20261029.md)).

### 2.8. Provenance and reproducibility

Source identities, fixed environmental extraction and executable model specifications link the reported estimates to the archived observations. The internal identifiers S1 and S2 denote source and test families, not current section numbers. H3c reuses S1 and does **not** create a source S3 or change the original 80/128-test families. The original coefficients, source hashes and complete correction tables remain unchanged. Supporting Information preserves additional exploratory analyses so that the focused main-text presentation does not redefine their test families. Durable anonymous access to redistributable tables and code is being prepared; source photographs retain their original licences.

**Scope:** no native-versus-introduced climatic-niche analysis, no native/introduced range × BIO18 interaction test, and no reanalysis of archived four-dimensional climatic niche occupancy is included.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Global image measurements and precipitation-associated capitulum presentation

The native *Cirsium* source comprised **313,350 observational records**. Photographic angle passed the relevant eligibility criteria in **168,907 records from 178 taxa**, whereas **156,683 shared native photographs from 174 taxa** supported the matched, eight-construct descriptive comparisons (Supporting Information). These counts have different measurement requirements and should not be treated as interchangeable numbers of independently sampled heads. For the within-species historical comparison, two species met the native- and introduced-range source-coverage criteria, yielding **297,622 wild source observations** (Table 1).

Across native taxa, the strongest multiplicity-supported climatic result was the association of **image-referenced capitulum angle with precipitation during the warmest quarter (BIO18)**. The standardized among-taxon coefficient was **+0.045447** (pointwise 95% CI **+0.022349 to +0.068545**, raw P=**0.00011508**, Holm P=**0.00920635**) using the 168,907 angle-eligible records. Because angles increase towards the bottom of the image, taxa photographed in wetter regions tended to show more downward-looking head presentation after conditioning on maximum warm-month temperature, solar radiation and elevation. This was the **only Holm-surviving association among the 80 original native-taxon trait–environment tests**. The within-taxon BIO18 coefficient is supplied with its raw, BH and Holm values in Supporting Information; it did not survive Holm correction (Figure 3; Table 2).

In a separate spatial-block resampling sensitivity, the original **+0.045447** coefficient was reproduced without geographic fixed effects. Whole-taxon bootstrap resampling across **26 spatial blocks** gave a **95% percentile interval of +0.014569 to +0.084483** (399 draws), with **99.5%** of draws retaining the positive sign and **all five largest block exclusions** retaining positive coefficients. Geographic-term conditioning separately attenuated the partial coefficient and cannot distinguish climate from spatially aligned taxon turnover; both analyses are reported as exploratory sensitivity in [Supporting Information](SI_S1_BIO18_SPATIAL_SENSITIVITY_20261029.md), not additional Holm tests.

### 3.2. Radiation and elevation gradients in native and introduced ranges

In the four species-by-range-status models, **three of 128 original trait–environment tests** passed Holm correction, all in introduced strata. For *C. vulgare*, increasing solar radiation (RSDS) was associated with higher photographic chroma (standardized β=**+0.134504**, CI **+0.066158 to +0.202850**, Holm P=**0.01456813**) and lower lightness (β=**−0.142801**, CI **−0.216718 to −0.068884**, Holm P=**0.01925414**), each from **27,200 trait-eligible observations**. For introduced *C. arvense*, lightness declined with elevation (β=**−0.173732**, CI **−0.250914 to −0.096550**, Holm P=**0.00131233**) among **26,171 trait-eligible observations** (Figure 4; Table 2).

The corresponding native-stratum point estimates were heterogeneous. In *C. vulgare*, radiation × chroma was **+0.136883** in the native stratum (95% CI **+0.056600 to +0.217166**, Holm P=**0.102398**) versus +0.134504 in the introduced stratum. Native *C. vulgare* lightness × radiation was **−0.048808** (CI **−0.147073 to +0.049457**, Holm P=1.0), compared with −0.142801 in the introduced stratum. For *C. arvense*, native lightness × elevation was **+0.056267** (CI **−0.018662 to +0.131195**, Holm P=1.0), compared with −0.173732 in the introduced stratum. The native–introduced differences in the **Holm decisions** are descriptive outcomes of separate regressions; **no direct range-status × environmental predictor interaction was tested**. None of the 16 original photo-angle × exposure terms in the four strata survived Holm correction.

The six native and introduced colour coefficients and eligible sample sizes were also reproduced exactly before a **20° spatial-block sensitivity**. Among the three original Holm-positive introduced terms, source-block bootstrap intervals remained negative for *C. arvense* lightness × elevation (**−0.22949 to −0.05578**) and *C. vulgare* lightness × solar radiation (**−0.28093 to −0.01521**). The introduced *C. vulgare* chroma × radiation interval (**−0.07581 to +0.20119**) included zero despite **94.7%** of block samples preserving its positive sign; all five largest contributing-block omissions maintained the original sign for each term. Native *C. vulgare* chroma × radiation had a positive spatial-block interval (**+0.06363 to +0.22540**). These post-freeze intervals are **exploratory spatial sensitivity**, not original S2 128-family Holm adjustments, not tests of native–introduced slope differences, and not a spatial–climate causal decomposition ([Supporting Information](SI_S2_COLOUR_SPATIAL_BLOCK_SENSITIVITY_20261029.md)).

### 3.3. Similar local climates are associated with more similar capitulum phenotypes

Photographic capitulum phenotypes were more similar between geographically separated taxa when comparisons were restricted to locally analogous climates. Joint quality control for image-referenced head angle, CIELAB chroma and two-dimensional outline aspect ratio yielded **157,822 records across 242 taxa**. Geographic sampling criteria retained **63 taxa** for the primary 1° analysis and **31 taxa** for the 5° sensitivity analysis. The local climate-analogue analysis matched annual precipitation (BIO12), mean temperature (BIO01) and shortwave radiation (RSDS), comparing different taxa from different longitude sectors.

At 1° resolution, the mean reduction in three-trait distance between climate-matched cells relative to all sampled cell combinations was **+0.04096** standardised distance units across 24 eight-cell rarefactions. The corresponding reduction was **+0.09433** at 5°. Both reductions exceeded expectations from permutations that reassigned complete three-trait vectors among cells within each taxon while preserving its overall phenotype distribution (199 permutations; **Holm-2 P=0.010** at each resolution, from the original exploratory two-metric family; Figure 5). Mean reductions under this null were −0.00127 and +0.00018, respectively.

The distance effect remained positive in all six additional random-seed series at both resolutions. Statistical support was more stable at 5°; the 1° estimates were sensitive to the matching threshold, trait composition and regional coverage ([Supporting Information](SI_H3C_POSTPOSITIVE_ROBUSTNESS_20261029.md)). These results identify a local correspondence between sampled climatic conditions and the joint photographic appearance of capitula across regions. Full matching and complementary density-overlap estimates are provided in [Supporting Information](SI_H3_CLIMATE_ANALOGUE_CONDITIONAL_OVERLAP_20261029.md).

## 4. Discussion

The photographic record reveals climatic associations of *Cirsium* capitula at three complementary levels. Native taxa differ in head presentation along a precipitation gradient; two widespread species show colour gradients within their native and introduced distributions; and geographically separated taxa have more similar joint head phenotypes where sampled climates are analogous. The third finding extends the interpretation from individual traits to the local arrangement of multivariate photographic phenotypes. Together, the results show how repeated, geographically referenced observations can connect broad trait gradients with resemblance at particular climatic settings.

### 4.1. Precipitation, head presentation and the rain-protection hypothesis

Native taxa photographed in wetter warm-quarter climates tended to have a larger downward-looking image angle, after accounting for temperature, radiation and elevation. This was the only Holm-surviving association in the original native 80-test family. The between-taxon result describes variation among taxa occupying different climates; the within-taxon estimate did not retain support under the same correction. The spatial-block analysis preserved the positive direction, while geographic-regressor sensitivity showed dependence on how spatial structure was represented.

The avoidance of pollen wetting and wash-off provides a biologically specific explanation to examine. In *Cremanthodium campanulatum*, water damaged pollen and experimentally holding normally nodding capitula upright reduced achene production (Chen et al., 2013). Work on *Platycodon grandiflorus* also connects floral orientation to pollen transfer and rain-damage avoidance (Nakata et al., 2022). These studies make head orientation a plausible component of reproductive exposure to rain. The *Cirsium* association identifies taxa and climatic settings in which that mechanism could be tested.

Our predictor is a **1981–2010 climatic normal**, and our response is an angle within an image. The association is therefore not evidence of rainfall-mediated selection. Shared ancestry, regional taxon turnover and photographic viewpoint remain possible contributors. A direct functional test would measure capitulum inclination relative to gravity and compare pollen wetting, viability or loss under comparable rain exposure. This would connect the observed biogeographic pattern to reproductive performance.

### 4.2. Floral colour and climate across native and introduced ranges

The conspecific native–introduced comparison provides a second ecological perspective. Introduced *C. vulgare* displayed greater chroma and lower lightness with radiation, while introduced *C. arvense* displayed lower lightness with elevation. These are three Holm-supported comparisons from a single 128-test family. The chroma association is qualitatively consistent with a positive radiation–chromatic contrast relationship in a broad-scale spectral study, although the colour metrics differ (Dalrymple et al., 2020). The elevation association likewise motivates comparison with published floral-colour clines (Berardi et al., 2016). Because chroma and lightness share the same photographed colour information, their associations need not represent separate mechanisms.

The native estimates help distinguish the occurrence of an association from evidence of a range difference. For radiation × chroma in *C. vulgare*, the native coefficient (**+0.136883**) was close to the introduced coefficient (**+0.134504**) on the reported standardised scales, despite the native Holm P of 0.102398. This is compatible with an association present in both distributional contexts. For *C. arvense*, the native lightness × elevation point estimate was weakly positive (+0.056267), whereas the introduced estimate was negative. That contrast identifies a more specific question for matched environmental sampling, but neither comparison supplies a formal test of slope differences.

Native and introduced populations may differ through source-population sorting, plasticity, ecological fitting or evolution following establishment (Hulme & Barrett, 2013; Colautti & Lau, 2015; van Kleunen, Bossdorf & Dawson, 2018). Controlled experiments have found stress-related expression differences between native and introduced *C. arvense* (Guggisberg et al., 2013), supporting the biological interest of these comparisons. Present-day photographs cannot distinguish those processes. In addition, spatial-block uncertainty remained wider for introduced *C. vulgare* chroma than for the lightness associations. The supported inference is thus a set of within-range colour gradients, with their persistence or change after introduction requiring a matched-scale interaction analysis and experimental measurements.

### 4.3. Local climatic similarity and joint capitulum resemblance

Taxa assigned to different geographic sectors had more similar joint photographic capitulum phenotypes at cells matched for precipitation, temperature and radiation. The reduction in distance exceeded expectations when complete cell-level trait vectors were reassigned within each taxon. Because these permutations retain the sampled phenotype distributions and relationships among the three traits, the observed correspondence depends on where those phenotypes occur within the sampled climatic distribution. The positive result therefore concerns geographical organisation of floral appearance beyond the overall phenotype differences between the compared taxa.

This perspective connects the individual-trait results to a broader biogeographic question. Angle, colour and outline need not each follow an identical environmental gradient for their combined phenotype to show local climatic correspondence. Kuppler et al. (2020) documented trait-specific and species-specific climatic associations in the extent of intraspecific variation, while Govaert et al. (2024) showed the value of retaining local trait measurements in environmental comparisons. Our analysis applies that spatial perspective to reproductive appearance across taxa. Its contribution is an observed relationship between climatic similarity and joint capitulum resemblance at occupied locations; it does not depend on assigning one representative phenotype to each taxon.

The pattern has a defined empirical scope. Six additional random-seed series retained positive distance effects relative to their permutation means at both resolutions, although unadjusted P < 0.05 occurred in two of six series at 1° and all six at 5°. At 1°, omitting chroma or the middle longitude sector reversed these null-relative effects under paired sensitivity draws. Thus the observed resemblance depends on spatial grain, trait composition and regional coverage. The 5° analysis uses fewer eligible taxa and a separately standardised trait space, so its larger numerical reduction is not a common-unit estimate of a stronger biological effect. These sensitivities delimit the positive result rather than provide independent replications.

Environmental responses within taxa, regional population differentiation and correlated local conditions are possible biological explanations. The present comparison does not separate them from spatially structured photography or unmeasured ecology. Geographic sectors also do not establish independent evolutionary origins. Field measurements of climate-matched populations, coupled with a source-matched phylogeny, would test whether the photographic resemblance persists in calibrated traits and whether it reflects repeated evolutionary changes.

### 4.4. What public photographs measure—and cannot measure

Globally distributed public photographs preserve repeated observations of continuous reproductive appearance. The three-trait analysis uses measurements from the same photographs, allowing presentation, colour and outline to be considered jointly. This complements species-level trait resources and identifies environmental settings for targeted field sampling. The full within- and among-taxon image-metric covariance analysis remains in Supporting Information as descriptive context.

Photographic resemblance also includes measurement variation. Camera position affects angle and apparent aspect ratio; illumination affects CIELAB colour; and flowering stage can affect all three. Cell medians reduce the influence of individual images but do not remove systematic regional or environmental differences in photographic practice. The gridded climatic normals likewise do not measure the microclimate experienced by a capitulum. Standardised multi-view photographs, gravity references and spectral measurements would help determine which components of the observed similarity correspond to anatomical or optical traits.

### 4.5. Statistical scope and generalisability

The analyses combine large photographic samples with much smaller numbers of taxa, geographic cells and range strata. Their precision and transferability depend on these higher-level units. Holm correction preserves the declared 80/128 regression-test families and the separate H3c two-metric family; it does not account retrospectively for all earlier model exploration. H3c is exploratory, and its within-taxon permutations disrupt spatial trait arrangement without controlling all spatial autocorrelation or photographic confounding.

The regional representation is uneven: the eastern sector contributes six eligible taxa at 1° and five at 5°, while the native–introduced comparison covers two species. Further geographically balanced sampling is needed to assess generality. The present evidence supports climatic associations of photographic capitulum traits and local multivariate resemblance, with adaptation, functional performance and evolutionary convergence remaining distinct questions.

## 5. Conclusion

Repeated public photographs reveal climatic structure in *Cirsium* capitulum appearance at complementary scales. Warm-quarter precipitation is associated with more downward-looking presentation among native taxa, while radiation and elevation are associated with floral colour within the two-species range comparison. Most directly, geographically separated taxa show greater joint capitulum resemblance at sampled locations with similar precipitation, temperature and radiation than expected from within-taxon rearrangements of their observed phenotypes.

This local correspondence makes the geographical placement of trait observations informative alongside their individual environmental gradients. Its sensitivity to spatial grain and sampling composition defines the scope of the present finding. Geographically resolved photographic phenomics can therefore identify where similar climates accompany similar reproductive appearances and guide field comparisons of the mechanisms producing that resemblance.
