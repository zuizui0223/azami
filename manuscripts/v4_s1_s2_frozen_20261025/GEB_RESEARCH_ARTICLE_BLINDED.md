# Scale-dependent climatic associations and partial phenotypic integration in globally photographed thistles

**Running title:** Thistle traits across scales

## Abstract

**Aim:** To examine continuous photographic capitulum phenotypes in globally observed *Cirsium*: their multivariate organisation, the relationship of image-referenced head angle with warm-quarter precipitation (BIO18), and environmental colour associations within native and introduced ranges.

**Location:** Worldwide native *Cirsium* taxa and wild native/introduced observations of *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare*.

**Time period:** CHELSA 1981–2010 climatological normals; photograph dates vary by observation.

**Major taxa studied:** *Cirsium* (Asteraceae).

**Methods:** We quantified eight image-derived constructs. Part 1 analysed 28 trait pairs and multi-trait configurations in 156,683 native photographs of 174 taxa. Part 2 modelled capitulum angle and other photographic traits against BIO18, temperature, radiation and elevation in a native-source cohort of 313,350 observations (80 Holm-adjusted tests). Part 3 analysed the same environmental variables within four species-by-native/introduced strata (297,622 wild source observations; 128 separately Holm-adjusted tests); only two of 350 source names passed two-range sampling gates.

**Results:** Part 1 showed larger environmentally adjusted among- than within-taxon RV for 24/28 trait pairs, stronger within-domain than cross-domain median RV and all eight tested three-trait configurations in 123/135 eligible taxa. Part 2 identified a positive among-taxon association between image-referenced capitulum angle and BIO18 (β=+0.0454; Holm P=0.0092). In Part 3, three floral-colour associations with elevation or radiation survived Holm correction in introduced strata, whereas none did in native strata. Their corresponding native point estimates differed in similarity and direction.

**Main conclusions:** Repeated photographs permit quantitative, multivariate phenotypic comparisons and reveal partial integration alongside environmental associations that depend on taxonomic scale and range strata. These observational results do not establish gravity-based nodding, rainfall shielding, genetic modularity or post-introduction adaptation.

**Keywords:** biogeography, capitulum, citizen science, climate, floral colour, introduced ranges, phenotypic integration, trait covariance

## 1. Introduction

Functional trait databases have made large-scale comparisons of plant form and ecology possible, yet a species-level representation cannot by itself preserve the joint distribution of traits within populations, photographs or geographic environments (Kattge et al., 2020). Intraspecific variation may contribute substantially to observed plant-trait distributions (Siefert et al., 2015), but its interpretation requires care: an observed trait range combines biological heterogeneity with differences in observation, sampling and measurement. Floral displays offer a particularly interesting case, because appearance is constructed jointly by colour, spatial presentation and organ geometry rather than by a single scalar trait.

Phenotypic integration describes covariation among traits, whereas phenotypic modularity concerns subsets of traits that are more tightly associated internally than with other subsets (Pigliucci, 2003; Diggle, 2014). A flower can exhibit detectable association among constituent measurements without behaving as one invariant, indivisible phenotypic syndrome. Conversely, differences between within- and among-species covariance need not reveal genetic modularity: they may arise from development, allometry, environmental structure, species delimitation or photographic measurement. Resolving even the **observable** covariance pattern requires repeated phenotypes at a shared observational scale rather than descriptions of the modal condition of each species.

Citizen-science photographs make repeated observations across geographic settings accessible and have been used in automated trait estimation (Schiller et al., 2021). Such pictures are not standardized experiments. Camera position changes apparent orientation and outline; illumination, JPEG processing and white balance change measured colour (Laitly et al., 2021); and photographic effort is uneven among species and locations. Their value therefore lies in carefully defined image-derived estimands, independent data-quality gates and explicitly limited inference, not in treating every image measurement as a direct estimate of anatomical or genetically determined morphology.

Thistles provide numerous capitula differing visibly in presentation, colour and outline. We ask whether these domains form one rigid visible package or show distinct combinations and partly separable covariance structures in photographs. We organise the analysis into **three linked parts**: (1) quantification of repeated visible traits and their phenotypic integration and configurations, (2) global native-taxon associations of capitulum image angle with warm-quarter precipitation (BIO18), and (3) a historically motivated native/introduced comparison of species-specific photographic colour–environment associations. The same photographed observations can contribute to multiple parts; these are **different research questions, not independent replicates**. We do **not** compare native and introduced climatic niches or their occupancy distributions.

Earlier work on trait integration and continuous image phenotypes provides the methodological basis for this study. The ecological question is whether specific components of visible floral appearance—not an undifferentiated floral syndrome—correspond to environmental gradients. Published comparative flower-colour research predicts increased chromatic contrast under high solar radiation and documents associations of pigmentation with elevation (Dalrymple et al., 2020; Berardi et al., 2016). We use these as biological contexts for interpreting **image-derived** colour gradients, not as grounds to infer pigment concentrations or adaptive protection.

The use of native and introduced strata has a **biogeographic-history rationale**, rather than assuming that introduced plants have undergone a recent *species radiation*. Native populations often have longer histories of exposure to regional environments, whereas introduced populations represent secondary colonization across anthropogenic biogeographic barriers; they may carry pre-existing variation, respond plastically, or evolve following establishment. Hulme and Barrett (2013) specifically framed differences and similarities in **trait–environment gradients** across these ranges as hypotheses about ecological fitting, plasticity and contemporary evolution, not direct tests of their causes. The comparison spans **different historical contexts within extant species**, not dated evolutionary lineages or a controlled contrast of slow versus fast evolutionary rates. Introduced populations can themselves have centuries of residence and may experience rapid evolution, and native populations are not necessarily ancient, homogeneous or locally adapted (Colautti, Maron & Barrett, 2009; van Kleunen et al., 2018).

The ecological motivation was matched to a **source-coverage requirement**: this within-species historical comparison is meaningful only when original records provide enough photographed heads, independent locations and observers **on both sides of the range classification**. The two focal species were named before the complete 350-name eligibility audit; that outcome-blind audit subsequently found seven species with both wild statuses and only those original two candidates passed the stronger two-range sampling gates (Section 2.1; Table S3). This is an *audited feasibility-based case selection*, not random or phylogenetically independent replication and not selection for significant flower–environment effects.

The three-part organisation is **editorial**, not a new set of experiments or a preregistered protocol. Part 1 asks whether visible covariance is concentrated within predefined domains while alternative head configurations occur within taxa. Part 2 retains the complete frozen four-environment native-taxon analysis but gives priority to the single familywise-supported **image-angle × BIO18** association. Part 3 examines environmental gradients within native and introduced species-by-range strata without treating introduction as a randomized intervention. Selection, function, phylogenetic origin and genetic autonomy remain unresolved.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Sources, taxon eligibility and non-independence

The Part 2 source population comprised **313,350** WCVP-assigned native observations. The Part 3 source population comprised **297,622** wild observations of *Cirsium arvense* and *C. vulgare*, classified separately by WCVP native or explicitly introduced regional status. Part 3 uses those four **species × range-status strata** to estimate photographed phenotype–environment associations, not to infer climatic-niche shifts or changes caused by introductions. Both focal species were initially chosen for extensive, measureable records across native and introduced ranges. A subsequent **source-verified census of all 350 original pre-detector species-rank names** identified seven species with both WCVP-assigned wild native and explicitly introduced regional records. The same *stronger* feasibility rule required, **in each range independently**, at least **100 image-angle-eligible observations**, **15 occupied equal-sine-latitude broad 5° geographic cells**, and **10 independent observers**. Only the two original focal species passed all three requirements **in both range statuses** (Table S3). The other five species had **2–32 assessable introduced-range photographs** and just **1–4 introduced cells**, below even the stronger minimum. This complete screen **followed** the initial focal choices; it retrospectively validated that no other original source species met the same coverage gates, rather than providing prospective random taxon selection. The source audit evaluated sampling adequacy without selecting on actual photographed angle values, climate associations or significance. Together the two species account for **156,608 of 156,750** explicitly introduced wild source observations (99.91%) within this **particular original source archive**, not 99.91% of all globally introduced *Cirsium* observations. Their introduced records are strongly geographically concentrated, particularly in North America. They therefore represent a narrow observational case set rather than independent replicated invasions.

For clarity, *C. arvense* contributed **78,646 native / 70,964 introduced** original wild observations, and *C. vulgare* contributed **62,368 / 85,644**, respectively. Their **angle-assessable** records numbered **49,072 / 43,195** and **33,637 / 47,833**, respectively (Table S3). These measurement-eligibility counts support **feasibility**, not a prediction that angle must be environmentally associated; the final Holm-surviving Part 3 findings concern colour, whose assessable denominators differ by trait.

These population totals represent different source definitions and must not be presented as numbers of heads measured for every phenotype. Part 2 and the native observations contributing to Part 3 can contain the same underlying photographs; they therefore do **not** provide independent confirmation when an estimate has a similar sign.

WCVP range assignments distinguish wild native from explicitly introduced status, without silently reclassifying other statuses as introduced (Govaerts et al., 2021). We retained source photograph/observation provenance and quality eligibility internally. Public reporting uses aggregates; original observer identifiers, precise source coordinates and photographs not licensed for redistribution are not re-hosted in the analytical results. Taxa, photos and detected heads are different levels of the sampling hierarchy.

### 2.2. Continuous visible phenotypes and matched photographs

Eight image-derived constructs were analysed: (1) capitulum angle relative to the image or EXIF-corrected camera vertical; (2) colour lightness; (3) chroma; (4) circular hue, represented jointly by its sine and cosine coordinates; (5) capitulum outline aspect ratio; (6) circularity; (7) solidity; and (8) width-profile coefficient of variation. The constructs form three registered observational domains: **presentation** (one), **colour** (three) and **outline** (four). Hue has two computational coordinates but is **one biological construct** for joint testing and the 28 construct-pair comparison.

Image-referenced angle is not a directly calibrated angle to gravitational vertical. Colour estimates are not measures of anthocyanin concentration, and two-dimensional outline measures are sensitive to perspective, occlusion and flowering stage. The Part 1 integration comparison used the exact set of 156,683 photographs across 174 taxa that had all eight constructs and the needed environmental values. This is not interchangeable with the larger angle-specific sample of 168,907 photographs/observations across 178 taxa. The limited involucre (bract) proxies were analysed separately.

### 2.3. Shared four-variable environmental model

Predictors were sampled at source-pixel resolution from CHELSA v2.1 climatological layers and WorldClim v2.1 elevation. The **single frozen primary model** contained BIO18 (mean monthly precipitation during the warmest quarter), BIO05 (maximum temperature of the warmest month), RSDS (downwelling shortwave radiation) and elevation. BIO18 is not a measure of rainfall during each actual flowering event, of individual storms, or of protection from precipitation. The original source environmental matrix had a maximum variance inflation factor of **4.9559** for this four-variable specification, not proof of causal separability.

The model was chosen after earlier V4 exploration and should not be described as prospectively preregistered or as an independently selected confirmatory model. Alternative four-predictor BIO12 models, VIF-pruned five-/seven-predictor sets and the 11 scenario explorations are preserved as **sensitivity/background evidence**, not pooled with the final Holm family or substituted into main-text conclusions.

### 2.4. Part 1 — Visible phenotypic integration and trait configurations

**Part 1 is a distinct analytical question applied to existing photographed observations, not an independent source cohort.** The principal analysis uses the identical **156,683 native-source photographs of 174 taxa** with all eight visible constructs; the separate Part 3 strata supply supplementary descriptive RV medians. We examine **observable** association among three pre-specified domains: presentation (one), colour (three) and outline (four), rather than testing a new genetic or developmental module.

The 28 unordered construct pairs were estimated on the same photograph-complete native-taxon cohort. Within-taxon covariation was calculated from taxon-centred phenotypes with equal total taxon weight, whereas between-taxon covariation was based on equally weighted taxon means of the matched observations. For scalar pairs we used the squared correlation as an RV-style association magnitude; hue comparisons used the full two-dimensional circular embedding. Environmental adjustment removed the fitted fixed effects of the frozen four predictors, without removing the species mean itself. This produces conditional descriptive residual associations, **not** estimates of genetic covariance or the fraction of integration caused by environment.

Median pairwise RV was compared within and across the registered domains. For an outcome-blind descriptive reference, the eight trait labels were reassigned exhaustively to groups of sizes 1, 3 and 4 (**280** allocations), and the original within-module minus between-module contrast was ranked among the allocations. These label ranks condition on one measured covariance matrix, whose components partly share pixels and mathematical definitions; they are not phylogenetically independent replications or hypothesis-test P values for heritable modules. Raw RV estimates also have sample-size- and dimension-dependent baselines, which the exploratory equal-sampling sensitivity attempts to diagnose.

The three-domain configuration example combined image angle, chroma and outline aspect ratio. One representation dichotomized each construct at its own taxon-level median, enabling enumeration of the eight visual combinations within taxa. A separate higher-order analysis contrasted observed triads with pair-constrained maximum-entropy expectations. A change in binarization rule was explicitly examined, because the direction of such parity or three-high contrasts is not invariant.

Two apparent involucre projection/spreading proxies **were included in the original native-taxon 80-test multiple-comparison family, but were interpreted only in Supporting Information**, because they were available for only **1,168 observations and 39 taxa each**, and chroma could be compared with these proxies on **1,007 common photographs and 38 taxa**. The subset-specific four-predictor VIF was **6.14**; those data therefore remain descriptive Supporting Information rather than a basis for primary four-variable mechanistic inference. No direct bract spine length, glandular secretion or defense measurement was available.

### 2.5. Part 2 — Native-taxon capitulum angle and BIO18

The native-taxon model separated among-taxon climate means from within-taxon environmental deviations using equal total taxon weighting and taxon-cluster-robust uncertainty. Predictor availability and quality gating were evaluated for each photographed outcome. All four frozen predictors were included simultaneously, and sine/cosine hue terms were combined in a joint two-degree-of-freedom test representing **one biological trait**.

The **80 biological comparisons** in the original native-taxon test family comprise 10 image constructs (eight principal head/colour/outline constructs plus two SI-only involucre proxies), four predictors and two taxonomic scales. Holm step-down correction was applied to this family separately from the two-species comparisons in Part 3. Reported 95% coefficient intervals are **pointwise**; the prior environmental model exploration is not retrospectively covered by the frozen model's Holm correction. The complete native-taxon 80-test family, including **unadjusted, BH-adjusted and Holm-adjusted P values**, is available in Supporting Information; a dedicated original-coefficient comparison for image angle × BIO18 at the two observational scales is [documented separately](SI_S1_BIO18_ANGLE_RAW_BH_HOLM_CONTEXT.md).

### 2.6. Part 3 — Native/introduced within-species trait–environment associations

The same four predictors were used **within each** of four strata: *C. arvense* native, *C. arvense* introduced, *C. vulgare* native and *C. vulgare* introduced. Within-stratum fits balanced observer × 5° geographic cell, adjusted identifiable month/year effects and used cell-clustered uncertainty. The original **128-test family** contains eight photographic traits × four predictors × four strata and was Holm-adjusted **separately** from Part 2. Hue sine/cosine terms were counted as one biological construct. No formal native-versus-introduced **range × environment** interaction was fitted.

To interpret the three Holm-surviving introduced-stratum colour coefficients, we additionally displayed their **exact native counterparts from the original frozen family**, including raw, BH and Holm-adjusted P values and pointwise confidence intervals (Table S2). This is a descriptive six-row comparison, not a new fit or a test that the slopes differ between ranges. Predictor scaling, climatic support, sampling effort and photography differ among strata.

To retain original source provenance without treating introduction as a climatic treatment, each focal species was analysed in **separate native and introduced photographic strata** under the same frozen four-variable BIO18/BIO05/RSDS/elevation model. The model conditions the image-derived trait on the local environmental predictors **within that stratum**. It does not estimate native-versus-introduced differences in climate occupancy, multivariate climatic-niche expansion, introduced-minus-native niche centroid shifts, or range-status × BIO18 effects.

Range labels are coarse WCVP regional statuses, not records of founding population ancestry, introduction timing or genetic changes. Spatial cells and photographic observers remain unevenly sampled. The temporal contrast is therefore **conceptual**: the 24 October V4 database provides no residence-time estimates, ancestral genotypes, population-level introduction dates or lineage divergence times. Invasion-biogeography studies find that geographic clines and unmatched source-population sampling can generate or reverse apparent native–introduced differences, even in common-garden datasets (Colautti, Maron & Barrett, 2009; Colautti & Lau, 2015). Comparing Part 2 among-taxon coefficients to Part 3 within-stratum coefficients highlights possible **differences in observational scale**; it does not constitute a randomized natural experiment, a paired genotype design or a formal test that the coefficients differ between scales.

### 2.7. Provenance and reproducibility

The frozen observational statistics were obtained from provenance-verified source observations, a fixed source-pixel climatic extraction and executable model/test-family specifications, with full corrected test results and RV matrices in the separate statistical supplement. The analysis contains source-level identifiers and third-party photographs that cannot all be openly redistributed. For submission, reviewer access to releasable source tables and code must be made identity-concealed and durable; neither access nor an archived V4 DOI is represented as complete in this draft.

**Archival naming:** the original validated 24 October source and CI use the internal identifiers **S1** (native-taxon observation/model family) and **S2** (two-species native/introduced family). In this article, **Part 1, Part 2 and Part 3** are the three editorial research questions. Part 1 reuses source S1 observations and supplementary source S2 covariance; it does **not** create a source S3, a third multiple-testing family or an independent replication. The numbers, source hashes and frozen corrections are unchanged.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Part 1 — Partial integration and alternative trait configurations

The Part 1 native-source complete-image cohort comprised **156,683 photographs of 174 taxa**. For the **28** construct pairs, between-taxon RV exceeded within-taxon RV in **21/28** pairs before adjustment and **24/28** pairs after adjusting for BIO18, BIO05, RSDS and elevation. These raw pair-direction counts should not be interpreted as 24 statistically independent positive tests.

After four-predictor adjustment, median within-taxon RV was **0.085245** for pairs within the same registered trait domain versus **0.001419** for cross-domain pairs. At the among-taxon scale, corresponding medians were **0.197455** and **0.015453** (Table 1). Among all 280 allocations of the measured traits into one-, three- and four-trait domains, the registered within-minus-between domain contrast was matched or exceeded by **1/280** allocations at the within-taxon scale and **3/280** at the among-taxon scale. This conditional rank demonstrates a distinctive observed grouping of the **measured** axes, but part of the contrast can be produced by common image colour coordinates and derived outline geometry.

**Table 1. Four-variable-adjusted descriptive median RV by pair grouping.**

| Taxonomic scale | Pairs within a registered domain | Pairs across domains |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Within taxa | 0.085245 | 0.001419 |
| Among taxa | 0.197455 | 0.015453 |

Using within-taxon median thresholds for angle, chroma and aspect ratio, **123 of 135 eligible taxa** exhibited **all eight** possible high/low combinations after the four-variable adjustment (124/135 before adjustment). This rules out describing the observed *image configurations* as one universally invariant combination, but does not measure the population frequency or functional viability of all combinations independently of photograph number.

The pair-constrained maximum-entropy reference gave a three-way parity contrast of **+0.03640** (taxon-resampling 2.5–97.5% range **+0.01456 to +0.05762**). When a different threshold and contrast defined the "all three high" configuration, its within-taxon observed frequency was **0.087729** versus a pair-only expectation of **0.091324**, a difference of **−0.003595** (resampling range **−0.007336 to −0.000049**). At the between-taxon scale the analogous difference was **+0.011494**, with a range spanning zero (**−0.004680 to +0.027401**). The sign therefore cannot be presented as a threshold-invariant three-way functional synergy.

The supplementary four-variable-adjusted RV medians from the existing Part 3 strata also differed among image-trait domains. Same-domain versus cross-domain RV was **0.0487/0.00532** in introduced *C. arvense*, **0.0218/0.0119** in native *C. arvense*, **0.1180/0.00785** in introduced *C. vulgare*, and **0.1102/0.0125** in native *C. vulgare*. These are correlations among photographed variables, and differ in sample composition across strata. They are not independent replicated tests of genetic modules or of a phenotypic change caused by introduction.

### 3.2. Part 2 — Among-taxon image angle corresponds to BIO18

Among the **80 frozen native-taxon** trait–environment tests, one passed Holm correction. Specifically, the among-taxon standardized coefficient of camera-image capitulum angle on BIO18 was **+0.045447** (95% pointwise CI **+0.022349 to +0.068545**; raw P = **0.00011508**; Holm P = **0.00920635**) using **168,907** angle-eligible records. Thus, wetter warm-quarter climates were associated with larger photographed presentation angles across the analysed native taxa after conditioning on BIO05, RSDS and elevation. This observation alone does not resolve whether the corresponding heads droop more relative to gravity or are exposed to less rain. The raw and multiplicity-adjusted evidence for both the among- and within-taxon original coefficients is reported in Supporting Information, rather than elevating the raw-only within-taxon result to another main finding.

### 3.3. Part 3 — Native/introduced colour–environment associations

In introduced *C. arvense*, colour lightness was negatively associated with elevation (standardized β = **−0.173732**, CI **−0.250914 to −0.096550**; n = **26,171**; Holm P = **0.00131233**). In introduced *C. vulgare*, RSDS showed a positive association with chroma (β = **+0.134504**, CI **+0.066158 to +0.202850**; n = **27,200**; Holm P = **0.01456813**) and a negative association with lightness (β = **−0.142801**, CI **−0.216718 to −0.068884**; the same n; Holm P = **0.01925414**). Chroma and lightness from the same colour-processing pipeline are not independent replications of a radiation mechanism. No common Holm-supported Part 3 photographic-angle association was established across the two introduced species.

The **native counterparts** of these three introduced-stratum coefficients show that "Holm-supported only in introduced strata" is not synonymous with "the associations only exist after introduction" (Table S2). For *C. arvense*, lightness × elevation was weakly positive in the native stratum (β = **+0.056267**, 95% CI **−0.018662 to +0.131195**, Holm P = **1.0**), compared with **−0.173732** in the introduced stratum. For *C. vulgare*, the chroma × RSDS coefficient was **+0.136883** in native observations (CI **+0.056600 to +0.217166**, raw P = **0.0008325**, BH P = **0.017760**, Holm P = **0.102398**) and **+0.134504** in introduced observations (Holm P = **0.014568**). Native *C. vulgare* lightness × RSDS was **−0.048808** (CI **−0.147073 to +0.049457**, Holm P = **1.0**) compared with **−0.142801** in its introduced stratum. These are **descriptive within-stratum point estimates**, not inferential contrasts between the stratum slopes.

The original frozen Part 3 family tests eight observable constructs against the four environmental predictors within each of the **four** species × range-status strata. Among these **128** Holm-adjusted tests, three colour coefficients in introduced strata survived (this section), whereas **none of the 16** original head-image-angle × exposure terms survived Holm adjustment. This indicates that the frozen analysis detects some photographic colour–environment structure in the focal strata, without a common supported head-angle association. Failure to reject those angle tests is **not** evidence of equal or unchanged head angles.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Part 1 — Integration without genetic independence

The measured covariance addresses the **organisation of multivariate floral phenotypes**: does the same flower display one indivisible character complex, or a partly separable arrangement of presentation, colour and outline? This question complements the climatic regressions without implying that any of the observed associations evolved independently. The most general result is **heterogeneity of association structure**. Pairs of measurements within colour or outline show larger descriptive RV values than most pairs crossing the predefined presentation, colour and outline domains, while many different three-domain configurations occur in photographs assigned to the same taxon. Together, these observations are compatible with partially modular **visible phenotypes**, rather than a single fixed capitulum configuration. This interpretation does not require—and the evidence cannot establish—freely interchangeable heritable components. A single photographic head can yield correlated colour estimates by construction; multiple shape indices similarly arise from the same segmented silhouette. Shared pixels are an important measurement-level alternative explanation for the high within-domain correlations.

The large 24/28 between-over-within count is also descriptive. Between-taxon taxon means may have much less within-group noise and a different range than individual images, and RV baselines depend on dimensionality and sample size. The fixed-size 280-label reference tests how unusual one *labelled grouping* looks in this observed matrix, not how frequently the same integration architecture evolved independently. The three-way analysis reinforces this boundary: combinatorial richness is visible, but a stable higher-order excess or deficit cannot be claimed when changing the dichotomisation changes the statistic’s direction.

The cross-domain correlations are ecologically more discriminating than the high within-colour and within-outline RV values, some of which reflect shared pixels or geometry. In the matched native cohort, environmentally adjusted among-taxon signed correlations were **−0.2251** for photographed head angle×chroma and **−0.2469** for angle×2D outline aspect ratio, compared with **−0.0281** and **−0.0694** within taxa; chroma×aspect was nearly uncorrelated (among **r=+0.0199**). If image orientation tracked actual presentation, lower photographed angles would tend weakly to coincide with higher chroma and relatively more elongated 2D silhouettes, but this is neither a fixed three-trait syndrome nor a demonstrated functional combination. The two angular associations have small squared correlations (~0.05–0.06), and camera projection can couple observed angle and aspect. They are not independently replicated, phylogenetically controlled evidence of biological selection.

One explanation is a sequence of partly separable **visitor filters: visual detection, successful landing, then effective pollen transfer**. Bumblebee target detection depends on size and colour contrast in insect receptor space (Spaethe, Tautz & Chittka, 2001), whereas artificial flower patterns can guide approach and landing positions (Richter et al., 2023); neither property is measured by photographic CIELAB chroma. Experiments changing floral orientation found altered landing rates despite similar approach frequencies (Ushimaru & Hyodo, 2005), changed pollen transfer associated with landing position (Ushimaru et al., 2009), and altered bumblebee entry-angle consistency and landing time with an interaction between corolla symmetry and orientation (Jirgal & Ohashi, 2023). **Those experiments did not manipulate capitulum aspect ratio.** Natural downward flowers improved effective bumblebee use while restricting other visitors in *Polygonatum cyrtonema* (Tang et al., 2026), yet purple nodding capitula of *Cirsium purpuratum* are visited by *Bombus diversus* (Makino, Ohashi & Sakai, 2007): downward presentation is not necessarily inconspicuous or inaccessible. Separately, matching nectar-tube depth to visitor tongue length predicts handling efficiency in Asteraceae (Klumpers, Stang & Klinkhamer, 2019); this is a **post-landing filter** that cannot be read from overall head aspect.

Visitor access may also impose antagonistic costs. **Absolute capitulum receptacle width**, not shape ratio, predicted pre-dispersal seed predation across Asteraceae (Fenner et al., 2002). In *C. purpuratum*, a larger **number of flowering heads per plant** increased bumblebee visitation and pre-dispersal seed attack (Ohashi & Yahara, 1998, 2000). Neither measure is the photograph-derived height-to-width ratio analysed here: the V4 result does not demonstrate greater landing area or seed predation in lower-aspect heads. Nor should honeybees and hoverflies be classified as intrinsically ineffective pollinators: a broad synthesis found honeybees approximately as effective as the **average** visitor per visit (Page et al., 2021). The testable hypothesis is that **orientation, visitor-relevant colour contrast and actual head/floret geometry jointly modulate detection, P(landing | approach), body alignment, conspecific pollen deposition and antagonist costs**. Present data identify candidate cross-domain combinations for experiments, not pollinator choice, correlational selection, herbivore deterrence or adaptation.

These results should be interpreted as distinct levels of inference. The study establishes (i) associations among **photographic measures** and (ii) occupancy of multiple **threshold-defined** configurations; it does not establish (iii) fitness-dependent functional complementarity, much less independent genetic assembly. With three balanced, independent binary traits, the chance of observing all eight possible combinations rises rapidly with the number of photographs per taxon. Consequently, configuration occupancy must ultimately be compared with a taxon-specific, sampling-matched null that preserves marginal and pairwise trait distributions, observer structure and measurement error. The domain-label ranking likewise conditions on one measurement matrix, including algebraically coupled colour and outline indices; the one-trait presentation domain cannot itself demonstrate an internally integrated genetic module.

Functional coupling would instead appear as a **configuration-dependent interaction** in a measurable biological outcome. Receptor-calibrated contrast could affect encounters, gravity-referenced inclination could affect the probability of landing given an approach, and independently measured three-dimensional geometry and rewards could affect legitimate pollen deposition **after landing**. Those sequential filters can compensate for, rather than merely reinforce, one another; the same visitor may be an efficient pollinator in one floral context and inefficient in another. Floral enemies can additionally damage displays or rewards before pollinators arrive, so antagonism may indirectly modify pollination rather than act only as a terminal loss of seeds (Johnson, Campbell & Barrett, 2015; Boaventura et al., 2022). The direct test is an orientation × insect-visible contrast × measured-access factorial effect on stage-specific behaviour and reproductive output, coupled to seed-predator exclusion, **not** a nonzero photographic correlation or an uncalibrated three-way frequency. Head age, flowering stage and viewpoint could themselves cause correlated angle, colour and silhouette changes; the four climate covariates do not remove those alternatives. Empirical evidence for pollinator-mediated selection on continuous colour variation is also much more limited than correlations with flower visitation might suggest (Trunschke et al., 2021).

### 4.2. Part 2 — Climatic precipitation and capitulum presentation

The Part 2 BIO18 coefficient is the strongest frozen ecological association involving capitulum presentation and survives Holm correction over all 80 Part 2 biological tests. Its cross-taxon scale is essential. The exposure is a long-term warm-quarter rainfall climatology, not rain deposited on the capitulum while photographed. The measured angle is relative to the camera frame, not gravitational vertical. Therefore, this result proposes a geographic pattern in **image presentation** worth testing against rainfall interception, head architecture, observer viewpoint and phylogenetic structure, but is not itself a demonstration of rainfall-driven selection for nodding.

Earlier V4 exploratory models using BIO12 or additional highly collinear climate predictors sometimes retain a positive coefficient and sometimes lose corrected support. These are useful sensitivity findings, yet they must not be combined with the frozen four-variable BIO18 test as if separate independent experiments. Geographic sorting of taxa, spatial autocorrelation, unequal photo availability and shared ancestry remain alternative interpretations. A field study measuring head orientation to gravity and wetting exposure, with geographically replicated populations and independently recorded photographic roll, would resolve substantially more than an additional reparameterisation of the same images.

This abiotic hypothesis has an experimentally grounded **biotic complement**: orientation can mediate both rainfall exposure and pollinator access, but the favoured direction depends on floral geometry and visitor behaviour. In *Platycodon grandiflorus*, horizontal rather than strictly downward presentation balanced avoidance of pollen damage by rain with successful pollen transfer (Nakata et al., 2022). In *Polygonatum cyrtonema*, downward orientation jointly protected reproductive structures from exposure and improved effective visitor use (Tang et al., 2026). Because these comparisons involve non-asteraceous single flowers, neither result establishes a protective or selectively filtering function for *Cirsium* capitula; they instead define contrasting predictions for head inclination, visitor-specific access and pollen survival that could be evaluated against the V4 BIO18 geographic association.

The biotic–abiotic interpretation is therefore a **conditional fitness-landscape hypothesis** rather than a prediction that one direction is universally optimal. Pollinator arrival, body alignment, pollen delivery, rain protection and enemy exposure can have distinct optima; an intermediate or horizontal inclination may outperform either vertical extreme. A decisive field comparison would cross **gravity-calibrated inclination × observed rain exposure** (including a rain-exclusion control), quantify wetting and pollen viability, and record guild-specific approach, landing and conspecific pollen deposition before measuring seed maturation. A parallel enemy-exclusion contrast, with **absolute** capitulum size, displayed head number and any true bract defense measured separately from 2D aspect, would distinguish mutualist benefit from seed-predator cost. No such interaction is tested by the present BIO18 climatological coefficient.

### 4.3. Part 3 — Colour gradients across native and introduced strata

All three Holm-surviving Part 3 colour–environment coefficients occurred in **introduced** strata. This is more informative than a simple count of associations because it invites two competing interpretations: a difference in ecological colour–environment organisation between range strata, or greater statistical detectability of an existing relationship in the more extensively sampled introduced records. The original frozen **native-stratum coefficients** allow us to distinguish candidate patterns while stopping short of claiming that a region-by-environment effect has been tested (Table S2). With the less stringent BH correction, some native colour–environment results also survive, so a categorical assertion of "no native-range signal" would be wrong.

The clearest example of **potentially retained environmental association** is *C. vulgare* image chroma versus solar radiation. Its native coefficient (**+0.136883**) is almost identical to its introduced coefficient (**+0.134504**) on their reported standardized scales. The native coefficient's pointwise interval excludes zero and its BH P is 0.017760, yet its Holm P of 0.102398 exceeds the stricter threshold. This strongly cautions against interpreting an introduced-only Holm decision as the de novo evolution of chroma sensitivity. Equally, coefficients standardized separately within ranges do **not** prove equivalent responses per physical unit of radiation; the absence of a direct interaction test prevents a formal conclusion of conservation.

By contrast, *C. arvense* image lightness × elevation has **opposite-signed point estimates** (native +0.056267, with a pointwise interval spanning zero; introduced −0.173732, Holm P 0.001312). This provides a more compelling **hypothesis** of range-specific association structure than the mere difference in significant/not-significant labels. In *C. vulgare*, lightness × radiation is negative in both strata but more negative in the introduced point estimate (native −0.048808, introduced −0.142801). Neither pattern by itself establishes a statistically supported change in slope **between** statuses: the present frozen fits are separate within-stratum regressions, with unequal records, climate gradients, geographic coverage and possible scaling.

The qualitative directions also connect these photographic coefficients to independently proposed **abiotic flower-colour hypotheses**. In *C. vulgare* introduced records, increasing solar radiation was associated with **greater image chroma and lower image lightness**. Dalrymple et al. (2020) predicted and reported greater chromatic contrast in flowers from regions with higher solar radiation. Although chromatic contrast measured from spectral or community data is **not identical** to CIELAB chroma estimated from photographs, the positive radiation–chroma direction is biologically interpretable rather than merely an isolated significant test. In introduced *C. arvense*, the negative image-lightness–elevation slope is compatible with the broader expectation of altitudinal colour variation, for which field and chemical evidence exists in other plants (Berardi et al., 2016). However, elevation itself bundles UV radiation, temperature, community composition and sampling; darker photographed colour is **not** measured anthocyanin or flavonoid concentration.

These are **taxon-, phenotype- and scale-specific** consistencies, not a universal radiation response. Among-taxon and within-range colour–environment coefficients need not have identical signs or magnitudes, because they describe different sampling hierarchies, photographic cohorts and estimated relationships. Accordingly, the direction of the introduced-range chroma–radiation association must not be generalized to every taxon or region. Moreover, the CIELAB chroma and lightness measurements share photographs and thus may share lighting and white-balance error.

Several distinct processes could produce these photographed patterns: sorting of colour phenotypes among geographically sampled populations; plastic responses of petal colour to local conditions; different founder/ancestral population compositions; local selection after establishment; or differences in photography, illumination, white balance and phenological stage. Climatic RSDS does not measure incident illumination during photography, and CIELAB lightness and chroma derive from shared photographs. Prior studies show both the promise and the illumination sensitivity of citizen-science colour measurement (Laitly et al., 2021). Comparative trait–environment studies across native and introduced ranges provide **hypotheses**, not automatic confirmation of evolution (Hulme & Barrett, 2013), and nonrandom source-region sampling can distort native–introduced comparisons (Colautti & Lau, 2015). Our data do not uniquely identify physiological, plastic, genetic or measurement explanations.

For GEB, the strongest ecological claim is therefore **trait-component-specific heterogeneity of detectable colour–environment relationships across the four Part 3 strata**, with an apparently retained chroma–radiation relation in *C. vulgare* and a candidate difference in the lightness–elevation association of *C. arvense*. This complements the central Part 2 result that overall photographic capitulum association geometry depends on taxonomic scale, **without** comparing native–introduced rainfall niches, treating introductions as controlled climatic interventions or concluding that floral colour adapted after transport.

The contrast with Part 2 also remains an inferential-scale comparison: one positive Part 2 among-taxon BIO18 × image-angle association does not guarantee Holm-supported Part 3 within-stratum angle effects. Native observations may overlap between Part 2 and Part 3, and an unsupported Part 3 slope does not prove an unchanged head or a trait equivalence.

Part 2 considers the structure of repeated photographs among native *Cirsium* taxa. Part 3 asks a different, limited question: among two abundantly photographed thistles, which **photographic traits** show associations with the **same four environmental predictors** within native and introduced strata? The observed differences in support across analyses can motivate hypotheses about taxonomic scale, environmental association structure and sampling dependence. They cannot be interpreted as an evolutionary response to introduction or as evidence of climatic-niche conservatism.

The prior literature directly supports the **comparison design**, but not a specific evolutionary interpretation of our photographs. Hulme and Barrett (2013) propose a shared trait–environment slope as a reasonable null expectation across native and introduced populations: native histories might permit local adaptation, whereas introduced populations could initially rely more heavily on standing variation or plasticity, with later adaptation also possible. For *C. arvense* itself, Guggisberg et al. (2013) experimentally compared five native European and five introduced North American populations under nutrient-deficiency and shading treatments and documented both range-dependent gene expression and treatment-by-range effects. This is concrete evidence that conspecific introduced populations can differ in *some* environmental-response systems, **not** evidence of a flower-colour response or the evolutionary origin of the photographic lightness–elevation coefficient analysed here. In the original observational Part 3, an introduced-only Holm threshold for a floral association is not evidence of an acquired genetic response. We did not fit a range-status interaction or perform common-garden experiments.

The selection of *C. arvense* and *C. vulgare* therefore has **two independent rationales**: a historically meaningful conspecific native–introduced contrast motivated by invasion ecology, and a source-verified opportunity to assess its photographic trait–environment associations in **both** ranges. The 350-name census reduced seven dual-range source species to two passing the same location/observer/image-support criteria, without selecting the later Holm-passing effect sizes (Table S3). Nevertheless, choosing two species for feasibility does **not** constitute replicated independent colonizations or support a general genus-wide test of contemporary evolution. Only the two original focal species passed retrospective coverage auditing across both statuses, and introduced records are strongly concentrated in particular geographic regions. Native and introduced observations are neither experimentally randomized nor paired by ancestral genotype. They do not provide independent transitions into comparable rainfall regimes. As a result, this study **deliberately does not estimate** wetward niche shifts, expansion, stability or unfilling, and does not assume that introduced distributions offer a natural rainfall intervention.

Nor can the unsupported Part 3 head-angle associations demonstrate morphological stasis. The original tests estimate **within-stratum slopes**, not native-versus-introduced phenotype differences under matched climates. Equivalence margins, physical gravity-referenced angles, matched climatic backgrounds, and source-descendant genotypes would be necessary for testing stronger adaptive or evolutionary claims. The manuscript retains Part 3 chiefly because its significant colour-environment coefficients and descriptive photographic trait integration provide a complementary, sharply delimited **within-stratum** view of global floral trait patterns.

### 4.4. What public photographs measure—and cannot measure

The gain from public photos is the ability to retain many observations and to compare the joint distributions of continuous visible traits across environmental settings. This complements traits summarized in reference databases, but does not replace standardized field phenotyping. Even correct image segmentation yields outline measures dependent on view angle and head maturity; JPEG colour is not a pigment assay; and an EXIF-corrected frame does not define gravity. The present results should accordingly be read as **image-derived phenotypic associations**.

Future mechanistic tests can be staged without rewriting the present source freeze: (i) independent orientation landmarks including a gravity marker and camera-roll record; (ii) spectral reflectance and chemical pigment assays; (iii) three-dimensional or standardized multi-view capitulum geometry, including true bract/spine structures; (iv) phylogenetic and cytotype-aware comparisons; and (v) pollinator, herbivory, rainfall and reproductive-fitness observations. Such data would permit testing whether the apparent modules are developmentally or genetically separable, and whether any configuration actually improves ecological function. These are next-study questions rather than claims about the present three-part study.

### 4.5. Statistical scope and generalisability

The estimates derive from a fixed, post hoc selected four-predictor model. Holm control applies to the declared 80/128 test families **within that model**, not to every environmental specification explored before the model was frozen. Pointwise intervals do not provide simultaneous coverage. Multiple photographs, observers, spatial cells and taxa still have complicated dependencies; clustered standard errors and balancing reduce but do not eliminate these concerns. A future independent sample and photo-to-field validation could change both the estimated coefficients and apparent module contrast.

The biological scope is native *Cirsium* source taxa in Parts 1–2 and exactly **two** introduced species in Part 3; extending the conclusion to all flowering plants or claiming a repeatable adaptation to introduction is premature. Strongly photographed taxa and regions, taxonomic revisions and camera behaviour could all affect which parts of morphospace enter the study. We explicitly exclude putative molecular-module reuse and evolutionary gain/loss reconstructions from the evidence for this paper.

## 5. Conclusion

**Part 1** demonstrates partially integrated but varied visible floral configurations: the original 156,683 common photographs of 174 native taxa yield greater among- than within-taxon RV for 24 of 28 adjusted trait pairs, relatively larger within-domain than cross-domain covariance, and all eight tested three-trait configurations in 123 of 135 eligible taxa. These correlations concern observed image-derived phenotypes, not genetically independent or freely recombinable modules.

**Part 2** identifies the strongest supported native-taxon ecological association in the fixed V4 four-predictor family: greater warm-quarter precipitation (BIO18) corresponds to a **larger capitulum image-referenced angle across taxa** (β=+0.045447, Holm P=0.00920635, n=168,907). This does not directly measure gravitational nodding or adaptive rain protection. **Part 3** identifies three Holm-surviving introduced-stratum colour–environment associations with elevation or radiation in two well-sampled *Cirsium* species, while native-stratum counterparts show partly similar and partly different estimates; no formal between-range slope-difference test was performed.

Together the three parts provide a method for retaining repeated continuous phenotypes and a bounded ecological account of **presentation, colour and outline** as partly integrated dimensions of floral variation across taxa and geographic histories.
