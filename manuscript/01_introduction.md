# Introduction

Comparative trait ecology links organismal attributes to environmental filtering, species interactions and ecosystem functioning, but broad syntheses often represent each species by one or a few characteristic values. This simplification is useful when measurements are sparse, yet it treats heterogeneous populations as homogeneous ecological units. Individual- and population-level variation can alter niche breadth, interaction strengths and coexistence, and its importance differs among traits and spatial scales (Messier et al. 2010; Albert et al. 2011; Bolnick et al. 2011; Violle et al. 2012). A global plant synthesis similarly showed that intraspecific trait variation can constitute a substantial component of community-level variation (Siefert et al. 2015). Species means are therefore informative summaries, but repeated measurements are needed to determine how much phenotype varies within species and whether that variation is environmentally structured.

Global trait databases such as TRY and BIEN made cross-species trait ecology possible at unprecedented scale (Kattge et al. 2011, 2020; Maitner et al. 2018), but broad comparative analyses still often compress unevenly replicated measurements into one species-level estimate. That aggregation is often unavoidable, yet it discards the distribution of repeated measurements and makes it difficult to ask whether observations carrying the same species name differ consistently across environmental space. Recovering within-species trait distributions at global extent therefore remains a complementary challenge to building ever larger species-mean databases.

Public biodiversity photographs offer unusually dense repeated observations across geographic and environmental gradients. Platforms such as iNaturalist contain georeferenced images spanning many populations and seasons, while computer-vision research has demonstrated object detection and taxonomic recognition in heterogeneous citizen-science imagery (Van Horn et al. 2018; Wäldchen & Mäder 2018; Rzanny et al. 2019). Multimodal approaches are also beginning to infer continuous plant traits from such images (Sharma et al. 2026). The distinctive opportunity, however, is not simply to assign a species identity or predict one representative trait value. If each assessable structure can instead be converted into a numerical phenotype—an angle, colour coordinate or contour statistic—public photographs become repeated trait observations. This preserves continuous variation among heads and observations that would disappear if images were reduced to categories such as upright versus nodding, pale versus pigmented, or globose versus elongate. At global extent, such repeated image-derived measurements offer a way to analyse within-taxon trait distributions that are rarely retained in conventional broad-scale trait compilations.

This opportunity is inseparable from measurement and sampling bias. Citizen-science records reflect observer behaviour, accessibility and taxonomic preference as well as organism occurrence (Troudet et al. 2017; Di Cecco et al. 2021). Photographs add variation in illumination, camera processing, distance, occlusion and viewpoint. These effects are consequential precisely because the target variables are continuous: colour coordinates can shift with exposure, an orientation angle can shift with camera roll, and contour statistics can change with viewpoint or developmental stage. A photograph in which a trait cannot be evaluated is not evidence that the trait is biologically absent. A defensible image-phenomics workflow must therefore preserve source-image provenance, trait-specific assessability, technical repeatability and the nested structure of multiple heads within photographs, while interpreting the resulting values as visible image phenotypes rather than error-free biological measurements.

A second challenge is biological scale. A trait–environment relationship can arise because individuals or populations of the same species differ across space, because species with different mean phenotypes occupy different environments, or because both traits and environments are structured by shared ancestry. These are not equivalent inferences. Within-species analyses can remove time-invariant species differences directly, whereas among-species analyses require explicit attention to phylogenetic non-independence and uncertain taxon placement. This distinction is particularly important in groups where hybridization, polyploidy and incomplete phylogenetic sampling make a single bifurcating tree an imperfect summary of history.

Thistles in the Carduus–Cirsium group provide an unusually informative system for this question. The same reproductive structure expresses conspicuous diversity in capitulum orientation, visible corolla colour, gross outline and involucral-bract architecture, allowing several potentially independent phenotype modules to be measured on the same observational unit. These modules can plausibly interact with different environmental dimensions: orientation changes exposure to radiation and precipitation as well as floral presentation; visible colour reflects pigment and optical properties; gross outline describes head architecture; and outward involucral projections capture a finer structural axis. The group therefore offers a natural test of whether conspicuous morphological disparity in a lineage containing recent regional radiations is organized along common environmental gradients or instead remains modular among traits.

The evolutionary history of *Cirsium* makes this comparison especially compelling. Early work on New World *Cirsium* found unusually high ecological diversity despite low nuclear-rDNA sequence divergence, consistent with either rapid ecological radiation or unusually conservative markers (Kelch & Baldwin 2003). More recent phylogenomics based on hundreds of nuclear loci places major *Cirsium* diversification in the late Neogene and identifies rapid Pleistocene radiations in both North America and Japan, associated with jump dispersal, geographic isolation and ecological specialization (Moreyra et al. 2025). At the same time, generic boundaries, hybridization, incomplete lineage sorting, polyploidy and phenotypic convergence complicate a simple one-tree/one-trait interpretation (Ackerfield et al. 2020; Bureš et al. 2018, 2023; Michálková et al. 2023). Thus, *Cirsium* combines recent regional diversification, high visible trait disparity and reticulate or difficult-to-resolve history—precisely the situation in which species means and a single phylogenetic comparison may fail to capture how phenotype is structured across ecological scales. We use this context to motivate multiscale trait–environment tests, not to claim that the present image data themselves demonstrate adaptive radiation.

Against this background, we developed a global public-image phenomics workflow designed to retain continuous within-taxon phenotype rather than collapse photographs into species-level categories. YOLO was used only to localize visible capitula; for every assessable detected head, deterministic image functions then returned numerical measurements of orientation, visible corolla colour and outline, with high-resolution crops additionally yielding exploratory involucral contour proxies. The resulting data structure therefore consists of repeated head- and observation-level trait values nested within named taxa. We used a balanced atlas to quantify how this visible phenotype is partitioned across heads, photographs and taxa, an exhaustive post-detection cohort to estimate within-taxon environmental associations, grouped SPDE-INLA models to account for residual spatial structure and additional environmental domains, and taxon-level niche and PGLS analyses to evaluate among-taxon and historical structure.

We addressed three questions. First, can heterogeneous citizen-science photographs be converted into reproducible continuous capitulum-trait observations at global extent without forcing phenotype into discrete categories and while keeping measurement failure explicit? Second, once repeated numerical measurements are retained, how much visible variation in orientation, colour and outline occurs among assigned taxa versus among photographs and heads within taxa? Third, which environmental gradients are associated with these continuous traits within taxa, and which among-taxon associations persist when shared ancestry is represented by the available dated backbone and alternative grafting scenarios?
